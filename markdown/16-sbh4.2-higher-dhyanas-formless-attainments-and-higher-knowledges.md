# Higher Dhyānas, Formless Attainments, and Higher Knowledges

*Śrāvakabhūmi · sbh4.2*

The gross characteristic in all the lower planes, up through the sphere of nothingness, is in brief to be understood as twofold: that in the lower and lower planes one dwells in more suffering and [Restored: in less calm], and has a shorter life-span. This is the twofold gross characteristic. He investigates [it] by the six investigation-subjects, as appropriate, for whichever plane he wishes to become dispassionate toward; and, as appropriate, the calm characteristic of the [plane] above — up through the attention that is the fruit of practice-culmination. Here, 'secluded from sense-pleasures':

Sense-pleasures are twofold: defilement-sense-pleasures and thing-sense-pleasures. [Conjectural: Seclusion from sense-pleasures] too is twofold: seclusion from association [with them] and seclusion from the object. 'Secluded from evil, unwholesome dharmas': [these are] the secondary defilements, the unwholesome dharmas caused by sense-pleasures — namely, bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, mental misconduct; taking up the rod, taking up the sword; quarrel, strife, contention, dispute, guile, deception, fraud and false speech — [these] come about. By the abandonment of these [he is] 'secluded from evil, unwholesome dharmas'. Because he does not [yet] see fault in [Conjectural: thought and examination], [it is] with wholesome *vitarka* and *vicāra* of its own plane that counter sense-pleasures — 'with *vitarka*, with *vicāra*'. The practice-culminating attention is seclusion from sense-pleasures. [What is] arisen immediately after it, caused by it, conditioned by it — therefore he says 'born of seclusion'. Because of attaining the desired, longed-for goal, because of not seeing fault in rapture, because of the disappearance of all encumbrance, and because of the abundant pliancy — the workability of mind and body — 'rapture-and-happiness', of one counting in order; and because he has for the first time risen out of the desire-realm — 'first'. From right contemplation of the object and from binding with one-pointed mindfulness — '*dhyāna*'. Because it is the fruit of practice-culmination — 'having attained'. And subsequently, from the accomplishment of cultivation and repeated practice — one who gains at will, one who gains without trouble, one who gains without difficulty — he passes a night in that *dhyāna* attainment, and a day too, as long as he wishes, up to seven days and nights. Therefore he says 'he dwells'.

Turning the mind away from the [Restored: signs of the] *samādhi* with *vitarka* and *vicāra*, he binds it to the signs of the *samādhi* without *vitarka* and *vicāra*. Withdrawing [it] from the object that moves unsteadily, on an object that moves steadily the mind, calm and clear, proceeds [Restored: through its homogeneity]; he establishes it. Therefore he says 'from the calming of *vitarka* and *vicāra*, from inward clarity'. Through repeated cultivation of that very *samādhi* without *vitarka* and *vicāra*, he passes beyond the stage [still] broken and interrupted [Restored: by these two] and reaches the unbroken, uninterrupted stage. Therefore he says 'from singleness of mind'. Because of the complete abandonment of *vitarka* and *vicāra* in every way, [it is] 'without *vitarka*, without *vicāra*'. *Samādhi* is the practice-culminating attention; [what follows] arises immediately after it, caused by it, conditioned by it. Therefore he says 'born of *samādhi*'. Because of attaining the desired, blameless goal, because of [Restored: not] seeing fault in rapture, [he is] in a state of rejoicing and [Conjectural: gladness]; because of the disappearance of all encumbrance of the defilement-side of the first *dhyāna* with its *vitarka* and *vicāra*, and because he is accompanied by [Conjectural: the happiness] of the pliancy — the workability of mind and body — that counters it: 'rapture-and-happiness'. 'The second', when counting in order. All the rest is to be understood as before.

He sees fault in the signs of rapture. Therefore he says 'from dispassion toward rapture'. And at that time the twofold defect that agitates the mind has [Restored: disappeared] for one placing his mind in the raptureless third *dhyāna*: *vitarka* and *vicāra* [already] at the second *dhyāna*, and now rapture. Therefore he says 'he dwells equanimous'. For these two dharmas are agitators of the mind, obstructors of uninterrupted equanimity. Here, in the first *dhyāna* there are *vitarka* and *vicāra*, by which uninterrupted equanimity does not proceed. In the second *dhyāna* there is rapture, by which here too uninterrupted equanimity does not proceed. Therefore for this contemplator [equanimity] does not exist in the first and second *dhyāna*s; therefore it is said that in the third *dhyāna* 'he dwells equanimous'. Being equanimous, he dwells with mindfulness so established that those perceptions and attentions accompanied by rapture do [Conjectural: not] occur. If, however, through lack of cultivation of the third *dhyāna*, through a lapse of mindfulness, perceptions and attentions accompanied by rapture occasionally occur in his mind, he quickly penetrates them with wisdom; he properly understands [them]. As they arise, he does not tolerate them; he abandons, dispels, brings [them] to an end, and makes the mind indifferent [to them]. Therefore he says 'mindful and clearly-knowing'.

For him who at that time dwells thus equanimous, mindful and clearly-knowing, through practice, [Restored: through cultivation,] through repeated practice, the [feeling] accompanied by rapture is abandoned — that which caused [Restored: exhilaration] of the mind — and a raptureless, calm, fully calmed feeling arises in his mind as the counterpart to rapture. At that time he experiences with the form-body and the mind-body happiness as feeling and happiness as pliancy. [Restored: Therefore he says 'and he experiences happiness with the body'.] Below the third *dhyāna* there is no happiness of this kind, nor uninterrupted equanimity; above the third *dhyāna*, although equanimity is found, there is no happiness. Because below it happiness and equanimity are absent, and above it happiness is absent, this is that sphere — the third *dhyāna* — of which the noble ones declare, with reference to the person who dwells in its attainment, '[Restored: equanimous,] mindful, dwelling in happiness, he dwells having attained the third *dhyāna*'. 'Noble ones', moreover, are [Restored: the Buddhas and the Buddhas' disciples].

Here, because the antidote is [Conjectural: of the same kind], the antidote for abandoning happiness is not stated; only the abandonment of happiness brought about by that antidote is stated.

What, further, is that antidote?

It is equanimity, mindfulness and clear-knowing. Through repeated practice of this, having moved beyond the third *dhyāna*, he abandons the happiness that belongs to the third-*dhyāna* plane. Therefore he says 'and from the abandonment of happiness'. At that time the contemplator attains the transcendence of pleasure and pain. Summing up both what was previously abandoned and what is now abandoned, he says: 'from the abandonment of happiness, from the abandonment of pain, and from the earlier disappearance of gladness and dejection'. [Restored: Here,] because happiness is abandoned at the time of attaining the fourth *dhyāna*, pain at the time of attaining the second, because gladness disappears at the time of attaining the third and dejection at the time of attaining the first — by now, with happiness and pain abandoned, only neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling [Conjectural: remains] for him. Therefore he says 'neither-painful-nor-pleasant'. At that time all the defects of the lower planes, beginning with the first *dhyāna*, have been abandoned: namely *vitarka* and *vicāra*, rapture, and in-and-out breathing. And through their abandonment, the equanimity there and the mindfulness become pure, fully purified, so that the mind of one who has attained the fourth *dhyāna* stands firm, immovable, [Restored: with all unsteadiness gone]. Therefore he says 'pure in equanimity and mindfulness'. Here 'the fourth' is to be understood as before, [Restored: as in the first and the other *dhyāna*s].

Of one resolved on space, the perception of colour — connected with blue, yellow, red, white and so on — is transcended [Conjectural: in that it no longer appears, and through disenchantment and dispassion]. Therefore he says 'from the transcendence of perceptions of form'. [Restored: From the transcendence of these,] because they go to non-appearance, the perception of obstruction — of many kinds, of many and varied sorts, caused by the accumulation of colour — has vanished too. Therefore he says 'from the disappearance of perceptions of resistance'. And further, because these have vanished, the perceptions based on accumulation that occur with regard to the remaining differentiated aggregates — namely perceptions of food, drink, [Restored: carriage,] clothing, ornament, house, garden, forest, army, mountain and so on — to these no directing of the mind whatever occurs for him. Therefore he says 'from non-attention to perceptions of variety'. Having thus [Conjectural: dispelled] the perceptions of form, resistance and variety, he becomes resolved on space under the aspect of infinity. Therefore he says 'infinite space'. Having passed beyond the preparatory stage (*sāmantaka*), [Restored: after] the practice-culminating attention he attains the basic [attainment] (*maula*), the fruit of practice-culmination. Therefore he says 'he dwells having attained the sphere of infinite space'.

As long as he has not attained the basic [attainment], his object is space; but once he has attained it, [his object is] that and the other aggregates of his own plane; in the preparatory stage, the aggregates of lower planes as well. [Conjectural: He — by the consciousness with which] he resolves on infinite space, that very consciousness, which resolves on space under the aspect of infinity — wishing to attain the sphere of infinite consciousness, having turned away the perception of the sphere of infinite space, resolves on that very consciousness under the aspect of infinity. The basic sphere of infinite space, with its preparatory stage, is transcended. Therefore he says 'having completely transcended the sphere of infinite space, "infinite consciousness"'. Having passed beyond the preparatory stage of the sphere of infinite consciousness — up to the practice-culminating attention — he attains the basic [attainment], the fruit of practice-culmination. Therefore he says 'he dwells having attained the sphere of infinite consciousness'.

Rising up from the sphere of infinite consciousness, seeking an object beyond consciousness, he does not find one — [any object] connected with anything, with form or formless. Not finding that object, having transcended the basic sphere of infinite consciousness with its preparatory stage, he resolves: 'There is no other object whatever.' He becomes resolved on the perception of nothingness. Through repeated practice of that resolve on the perception, having passed beyond the preparatory stage of the sphere of nothingness — up to the practice-culminating attention — he attains the basic [attainment], the fruit of practice-culmination. Therefore he says 'having completely transcended the sphere of infinite consciousness, "there is nothing", he dwells having attained the sphere of nothingness'.

Rising up from the sphere of nothingness, perceiving grossness, perceiving fault in the perception of nothingness, he turns away the perception of the sphere of nothingness. Thereby, previously, at the time of attaining the sphere of nothingness, the perception of [Restored: something] was transcended; now the perception of nothing is transcended. Therefore he says [Conjectural: 'neither perception' — that is, neither the perception of something nor the perception of nothing]. But it is not that all perception whatever has ceased for him, as in the attainment of non-perception or the attainment of cessation; only that this perception is subtle and proceeds on a [Restored: signless] object. [Therefore he says] 'neither perception nor non-perception'. Thus resolved on that sphere, having passed beyond the basic sphere of nothingness with its preparatory stage [Restored: and] the preparatory stage of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — up to the practice-culminating attention — he attains the basic [attainment], the fruit of practice-culmination. Therefore he says 'having completely transcended the sphere of nothingness, he dwells having attained the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception'. There, at the time of *dhyāna* attainment, [the body appears] as if entering Rasātala below — [this is] the sign in the body's appearance; at the time of formless attainment, [Conjectural: as if flying up into the sky]. In that case, right practice is to look on [them] with equanimity at the time of *śamatha*.

Here, there are two attainments without mind: the attainment of non-perception and the attainment of cessation. Here, only an ordinary person enters the attainment of non-perception, by the attention turned away from perception; but only a noble one [enters] the attainment of cessation. Here, entry into these two attainments is by two attentions: namely, into the attainment of non-perception by the attention turned away from perception; and into the attainment of cessation by the attention that has risen up from neither-perception-nor-non-perception and wholly suppresses the object. Here: 'perception is a disease, perception is a boil, perception is an arrow; this is calm, this is sublime, namely the state of non-perception' — taking up the attention turned away from perception, he practises [Restored: non-mindfulness and non-attention] toward each perception as it arises. Through his cultivation, on the path of preparation, there is a state still with mind; and [Restored: as soon as] he has entered the attainment, mind no longer proceeds. Thus, in one free of passion for the [Restored: Śubhakṛtsna gods] and [Restored: not] free of passion for the Bṛhatphala [gods], the cessation of mind and mental dharmas by an attention preceded by the perception of escape — this is called the attainment of non-perception. And this is how it is obtained. Here, the noble one who has gained the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, wishing to dwell in a further calm dwelling, raises his mind up out of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. That mind, risen up, does not find an object. Not finding [one], it ceases; it does not proceed. Thus, in a trainee free of passion for the sphere of nothingness, or in an arhat, the cessation of mind and mental dharmas by an attention preceded by the perception of dwelling — this is called the attainment of cessation; and this is how it is obtained.

Here, the bringing forth of the five higher knowledges occurs with *dhyāna* as its support.

And how does it occur?

In this way: the contemplator becomes a gainer of pure *dhyāna*. Relying on that pure *dhyāna*, whatever teaching with the higher knowledges as its chief topic he has heard, grasped and mastered — concerning the range of supernatural power, or past abodes, the divine ear, death-and-rebirth, and the ways of [others'] minds — attending to that very [teaching] with the attention of the concentrated plane, he comes to understand the meaning and to understand the dharma. For him who understands the meaning and understands the dharma, shaping his mind thus and thus, through repeated practice there comes a time, an occasion, when the five higher knowledges, the fruits of his cultivation, arise.

Moreover, he who thus understands the meaning and understands the dharma cultivates twelve perceptions in order to bring forth all the higher knowledges. Namely: the perception of lightness, the perception of softness, the perception of the space-element, the perception of the fusion of body and mind, the perception of resolve, the perception of recollecting in sequence the conduct previously experienced, the perception of the noise of assembled sounds of various kinds, the perception of the [Restored: luminous] form-sign, the perception of changes in form produced by defilements, the perception of [Conjectural: the liberations], the perception of the bases of mastery, and the perception of the totalities.

Here, in the perception of lightness, he resolves on himself as light, like a [Restored: tuft] of cotton-wool or a [Restored: tuft] of kapok in a whirlwind. Resolving thus, he impels [himself] there by the attention of resolve itself, as from the couch [Conjectural: to the stool], from the stool to the couch, and so from the couch [Conjectural: to the grass-mat], from the grass-mat to the couch. Here, the perception of softness: he resolves on the body as soft, like [Restored: silk], [Restored: raw silk], or [Restored: woven silk]. Thus this perception of softness nourishes and supports the perception of lightness, whereby the perception of lightness, so supported, comes to extension, growth and abundance. Here, the perception of the space-element: by this perception he resolves on his own lightness and softness. If he wishes to go somewhere, whatever material thing lies in between and obstructs his going, he resolves to be space, by the attention of resolve. Here, the perception of the fusion of mind and body: by which he fuses the mind into the body, or the body into the mind, whereby his body becomes lighter, softer, more workable, more luminous, conforming to mind, bound to mind, proceeding in dependence on mind. Here, the perception of resolve: by which perception he resolves on the far as near, the near as far, the small as large, the large as small, earth as water, water as earth — thus with each great element in respect of the other, in full. Likewise he also resolves on magical creations, whether creations of form or creations of sound. By these five perceptions, perfected through cultivation, he experiences the range of supernatural power of many kinds: being one, he displays himself as many — namely, by the perception of resolve on creation; then, having displayed himself as many, he becomes one — namely, by the perception of resolve on the disappearance of the creation; he passes through walls, [Restored: through mountains,] through ramparts with body unhindered; he emerges from and plunges into the earth as if in water; on water he goes with the stream parting [before him], as if on earth; he travels through the sky cross-legged, like a winged bird; and he rubs with his hand these sun and moon, so mighty, so powerful, he strokes [them]. Up to the Brahmā world he wields power with his body. All this he does by the perception of resolve, assisted by the perceptions of lightness, softness, the space-element, and the fusion of mind and body. [This] is to be understood as appropriate. Here, the wielding of power over the Brahmā world with the body is twofold: he wields it by going [there], and, [Restored: by resolve as he wishes,] over [Restored: the four great elements] below the Brahmā world and over a part of derived form. Here, the perception of recollecting in sequence the conduct previously experienced: by which, from the state of childhood onward, wherever his memory proceeds and is not obstructed — wherever he has gone, stood, sat or lain — recollecting in detail all the conduct he previously experienced, [Conjectural: in gross outline, in due sequence and without skipping], he recognizes it. Through cultivation of this, as the fruit of cultivation he recollects his manifold past abodes, up through *saṁsāra*, with their particulars, in full. Here, the perception of the noise of assembled sounds of various kinds: in whatever village, town, guild, corporation, assembly, spacious house or chamber the mingled, varied noise of a crowd of people of various kinds, seated and assembled, issues forth, which is called the sound of hubbub, or the noise of a great river in flood — grasping the sign there, the cultivation of the perception by which, with the attention of the concentrated plane, he [Conjectural: fixes] his mental focus on sounds noble and ignoble, divine and human, far and near. Through repeated practice of it, he gains the divine ear as the fruit of cultivation, by which he hears divine and human sounds, both those far and those near. Here, the perception of the luminous form-sign: as before, grasping the sign of light, he attends to that very sign. [Restored: And grasping the sign from the variety of beings, he attends to that very sign, distinguishing by the performance of wholesome and unwholesome deeds and the like.] This is the perception of the luminous form-sign. Through cultivation of it, he gains the knowledge of death-and-rebirth as the fruit of cultivation, by which, 'with the purified divine eye' — in full, up to — '[beings] at the breakup of the body are reborn in a [Restored: good destination], in a heavenly world, among the gods'. Here, the perception of changes in form produced by defilements: by which he observes the bodily condition of beings who are lustful, hateful and deluded, whose minds are enwrapped by the defilements and secondary defilements anger, enmity, [Restored: concealment], burning, [Restored: deceit, dissimulation,] shamelessness and lack of dread of blame, and discerns it. Such is the bodily condition of one who is lustful, the change in [his] appearance: namely, agitated senses, [Restored: excited senses,] a smiling face. Such is the bodily condition of one who is hateful, the change in [his] appearance: namely, a pale face, a stammering voice, a knitted brow. Such is the bodily condition of one who is deluded, the change in [his] appearance: namely, muteness, inability to grasp a meaning when it is pondered, and [Conjectural: vulgar] speech. By these aspects and the like — up through one enwrapped by shamelessness and lack of dread of blame — whatever bodily condition there is, [whatever] change in appearance, grasping the sign from that, he attends [to it]. Through repeated practice of it, the knowledge of the ways of [others'] minds arises as the fruit of cultivation, by which he knows with his own mind, as it actually is, the mind of other beings and other persons, with what it thinks and ponders. Here, the cultivation of the perceptions of the liberations, the bases of mastery and the totalities is to be understood as before, namely, in the concentrated plane. By this cultivation he brings forth noble supernatural power — [the power] of transforming things, of creation, of resolve — and likewise *araṇā*, the knowledge from resolve, and the four discriminations: the discrimination of dharma, the discrimination of meaning, the discrimination of language, and the discrimination of expression.

Here, this is the distinction between noble and ignoble supernatural power: by noble supernatural power, whatever thing he transforms, whatever [Restored: creation] he creates, [Conjectural: whatever he resolves on,] it becomes just so, not otherwise, and with all of it the [intended] work can be done. By ignoble [supernatural power], on the other hand, it does not become so; rather, only a mere show appears, as of an illusionist.
