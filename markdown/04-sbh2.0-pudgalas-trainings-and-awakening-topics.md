# Pudgalas, Trainings, and Awakening Topics

*Śrāvakabhūmi · sbh2.0*

## II. The Second Yogasthāna

[Covering] the going to dispassion by the worldly [path] and likewise by the supramundane [path], and their requisite-stage, called renunciation.

Here, how many persons are there who go forth in this stage of renunciation, as taught and as proclaimed?

And how is the delineation of persons [made]?

- What is the object (*ālambana*)?
- What is the instruction?
- What is the training?
- What are the dharmas conducive to training?
- What is the loss of yoga?
- What are the yogas?
- What is attention (*manaskāra*)?
- How many practitioners of yoga [are there]?
- What is to be done for yoga?
- What is yoga-cultivation?
- What is the fruit of cultivation?
- How many person-designations [are there]?
- How many māras [are there]?
- How many māra-actions [are there]?
- How does an undertaking become fruitless?

There are twenty-eight persons.

Which twenty-eight?

Namely: [1] one of mild faculty, [2] one of sharp faculty, [3] one intoxicated with lust, [4] one intoxicated with hatred, [5] one intoxicated with delusion, [6] one intoxicated with conceit, [7] one intoxicated with applied thought (*vitarka*), [8] the balanced person (*samaprāpta*), [9] one of slight-dust nature (*mandarajaska*), [10] the practitioner (*pratipannaka*), [11] the fruit-attainer (*phalastha*), [12] the faith-follower (*śraddhānusārin*), [13] the dharma-follower (*dharmānusārin*), [14] [Conjectural: the faith-resolved one], [15] the view-attainer (*dṛṣṭiprāpta*), [16] the body-witness (*kāyasākṣin*), [17] [Conjectural: the seven-times-at-most one], [18] the family-to-family one (*kulaṁkula*), [19] the one-interval one (*ekavīcika*), [20] one [attaining] final-nirvāṇa-in-between (*antarāparinirvāyin*), [21] one [attaining] final-nirvāṇa-on-rebirth (*upapadyaparinirvāyin*), [Lacuna] [23] one [attaining] final-nirvāṇa-with-effort (*sābhisaṁskāra-parinirvāyin*), [24] the upstream-goer (*ūrdhvasrotas*), [25] the sometimes-liberated one (*samaya-vimukta*), [26] one of unshakable dharma (*akopya-dharman*), [27] [Conjectural: the wisdom-liberated one], and [28] the both-ways-liberated one (*ubhayatobhāga-vimukta*).

What is the person of mild faculty?

The person whose faculties are mild, slow-functioning, weak-functioning, in [their relation to] the knowable entity — as before. He is again to be understood as twofold: [one mild] from the beginning [by virtue of] mild-faculty lineage, and [one mild because his] faculties [are] not-yet-cultivated.

What is the person of sharp faculty?

The person whose faculties are not slow-functioning, not weak-functioning, in [their relation to] the knowable entity — as before. He again is to be understood as twofold: [one sharp] from the beginning [by virtue of] sharp-faculty lineage, and [one sharp because his] faculties [are] cultivated.

What is the lust-intoxicated person?

The person by whom, in previous other births, lust was resorted to, cultivated, [and] made much of — he, by that cause, by that condition, now has intense lust and prolonged lust toward a lust-arousing thing. This is called the lust-intoxicated person.

What is the hatred-intoxicated person?

The person by whom, in previous other births, hatred was resorted to, cultivated, [and] made much of — by that cause, by that condition, [he] now has intense hatred and prolonged hatred toward a hatred-arousing thing. This is called the hatred-intoxicated person.

What is the delusion-intoxicated person?

The person by whom, in previous other births, delusion was resorted to, cultivated, [and] made much of — by that cause, by that condition, [he] now has intense delusion and prolonged delusion toward a delusion-arousing thing. This is called the delusion-intoxicated person.

What is the conceit-intoxicated person?

The person by whom, in previous other births, conceit was resorted to, cultivated, [and] made much of — by that cause, by that condition, [he] now has intense conceit and prolonged conceit toward a conceit-arousing thing.

This is called the conceit-intoxicated person.

What is the applied-thought-intoxicated person?

The person by whom, in previous other births, applied thought was resorted to, cultivated, [and] made much of — by that cause, by that condition, [he] now has intense applied-thought and prolonged applied-thought toward an applied-thought-arousing thing. This is called the applied-thought-intoxicated person.

What is the balanced person (*samaprāpta*)?

The person by whom, in previous other births, lust, hatred, delusion, conceit, [and] applied-thought were not resorted to, cultivated, [or] made much of, and [for whom] these dharmas were not seen as dangerous, [not] found fault with, [not] thoroughly examined — he, by that cause, by that condition, in a lust-arousing or hatred-arousing [Lacuna] or conceit-arousing or applied-thought-arousing thing, does not have intense lust, nor prolonged lust, and lust does not become manifest in him through that thing. As with lust, so with hatred, delusion, conceit, [and] applied-thought. This is called the balanced person.

What is the slight-dust person (*mandarajaska*)?

The person by whom, in previous other births, lust was not resorted to, [not] made much of, and [who] has often seen it as dangerous, found fault with it, thoroughly examined it — he, by that cause, by that condition, now toward a lust-arousing thing [that is] encountered, present, strong, abundant, [and] glaring, produces [Conjectural: slight] lust; toward a middle or small one, he does not produce [it]. As with lust, so with hatred, delusion, conceit, [and] applied-thought are to be understood. This is called the slight-dust person.

What is the practitioner person?

Answer: there are four practitioner persons, namely: the practitioner for the fruit of stream-entry, the practitioner for the fruit of once-returning, the practitioner for the fruit of non-returning, [and] the practitioner for the fruit of arhatship. This is called the practitioner person.

What is the fruit-attainer person?

Answer: the stream-enterer, the once-returner, the non-returner, [and] the arhat. This is called the fruit-attainer person.

What is the faith-follower person?

The person who seeks instruction-and-admonition from another and by its force practices, namely, for the attainment of the [path-]fruit, [because] in the dharmas as grasped, as mastered, reflected on, weighed, [and] examined, he is not himself capable, [not] fit, to follow those dharmas by way of cultivation. He practices only with a wisdom that follows [another] person — therefore he is called faith-follower.

What is the dharma-follower person?

The person who, in the dharmas as heard, mastered, reflected on, weighed, [and] examined, is himself capable, fit to follow those dharmas by way of cultivation, [and]

does not seek instruction-and-admonition from another, namely, for the attainment of the [path-]fruit — this is called the dharma-follower person.

What is the faith-resolved person (*śraddhādhimukta*)?

That faith-follower person, at the moment when he attains [Conjectural: the recluse-fruit], touches it, [and] realizes it by the path of instruction-and-admonition from another — at that moment he is called faith-resolved.

What is the view-attainer person (*dṛṣṭiprāpta*)?

Answer: that very dharma-follower person, at the moment when he attains the recluse-fruit, touches it, [and] realizes it — at that moment he is called view-attainer.

What is the body-witness person?

This person attains the eight liberations (*vimokṣa*) in forward and reverse order, and rises out [of them], and, having realized [them] with the body, dwells much [therein], but does not entirely attain the destruction of the fluxes — this is called the body-witness person.

What is the seven-times-at-most person (*saptakṛdbhavaparama*)?

This person, by the abandonment of the three fetters — personality-view (*satkāya-dṛṣṭi*), clinging-to-virtue-and-vow (*śīlavrata-parāmarśa*), [and] doubt (*vicikitsā*) — becomes a stream-enterer, of non-falling nature, certain, bound for awakening. [As] one whose maximum is seven times, [after] seven times transmigrating among the gods and humans, he makes an end of suffering. [Lacuna] Both are to be understood as stream-enterer persons.

Now, what is the one-interval person (*ekavīcika*)?

The once-returner person practicing for the fruit of non-returning, for whom the strong and middling realm-of-desire defilements are abandoned, [and] only the mild ones remain; and, having produced only one realm-of-desire existence, there itself he attains final nirvāṇa. He once returns to this [world] — this is called the one-interval [one].

What is the final-nirvāṇa-in-between person (*antarāparinirvāyin*)?

Answer: [there are] three [kinds of] final-nirvāṇa-in-between persons. The first in-between-nirvāṇa person: just upon decease, at the time of intermediate-existence-generation, [he] generates the intermediate existence and at the very moment of generation attains final nirvāṇa — like a small spark of fire, which, when arisen, is at once extinguished. The second in-between-nirvāṇa person: having generated the intermediate existence, [and] standing there some time after the generation, after some time attains final nirvāṇa — but not [so that] he has [Conjectural: approached] where the rebirth-existence is — like an iron spark that flies out from [Conjectural: iron lumps] or iron plates, heated glowing and beaten with iron hammers — so he attains final nirvāṇa. The third in-between-nirvāṇa person: having generated the intermediate existence, he approaches [the place] where the rebirth-existence [will be], and, having approached, [but] not yet reborn, attains final nirvāṇa — like an iron-spark that has flown out [and], before falling on the earth, [is extinguished] — [thus] he attains final nirvāṇa. These three intermediate-existence-nirvāṇa persons, gathered into one, are called the in-between-nirvāṇa person.

What is the upon-rebirth-nirvāṇa person (*upapadya-parinirvāyin*)?

He who, just upon being reborn, attains final nirvāṇa.

What is the without-effort-nirvāṇa person (*an-abhisaṁskāra-parinirvāyin*)?

He who, reborn there, brings the path into presence without effort, without exertion, without exhaustion, and attains final nirvāṇa — this is called the without-effort-nirvāṇa person.

What is the with-effort-nirvāṇa person (*sābhisaṁskāra-parinirvāyin*)?

He who, reborn there, brings the path into presence with effort, with exertion, with exhaustion, and attains final nirvāṇa — this is called the with-effort-nirvāṇa person.

What is the upstream-goer person (*ūrdhvasrotas*)?

The person who is a non-returner, [and] who, reborn in the first dhyāna, does not attain final nirvāṇa there itself, but, having passed away from there, by progressively higher rebirth, goes up to the Akaniṣṭha gods, or [up to] the neither-perception-

nor-non-perception sphere. This is called the upstream-goer person.

What is the sometimes-liberated person (*samaya-vimukta*)?

The mild-faculty-lineage person who falls back from the worldly seen-dharma pleasure-dwellings (*dṛṣṭa-dharma-sukha-vihāra*), or thinks of [his own] death, or protects [his] liberation, [having] fallen into intense fear of heedlessness; namely, taking that very [tendency to] falling-back as predominant, [only] that much is his wholesome side, and, through the passing of those nights and days, moments, instants, [and] hours, he does not greatly proceed to distinction, so long as he does not make intense effort. This is called the sometimes-liberated person.

What is the unshakable-dharma person (*akopya-dharman*)?

By the reverse of [the above], the unshakable-dharma person is to be understood.

What is the wisdom-liberated person (*prajñā-vimukta*)?

The person who entirely attains the destruction of the fluxes, but does not dwell, having attained, realizing the eight liberations with the body. This is called the wisdom-liberated person.

What is the both-ways-liberated person (*ubhayato-bhāga-vimukta*)?

The person who entirely attains the destruction of the fluxes [and] dwells having attained the eight liberations with the body. His mind is liberated from the obstacle of defilement (*kleśa-āvaraṇa*) and from the obstacle to liberation (*vimokṣa-āvaraṇa*) — this is called the both-ways-liberated person.

What is the delineation of persons?

The delineation of persons is to be understood by eleven divisions. By which eleven? Namely: by division of faculty, by division of community (*nikāya*), by division of conduct, by division of vow, by division of practice, by division of path-fruit, by division of application, by division of attainment, by division of rebirth, by division of falling-back, [and] by division of obstacle.

By division of faculty, [there is] the delineation of mild-faculty and sharp-faculty persons.

By division of community (*nikāya*), the delineation of seven kinds of person: monk, nun, female probationer, male novice, female novice, layman, laywoman.

Now, by division of conduct, the delineation of seven persons: he who is the lust-intoxicated person is lust-conducted; he who is hatred-intoxicated is hatred-conducted; he who is delusion-intoxicated is delusion-conducted; he who is conceit-intoxicated is conceit-conducted; he who is applied-thought-intoxicated is applied-thought-conducted; the balanced person (*samaprāpta*) is even-balanced in conduct; the slight-dust person (*mandarajaska*) is slight in conduct — [thus they are] to be understood.

Now, what are the marks of the lust-conducted person?

Here the lust-conducted person, [even] toward a slight, wholly inferior lust-arousing thing, produces a dense, strong lust-obsession —

what more toward a middle or fine [thing]?

And that lust-obsession he maintains in continuity for a long time, [remains] bound [by it] for a long time. Through that obsession, he is overcome by lust-arousing dharmas, but he is not able to overcome the lust-arousing dharmas. He is of smooth faculties, not of stiff faculties, [Conjectural: not] of rough faculties, not of [Conjectural: coarse] faculties; not too much harming others — namely by body or by speech; [yet he is] hard to detach, hard to stir; of inferior conviction; firm in undertakings, steady in undertakings; of firm vow, of stable vow; magnanimous; greedy by nature for requisites and provisions, giving great weight to them; full of joy, full of delight, free from frowning, of open-faced complexion, accompanied by a smile — these and the like are to be understood as the marks of the lust-conducted person.

What are the marks of the hatred-conducted person?

Here the hatred-conducted person, toward a hatred-arousing thing, [even] by a slight sign of an aversion-provoking thing (*pratigha-vastu-nimitta*), produces a dense, abundant aversion-obsession —

what more by a middle or strong [one]?

And that aversion-obsession he maintains in continuity for a long time, [remains] bound [by it] for a long time. Through aversion-obsession, he is overcome by hatred-arousing dharmas, but cannot overcome the hatred-arousing dharmas. He is of withered faculties, of stiff faculties, of rough faculties, of coarse faculties, and is excessively harming others by nature — namely by body and by speech. Yet he is easy to detach, easy to stir; [but] he is clamorous, talkative, brazen, of much non-conviction; not firm in undertakings, not steady in undertakings; not of firm vow, not of stable vow; full of dejection, full of distress; impatient, not magnanimous; of perverse nature; one who takes things the wrong way, hard to bring around, full of grudges; of cruel intent, fierce, taking offense, replying word-for-word — even when slightly addressed, he is offended, becomes angry, bears ill will; sullen, [he] stands [thus], generates anger; of frown-marked face, of non-open face, hating others’ fortune, full of envy — these and the like are to be understood as the marks of the hatred-conducted person.

Now, what are the marks of the delusion-conducted person?

Here the delusion-conducted person, [even] toward a slight delusion-arousing thing, produces a dense, abundant delusion-obsession — [and] still more toward a middle or strong [thing]; and he maintains the continuity of that delusion-obsession for a long time, and is bound by it. He is overcome by delusion-arousing dharmas, but cannot overcome the delusion-arousing dharmas. He is of slow faculties, of dull faculties, of intoxicated faculties, of loose bodily action; an ill-thought-thinker, ill-speech-speaker, ill-deed-doer; lazy, not endowed with [the capacity for] rising; of limited capacity, dull-witted; of lost mindfulness; not dwelling with full awareness; a wrong-grasper, hard to detach, hard to stir, of inferior conviction; born deaf-mute, [Conjectural: speaking by hand signs], unfit to understand the meaning of well-spoken and ill-spoken [teachings], and by nature led along by conditions, other-led, other-driven — these and the like are to be understood as the marks of the delusion-conducted person.

Now, what are the marks of the conceit-conducted person?

Here the conceit-conducted person, [even] toward a slight conceit-arousing thing, produces a dense conceit-obsession —

what more toward a middle or strong [one]?

And the continuity of that conceit-obsession he maintains for a long time, and is bound [by it]. He is overcome by conceit-arousing dharmas, but cannot overcome the conceit-arousing dharmas. He is of restless faculties, of elevated faculties; engaged in adorning the body, he speaks an excessively haughty speech, not lowered [in tone]; and toward [Conjectural: parents,] teachers and those in the place of teacher he does not, from time to time, pay the proper respect. He is stiff, of unbowed body, not given to salutation, bowing, rising, joined palms, and proper service; self-grasping, self-exalting, other-deprecating; desirous of gain, desirous of honor, desirous of fame, voice, [and] eulogy; frivolous, given to jesting, [Conjectural: hard to detach], hard to stir, of lofty inclination, of weak compassion, and is full of strong views of self, being, soul, nourisher, person, and individual, and of resentment,

and full of grudges — these and the like are to be understood as the marks of the conceit-conducted person. Now, what are the marks of the applied-thought-conducted person?

Here the applied-thought-conducted person, [even] toward a slight applied-thought-arousing thing, produces a dense obsession, and that obsession he maintains for a long time, and is bound [by it]. He is overcome by applied-thought-arousing dharmas, but cannot overcome the applied-thought-arousing dharmas. He is of unstable faculties, of fickle faculties, of restless faculties, of distracted faculties; of unstable bodily action, [Conjectural: erring in verbal action]; hard to detach, hard to stir; fond of [conceptual] proliferation (*prapaṁca*), delighting in proliferation; full of doubt, full of indecision; and of varied desire, of unstable vow, of undetermined vow; of unstable action, of undetermined action; full of suspicion, of lost mindfulness; non-delighting in seclusion, full of distraction; following desire-and-lust in worldly variety; skilful, not lazy — these and the like are to be understood as the marks of the applied-thought-conducted person.

This is the delineation of persons by division of conduct, [thus] to be understood.

Now, the delineation of persons by division of vow (*praṇidhāna*): there is a person who has made his vow in the disciple-vehicle, there is one in the solitary-buddha-vehicle, [and] there is one in the great vehicle. Now, this person who has made his vow in the disciple-vehicle may be of disciple-lineage, may be of solitary-buddha-lineage, [or] may be of great-vehicle-lineage. [Likewise,] this person who has made his vow in [the path of] solitary awakening may also be of solitary-buddha-lineage, of disciple-lineage, [or] of great-vehicle-lineage. [And] this person who has made his vow in the great vehicle may also be of disciple-lineage, may be of solitary-buddha-lineage, [or] may be of great-vehicle-lineage. [Now,] this disciple-lineage person who has made his vow in solitary awakening or in supreme perfect awakening — he, on account of his disciple-lineage, necessarily at the final time will turn back that vow and stand only in the disciple-vehicle vow. Thus the solitary-buddha-lineage and great-vehicle-lineage [persons] are to be understood. For these persons there is accumulation of the vow and alteration of the vow, but no accumulation of lineage or alteration of lineage. In this context, these persons are to be understood as of disciple-vehicle vow and of disciple-lineage.

Thus is the delineation of persons by division of vow.

How is the delineation of persons by division of practice (*pratipat-prabheda*)?

Of these persons just pointed out [and] proclaimed, [their] going forth is by four practices.

By which four?

There is the painful practice with slow knowledge; there is the painful practice with quick knowledge; there is the pleasant practice with slow knowledge; [and] there is the pleasant practice with quick knowledge.

Of the mild-faculty person [Conjectural: who has not attained the basic dhyāna], the practice is called painful, with slow knowledge. Of the sharp-faculty person [Conjectural: who has not attained the basic dhyāna], the practice is called painful, with quick knowledge. Of the mild-faculty person who has attained the basic dhyāna (*mauladhyāna*), the practice is called pleasant, with slow knowledge. Of the sharp-faculty person who has attained the basic dhyāna, the practice is called pleasant, with quick knowledge. Thus the delineation of persons by division of practice is to be understood.

Now, how is the delineation of persons by division of path-fruit to be understood?

Namely, of the four practitioners — practitioner for the fruit of stream-entry, of once-returning, of non-returning, [and] of arhatship — and of the four fruit-attainers — stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, [and] arhat — and of those for whom the practitioner-paths [still] proceed: they are practitioners; their delineation is by the practitioner-path. Those established in the recluse-fruits, [up to] the arhat-fruit — their delineation is by the path-fruit. Thus is the delineation of persons by division of path-fruit.

How is the delineation of persons by division of application?

Namely, by [the categories of] faith-follower and dharma-follower. The person who is applied [to the path] by faith-following is the faith-follower; the one applied to the dharmas by following a discipline [Conjectural: not] dependent on another’s condition is the dharma-follower. Thus is the delineation of persons by division of application.

Now, how is the delineation of persons by division of attainment (*samāpatti*)?

Namely, of the body-witness, who, having realized the eight liberations with the body, dwells having attained [them], and does not entirely attain the destruction of the fluxes. [Being] formed [himself], he sees forms. Inwardly with non-form perception, [yet] externally he sees forms. He realizes the pure liberation with the body and dwells having attained [it]. The sphere of infinite space, of infinite consciousness, of nothingness, of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and the cessation of perception-and-feeling he attains and rises out of, in forward and reverse order. Thus is the delineation of persons by division of attainment.

How is the delineation of persons by division of rebirth?

Namely, [the delineation] of the seven-times-at-most, of the family-to-family, of the one-interval, of the in-between-nirvāṇa, of the upon-rebirth-nirvāṇa, [Lacuna] [and] of the upstream-goer. Thus is the delineation of persons by division of rebirth.

How is the delineation of persons by division of falling-back [and non-falling-back]?

Namely, of the sometimes-liberated arhat, who is liable to fall back from the pleasant dwellings of the present life; and the delineation by the division of non-falling-back [is]

of the unshakable arhat, who is not liable to fall back from the pleasant dwellings of the present life. Thus is the delineation of persons by division of falling-back [and non-falling-back].

Now, how is the delineation of persons by division of obstacle (*āvaraṇa*)?

Namely, of the wisdom-liberated and both-ways-liberated arhats. The wisdom-liberated arhat is liberated from the obstacle of defilement, [but] not from the obstacle of attainment (*samāpatti-āvaraṇa*); the both-ways-liberated, however, the arhat, is liberated from the obstacle of defilement and from the obstacle of attainment — therefore he is called both-ways-liberated. Thus is the delineation of persons by division of obstacle. — By these three divisions, as pointed out, the delineation of the persons as pointed out is, in order, to be understood.

What is the defilement-purifying object?

Answer: The grossness of the lower planes: namely, [that] of the realm of desire relative to the first dhyāna, and so on up to [that of the sphere of nothingness relative to] the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

What, in this context, is grossness?

Grossness is of two kinds: grossness in essential nature (*svabhāva-audārika*), and grossness in number/quantity (*saṁkhyā-audārika*). As for grossness in essential nature: in the realm of desire the five aggregates are [also] present, but relative to the first dhyāna, those that pertain to the realm of desire are more flawed and more painful in their abiding, and shorter-lasting, and inferior, and more rejected. This is their grossness in essential nature relative to the first dhyāna; and so those [of the first dhyāna] are said to be calmer and more excellent.

What is grossness in quantity?

The form-aggregate pertaining to the realm of desire is more abundant, to be fully known and abandoned; and likewise up to the consciousness-aggregate. This is called grossness [Conjectural: in quantity].

Likewise in the upper planes, grossness in essential nature and grossness in quantity are to be understood as appropriate to each case. But in the upper planes, [going] up to the sphere of nothingness, all the lower planes should be understood as more gross relative to those above, more painful in abiding, and shorter-lasting; the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is simply calm, since there is no plane above it that is more excellent. Here the meaning of disadvantage, in brief, is the meaning of grossness: whichever plane has greater disadvantage, that, in respect of disadvantage, is called gross; [and] the plane on which there is less disadvantage is called, in respect of disadvantage, calm. This is the defilement-purifying object of worldlings, by the worldly path: for one who sees the lower plane as disadvantage, as disease, as without security, and the upper plane as calm. [Thus] the defilements of the lower planes, [going] up through the sphere of nothingness, beginning with the realm of desire, are abandoned, but not abandoned absolutely; they are subject to re-arising. By the supramundane path, on the other hand, the defilement-purifying object is fourfold, namely: the truth of suffering, the truth of origin, the truth of cessation, and the truth of the path.

Now, what is the truth of suffering?

Namely: birth is suffering, aging too is suffering, illness [is suffering], death [is suffering], union with the unloved [is suffering], separation from the loved [is suffering], and frustration of desire [is suffering] — in short, the five aggregates of clinging are suffering. The noble truth of origin is craving — productive of further existence, bound up with delight and lust, taking delight here and there.

The noble truth of cessation is the complete abandonment of that very craving.

The truth of the path is the noble eightfold path. The fourfold delineation of [these] truths is made by reference to the dark side and the bright side, by the structure of cause and effect:

the truth of suffering is the effect, the truth of origin is the cause [on the dark side]; the truth of cessation is the effect, the truth of the path is the cause [on the bright side] — for the attainment, for the direct touching [of cessation]. The truth of suffering is like the illness: it is to be fully known first. The truth of origin is like the cause of the illness: it is to be discarded next. The truth of cessation is like health: it is to be touched, to be realized directly. The truth of the path is like the medicine: it is to be resorted to, to be cultivated, to be made much of. And [each] is real, just so, not otherwise, not contradicted, not perverted: suffering by way of being suffering, [and so on] up to the path by way of being the path — therefore [each] is called a truth. And its specific characteristic does not deceive, and from seeing it unperverted cognitions arise. For that reason it is called truth.

Why, again, are these truths only of the noble ones (*ārya*)?

The noble ones alike regard them [as truths]; they know and see them as truths, as they actually are. The foolish, on the other hand, do not know, [do not] see [them] as they actually are. Therefore they are called noble truths. For the foolish, this is truth by the nature of things, not by being understood; for the noble ones, in both ways. ‘[Birth is] suffering’: for one being born, painful feeling arises, bodily and mental; but birth itself is not the suffering — it is the cause of suffering; and so on up to ‘frustration of desire is suffering’:

Suffering arises caused by frustration of desire, [suffering both] bodily and mental; but the frustration of desire itself is not the [felt] suffering — it is rather the cause of suffering, and [the same holds] for the rest. ‘In brief, the five aggregates of clinging are suffering’: by these modes beginning with birth only the suffering-of-suffering (*duḥkha-duḥkhatā*) is elucidated; the suffering of change and the suffering of conditioning (*saṁskāra-duḥkhatā*) remain. But these are elucidated by the suffering of the five aggregates, for the five aggregates of clinging are pervaded by the three feelings, and are the vessel of the suffering-of-suffering just described, and [also of] the suffering of change, not [explicitly] stated, and [of] the suffering of conditioning — these too are to be seen in just these aggregates. For what reason, then, did the Blessed One proclaim the suffering-of-suffering alone

by its own term, but the suffering of change and the suffering of conditioning [only] by another mode?

Because regarding the suffering-of-suffering, for the noble ones and the foolish alike, the cognition of suffering arises equally. And the suffering-of-suffering is exceedingly sobering; and for those who have not cultivated wisdom before, when it is taught so, there is easy entry into the truths for those to be trained.

How, then, is the threefold suffering delineated?

Now, what was said — ‘suffering is birth, [and so on] through to frustration of desire is suffering’ — by this the painful feeling with its basis is declared.

And that is the suffering-of-suffering. This is the delineation of the suffering-of-suffering. [Now] those dharmas which are its opposite — namely youth [as opposed] to aging, health to illness, life to death, union with the loved to union with the unloved, separation from the unloved [as opposed] to separation from the loved, [and] gratification of desire to its frustration — and the defilements, with their basis, that proceed in painful feeling, and the defilements that proceed in regard to dharmas that are occasions of pleasure, such as health, and in regard to the feeling born of them — this is called the suffering of change.

A pleasant feeling with its basis, [being] impermanent, undergoes change, [and] because [Conjectural: becoming otherwise] predominates, it produces suffering. The defilements, again, wherever they arise, are painful by the very force of obsession, and this is [the] changed-state of mind. Hence it is called the suffering of change.

As was said by the Blessed One: ‘with a mind overcome and changed, the taking of a woman by the hand’ — and so on at length.

And as was said: ‘Gripped by the obsessive desire of sense-lust and conditioned by that sense-desire obsession, he experiences mental pain and dejection born thereof.’ Likewise [for one] gripped by ill-will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-regret, [and] doubt. Hence, by this scriptural tradition, supremely attested, the suffering-character is found in defilements, and so is the change-character. Therefore one speaks of defilement-as-change-suffering. This is the delineation of the suffering of change. The suffering of conditioning, again, is all-pervasive in the aggregates of clinging: in brief, setting aside [Conjectural: whatever] suffering-of-suffering [there is], the change-nature comprised under defilement, and the pleasant feeling with its basis, the remaining aggregates — [Conjectural: accompanied by neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling], born from it, conditions for its arising, and vessels for its abiding once arisen — this is called the suffering of conditioning.

The aggregates [are] impermanent, marked by arising and vanishing, accompanied by clinging, attached to the three feelings, afflicted by encumbrance (*dauṣṭhulya*), fallen into insecurity, not released from the suffering-of-suffering and the suffering of change, and not proceeding under their own control — this is called suffering by way of the suffering of conditioning. This is the delineation of the suffering of conditioning. Here ‘craving’ (*tṛṣṇā*), ‘longing,’ ‘yearning,’ [and] ‘delighting’ are synonyms. That yearning, again, proceeds along three avenues: yearning for further existence, and yearning for sense-objects. The yearning for further existence is the craving that is productive of re-becoming. Yearning for sense-objects is twofold: [a] with respect to sense-objects already attained, accompanied by joy and clinging to them; and [b] with respect to sense-objects not yet attained, accompanied by longing for union with them. Of these, the one with respect to objects attained, accompanied by joy and clinging, is what is called ‘accompanied by delight and lust.’ The one with respect to objects not yet attained, accompanied by longing for union, is what is called ‘delighting here and there.’ Cessation, too, is twofold: cessation of defilement (*kleśa*), and cessation of secondary defilement (*upakleśa*). The path, too, is twofold: that of the trainee (*śaikṣa*) and that of the non-trainee (*aśaikṣa*). This defilement-purifying object is to be understood as belonging to the supramundane path. Thus he speaks of a fourfold object: the pervasive object (*vyāpyālambana*), the conduct-purifying object (*caritaviśodhana*), the skill object (*kauśalyālambana*), and the defilement-purifying object (*kleśaviśodhana*).

Now, what is instruction (*avavāda*)?

Fourfold instruction: unperverted instruction, graduated instruction, instruction from tradition, and instruction from direct realization.

What is unperverted instruction?

That by which one teaches [Conjectural: the Dharma and its meaning] in an unperverted way, [and] brings the hearer to grasp it — [teaching what is] real, which leads him out, for the right destruction of suffering, for the making of an end to suffering — this is called unperverted instruction.

What is graduated instruction?

When in due time one teaches the Dharma, [and] points out first of all the more accessible matters, has them learnt, has them recited, and afterwards the deep ones — first, for the realization of the first truth, one instructs concerning that, [and] afterwards concerning the truths of origin, cessation, and path; [or] for the attainment of the first dhyāna, one instructs first concerning that, [and] afterwards concerning the other dhyānas and attainments. This kind of instruction is to be known as graduated instruction. Instruction from tradition is what one has received from teachers or those in the place of teacher — from preceptors and instructors who know yoga, or from the Tathāgata, or from a disciple of the Tathāgata — and [transmits], neither subtracting nor adding, instructing others. This is called instruction from tradition. Instruction from direct realization: [the case in which] one to whom these dharmas have become known, touched, contacted, [and] made manifest — having dwelt alone, secluded — [then,] for the attainment of [the same] by others, for their touching, for their making-manifest, instructs [them]. This is called instruction from direct realization. There is, moreover, a wholly complete instruction, of every aspect.

What is it?

That by which one instructs through the three wonders (*prātihārya*): the wonder of psychic power, the wonder of mind-reading, and the wonder of admonition. By the wonder of psychic power, one displays manifold ranges of psychic power and so engenders respect [for oneself] in others, such that, born of respect, they become eager in applying the ear and in attention. Then, by the wonder of mind-reading, one investigates the operations of their mind; by the wonder of admonition, one teaches the Dharma to suit their faculties, their conduct, [and] their access [to teaching], and admonishes [them] by appropriate antidotes. Thereby this instruction, subsumed under the three wonders, is the complete instruction.

Now, what is training (*śikṣā*)?

Answer: the three trainings — training in higher virtue (*adhiśīla*), training in higher mind (*adhicitta*), [and] training in higher wisdom (*adhiprajñā*).

What is the training in higher virtue?

Just as ‘… he dwells possessed of virtue,’ and so on in detail, as above.

The training in higher mind: secluded from sense-desires, secluded from evil unwholesome dharmas, with applied and sustained thought, born of seclusion, accompanied by joy and pleasure, and with one-pointedness of mind — he enters and abides in the first dhyāna, [and so on] up to the fourth dhyāna. This is the training in higher mind. Moreover, all the formless attainments and other concentration-attainments are called training in higher mind. But it is in reliance on the dhyānas that the realization of the truths, the entry [into the fixed course], first comes about, not entirely without the dhyānas. Therefore the dhyānas, taken as primary, are called the training in higher mind. The training in higher wisdom is the knowing of the four noble truths as they actually are.

For what reason are there just three trainings, no more?

Answer: in the sense of ‘establishment of concentration’; in the sense of ‘support of knowledge’; and in the sense of ‘doing the task’. [That is,] in the sense of ‘establishment of concentration’, [there is] the training in higher virtue — [that] training; for, having been established in virtue, one touches the one-pointedness of mind,

the concentration of mind.

In the sense of ‘support of knowledge’, [there is] the training in higher mind. For when the mind is concentrated, one-pointed, knowing and seeing of the knowable entity as it actually is proceeds by mindfulness. In the sense of ‘doing the task’, [there is] the training in higher wisdom; for by perfectly purified knowing-and-seeing one directly realizes the abandonment of defilements. For this is one’s own good, this is the supreme task — namely, the abandonment of defilements; Beyond that there is nothing further to be done; therefore there are just these three trainings. What, moreover, is the sequence of these trainings? In one of well-purified virtue there is [Conjectural: no] regret; in the regret-free, gladness; in gladness, joy; in joy, pliancy; in pliancy, pleasure; in the pleased, mental concentration. The concentrated mind knows things as they actually are, sees [them] as they actually are; knowing and seeing [them] as they actually are, one grows weary; wearied, one becomes detached; detached, one is liberated; liberated, without clinging, one attains final nirvāṇa. Thus these virtues, when cultivated, lead on to the peak — namely, to nirvāṇa without clinging. This is the sequence of these trainings.

For what reason is the training in higher virtue called ‘higher virtue’?

Similarly higher mind [and] higher wisdom — [each is so called] by reason of referent and by reason of [Conjectural: pre-eminence]. How [is it so called] by reason of referent? With higher mind as referent, virtue is the training in higher virtue. With higher wisdom as referent, mental concentration is the training in higher mind. With reference to abandoning defilement, that knowing-and-seeing is the training in higher wisdom. [Conjectural: How by reason of pre-eminence?] [Lacuna] And whichever is the training in higher mind, and whichever is the training in higher wisdom — these trainings are unique to this teaching, not shared with those outside it. Thus [they are called ‘higher’] by reason of pre-eminence. There is also a training in higher mind that brings on the training in higher wisdom, and a training in higher wisdom that brings on the training in higher mind. For example: the noble disciple, not yet possessed of the basic dhyānas, a trainee, one who has seen the track (*dṛṣṭapada*), afterwards engaging for the abandonment of the defilements to be abandoned by cultivation, cultivates the awakening-factor of mindfulness. This is the training in higher wisdom that brings on the training in higher mind. The training in higher mind that brings on the training in higher wisdom was stated earlier.

Here: there is training in higher virtue without higher mind or higher wisdom; there is higher-virtue-with-higher-mind without higher wisdom; but there is no training in higher wisdom without both higher virtue and higher mind. Therefore where higher wisdom is [being] trained, there all three trainings are to be understood [as present].

This much is the delineation of the trainings. Here a yogin engaged in yoga should train. Three [kinds of] person attain the truths: which three? Namely, one [wholly] not free from lust, one largely free from lust, and one wholly free from lust. The one wholly not free from lust, attaining the truths, with the realization of the truths becomes a stream-enterer. The one largely free from lust becomes a once-returner. The one wholly free from lust, with the realization of the truths, becomes a non-returner. [There are] three faculties: the faculty ‘I shall come to know what is not yet known’ (*anājñātam-ājñāsyāmi-indriya*), the faculty of knowing (*ājñendriya*), [and] the faculty of one who has known.

How is the delineation of these faculties made?

For one engaged in the realization of truths not yet realized, [there is] the delineation of the faculty ‘I shall come to know the unknown.’ For the trainee who has realized [them], [there is] the delineation of the faculty of knowing. For the non-trainee, the arhat, whose task is done, [there is] the delineation of the faculty of one who has known. [There are] three doors of liberation (*vimokṣa-mukha*), namely: emptiness (*śūnyatā*), wishlessness (*apraṇihita*), [and] signlessness (*animitta*). How is the delineation of these three doors of liberation made? Answer: there are these two, the conditioned and the unconditioned. Of them, the conditioned is the five aggregates bound up with the three realms; the unconditioned is nirvāṇa. This pair, what is conditioned and what is unconditioned, is what is spoken of [as real]. But what is spoken of as self, being, soul, or person — that is unreal. Now, from seeing fault and disadvantage in the conditioned, no wish toward it arises; from this absence of wishing, the door of liberation of wishlessness is delineated. In nirvāṇa, on the other hand, for one possessed of the wish, there is wishing — seeing it as calm, as sublime, and as the way out (*niḥsaraṇa*); and from this seeing-the-way-out, the door of liberation of signlessness is delineated. Now, in what is unreal, non-existent, there is no wishing at all. ‘Just as it is unreal, so it is unreal’ — for one who knows and sees it [thus], the door of liberation of emptiness is delineated. Thus the delineation of the three doors of liberation is made.

Now, what are the dharmas conducive to training (*śikṣānulomika-dharma*)?

Answer: there are ten dharmas adverse to training, [and] as antidotes to them, ten dharmas conducive to training are to be understood. What are the dharmas adverse to training? Namely: a woman — young, of stately complexion, captivating — for a son of good family engaged in training, an extreme obstacle, a waylayer; [Conjectural: craving] for the formations comprised in the personality (*satkāya*); laziness, indolence; personality-view; lust for taste, dependent on morsel food; desire, lust and attachment in manifold worldly tales of every kind; the fault of dharma-reflection and yoga-attention. And what is that? Namely: regarding [Conjectural: the Jewels], or the truths, or the aggregates, or the fruit of action, [Lacuna]; for one engaged in exertion, bodily encumbrance; for one who is slack, attention to the faults of *śamatha* (calm) and *vipaśyanā* (insight), or the overcoming of the mind by sloth-and-torpor, contraction of mind; for one of [Conjectural: over-exerted] energy, bodily fatigue [and] mental distress; for one of overly slack energy, failure to attain distinction, [and] the exhaustion of the wholesome side; through [Conjectural: gain], or fame, or praise, or simply some trivial pleasure of one sort or another — delight, joy, restlessness, lack of quieting, anxiety and agitated elation; at [the prospect of] the cessation of personality, at [the prospect of] nirvāṇa, alarm, paralysis; [Damaged] application in immoderate measure, excessive talking, [and,] while speaking a Dharma-talk, taking up disputatious talk and pursuing it; in objects previously seen, heard, or experienced — manifold and of many kinds — scattering of mind, distraction of mind, and, in impermanent formations, brooding fixation. — These dharmas are to be understood as the faults of reflection and yoga-attention. savoring the pleasure of attainment in dhyāna; following after the signs of formations for one who wishes to enter the signless attainment; [and] for one touched by bodily feelings, painful and even life-taking, [Conjectural: craving] for life, hope for life, and, for one given over to that hope, grieving, exhaustion, lamentation. These are the ten dharmas adverse to training.

What ten dharmas, as antidotes to these ten dharmas adverse to the bases of training, are conducive to training?

Namely: perception of the unattractive (*aśubha*), [Lacuna] perception of suffering in the impermanent, perception of non-self in suffering, perception of food as repugnant, perception of non-delight in the whole world, perception of light (*āloka-saṁjñā*), perception of dispassion (*virāga*), perception of cessation (*nirodha*), [and] perception of death (*maraṇa*). These ten perceptions, resorted to, cultivated, [and] made much of, lead to the abandonment of the tenfold obstructor of training, the ten dharmas adverse to training. Now: the light of Dharma, the light of meaning, the light of *śamatha*, and the light of *vipaśyanā* — making these lights predominant, the perception of light is what is intended in this connection, [as regards] dharma-reflection and yoga-attention, for the abandonment of the obstruction [to them].

Now, another ten dharmas conducive to training are to be understood.

Which ten?

Namely: prior cause, appropriate instruction, thorough application, continual respectful exertion, intense desire, the establishment of the power of yoga, the pacification of bodily and mental encumbrance, repeated reflection, non-distress, and freedom from conceit. — What is the prior cause? That which is the prior ripening of the faculties, and the perfecting of the faculties. What is appropriate instruction? That instruction which is unperverted and graduated.

What is thorough application?

Just as one has been instructed, so does one apply oneself; and applying himself thus, he produces right view.

What is continual respectful exertion?

By sixfold application he does not die in vain with respect to the wholesome side, and quickly brings the wholesome side to completion.

What is intense desire?

As when he generates longing for the higher liberation: ‘When shall I enter and dwell in that sphere in which the noble ones, having entered, dwell?’

What is the establishment of the power of yoga?

By two causes one comes to possess the power of yoga: by being sharp-faculty by nature, and by long-time practice. The [Conjectural: pacification] of bodily and mental encumbrance: as when, for one whose body is wearied, fatigued, body-encumbrance and mind-encumbrance arise — he pacifies them by adopting a different posture; [when] through excessive applied thought and sustained thought bodily and mental encumbrance arises, then by application to [Conjectural: inward] *śamatha* of mind he pacifies it; [when] through contraction of mind, through mental lethargy, an obsession of sloth-and-torpor arises, he pacifies that bodily and mental encumbrance by higher-wisdom *vipaśyanā* into dharmas and by gladdening attention. Or [when,] for one whose defilements are by nature [Conjectural: not] abandoned, the bodily and mental encumbrance of the defilement class has not departed [and] continues to follow, he pacifies it by cultivation of the right path.

What is repeated reflection?

With respect to the virtues, he repeatedly reflects on what was done badly and what was done well, [and] reviews what was left undone, and what done; he turns away from what was done badly and from what was left undone, [and] from what was done well [and] done, he does not turn back. Likewise, making investigative attention predominant with respect to the abandoned and not-yet-abandoned defilements, he reflects repeatedly; [and,] knowing [them] abandoned, [Lacuna] again and again he cultivates that very path. What is non-distress? [As to] what is in time to be known, to be seen, to be attained, [but] not knowing, not seeing, not attaining that, distress arises: mental fatigue, mental affliction; when it has arisen, he does not accommodate it, [but] abandons it.

What is freedom from conceit?

With respect to direct realization and attainment, with respect to touching, he is free from conceit. He grasps without distortion: with respect to what is attained, he has the perception ‘attained’; with respect to what is realized, the perception ‘realized.’ Thus these ten dharmas, for the yogin who desires training,

from beginning to middle to end, conform to training; they do not run counter to it. Therefore they are called dharmas conducive to training.

What, there, is loss of yoga?

Answer: four kinds of loss of yoga.

Which four?

There is final loss of yoga; there is temporary; there is loss-by-falling-away; there is loss caused by wrong practice. There, final loss of yoga is to be understood with reference to persons [Conjectural: not established in the lineage]. Because they are of a nature not to attain nirvāṇa, they are absolutely fallen away from yoga. There, the temporary: namely, those who, established in the lineage, of a nature to attain nirvāṇa, but lacking the conditions — for they, even after going far and very far, will surely come upon the conditions and, bringing forth yoga, will cultivate and reach final nirvāṇa. For them, by that very fact, the loss is only temporary. There, loss of yoga by falling away from what has been attained: as when someone here falls away from the knowledge, vision, contact and dwelling that he had attained and gained. There, loss of yoga caused by wrong practice: as when someone here applying himself unwisely does not succeed in yoga; he does not succeed in the wholesome dharma worthy of being meditated upon. As when someone here is full of defilements, of much-dust nature, and is also of great discernment, great intellect, endowed with great intelligence. He takes up the scriptures, masters the scriptures, little or much; and dwelling in the forest, by his preaching of Dharma he gratifies the minds of those who come — householders and renouncers, straight persons, straightforward by nature. By behavior pursued out of pretense, connected with body and speech, by that condition, by that means there arises for him gain, honor, fame; he becomes known, in great comfort, he becomes a recipient of robes, alms, dwelling-place, medicine for the sick, requisites, and he is honored, respected by kings, royal ministers, even by caravan leaders, and [Conjectural: reputed to be an arhat]. Around him gather disciples, householders, renouncers. [Conjectural: they are attached to him and follow him about, and to draw in more,] — whence there occurs to him: ‘There are disciples, householders and renouncers, who hold me in high regard, who hold me as great. If they were to come to me and ask a question concerning yoga, attention, *śamatha* and *vipaśyanā*, and I, asked, were to answer, ‘I do not know,’ — then the regard they have would diminish, would lessen, and I would no longer be reputed as an arhat. Better that I myself think it out, weigh it, examine it, and so establish a yoga.’ Making this very thing predominant, greedy for gain and honor, alone and in seclusion, he himself thinks it out, weighs it, examines it, and so establishes a yoga. And that yoga of his is not in the discourses, is not seen in the vinaya, and runs counter to the nature of things. Those monks who are sūtra-bearers, vinaya-bearers, mātṛkā-bearers — from them he conceals that yoga-topic and does not reveal it. Those who are his disciples, householders and renouncers — them too he instructs in the practice of that yoga. ‘Why? Lest those sūtra-bearers, vinaya-bearers, mātṛkā-bearers, hearing of this yoga-topic, should bring it to the sūtras, and it would not fit; should display it in the vinaya, and it is not seen [there]; should examine it by the nature of things, and it would contradict the nature of things. And they, on that account, would not be convinced, and would challenge me with words of disbelief, and would raise disputes. Thus I would once more not be honored, not held as great by kings, royal ministers, the wealthy, the chiefs, the caravan leaders, and would no longer be a recipient of robes, alms, dwelling, medicine for the sick, and requisites.’ Making that very desire for gain and honor predominant, perceiving as Dharma what is not Dharma, having set up a distorted perception, he elucidates non-Dharma as Dharma, proclaims it. And those who come into his view’s persuasion, they too in non-dharma have the perception of dharma. Through dullness and delusion, they have the perception of dharma in non-dharma; even when instructed accordingly and practicing, they are practicing wrongly — so it should be known. This kind of loss of yoga produced by wrong practice is a counterfeit of the true dharma, a false dharma that tends to the disappearance of the true dharma. These four kinds of loss of yoga are to be recognized and avoided by the meditating monk, the practitioner of yoga.

What, then, is yoga?

Answer: yoga is fourfold, namely faith, desire, energy, and means. Of these, faith is twofold in basis, and has the aspect of conviction and the aspect of clarity; it is based on examining the reasoning of the dharmas, and based on trust in the power of the person. Desire too is fourfold, namely for attainment, as when someone here engenders eagerness for the higher liberation, in detail as before. For inquiry, as when someone here engenders eagerness, in detail as before, for inquiry, for going to the monastery, into the presence of discerning fellow brahma-farers who know yoga, for the hearing of what has not been heard, for the clarification of what has been heard. Desire for the gathering of the requisites; as when someone here generates progressively higher eagerness for the purification of the restraint of virtue, of the restraint of the senses, knowing the right measure in food, application to wakefulness, dwelling with full awareness. Desire for application; as when one engenders longing, yearning, willingness, in continual application and in respectful application to cultivation of the path — this is the fourfold desire. namely: for attainment, for inquiry, for the gathering of the requisites, and for application. There energy too is fourfold, namely: for hearing, for reflection, for cultivation, and for purification from obstacles. There, energy for hearing: for one hearing what has not been heard, clarifying it; mental exertion and uninterrupted application. Likewise, for one who, alone and secluded, [takes] the meaning of the dharmas as heard, reflecting on it, weighing it, examining it; likewise for one who, having entered seclusion, from time to time cultivates *śamatha* and *vipaśyanā*; likewise for one who, applied night and day, purifies the mind of the hindrances by walking and sitting — whatever mental exertion and uninterrupted application [there is in these cases] — [Damaged] Means too is fourfold, namely: making the restraint of virtue and restraint of senses predominant, well-established mindfulness; and so for one whose mindfulness is established, heedfulness, the guarding of the mind, the resort to wholesome dharmas; likewise, for one who is heedful, inward application to *śamatha* of mind, and *vipaśyanā* into dharmas by higher wisdom. This yoga is fourfold and is of sixteen aspects. There, through faith, one takes the goal to be attained as predominant. Out of trust in attainment, one generates the willingness to do wholesome dharmas. So willing to act, applied night and day, he dwells enthusiastic, of firm exertion. And [the means], taken hold of in dependence on that energy — to the attainment of the not-attained, to the reaching of the not-reached, to the direct realization of the not-yet-realized — it conduces. Therefore these four dharmas are called yoga.

Now, what is attention (*manasikāra*)?

[There are] four attentions. Which four?

Namely: forceful, interrupted, [Lacuna] and effortless.

What is forceful attention?

Namely, for the beginner steadying the mind inwardly and stabilizing it, discerning dharmas, until he attains [stable] attention, his is forceful attention: by force he holds the mind down and establishes it in one-pointedness; hence it is called forceful.

What is interrupted attention?

[Interrupted attention is] the characteristic-knowing attention of one who has gained attention and proceeds by the worldly or the supramundane path. For there concentration is interspersed with reflection (*cintā*); not exclusively by the cultivation-mode. The uninterrupted attention: from beyond the characteristic-knowing attention up to the practice-culminating attention. The effortless attention: the fruit-of-practice-culmination attention. [There are] four other attentions, namely: conforming (*ānulomika*), antidotal (*prātipakṣika*), gladdening (*prasadanīya*), and reviewing (*pratyavekṣaṇīya*). Conforming attention: that by which one finds fault with the object and undertakes right application — but does not yet abandon defilement.

The antidotal: that by which one abandons defilement. The gladdening: that by which one gladdens a slack mind with uplifting signs, cheers it, [and] uplifts it. The reviewing attention: namely, investigative attention, making which predominant, one reviews the abandoned and not-yet-abandoned state of [the] defilements.

Now, for one attending to the object, how many signs are to be attended to?

Answer: four, namely: the object-sign (*ālambana-nimitta*), [Lacuna] the sign to be avoided, and the sign to be resorted to. The object-sign: the reflection that resembles the knowable entity, [its] semblance.

The cause-sign: namely, the accumulation of the requisites of concentration, appropriate instruction; accompanied by cultivation, intense desire; urgency with respect to things that arouse urgency, the full knowledge of distraction and non-distraction, and attentiveness; and, from others, [Conjectural: no disturbance] — whether caused by humans, by non-humans, by sound, or [Conjectural: by activity]. Likewise, with *vipaśyanā* as forerunner, inward contraction of the mind is the cause-sign for a subsequently more intense *vipaśyanā*; likewise, *vipaśyanā* with *śamatha* as forerunner is the cause-sign for a subsequently more intense *śamatha*. The sign-to-be-avoided is fourfold, namely: sign of slackness (*laya*), sign of agitation (*auddhatya*), sign of attachment, [and] sign of distraction. The sign of slackness: that by which, through the object-sign and the cause-sign, the mind goes to slackness. The sign of agitation: that by which, through the object-sign and the cause-sign, the mind is excited. The sign of attachment: that by which, through the object-sign [or] through the cause-sign, the mind is enamored of the object,

is impassioned, is defiled. The sign of distraction: that by which, through the object-sign and the cause-sign, the mind is thrown outward and distracted. These signs, moreover, are as in the concentrated plane. When one resolves upon the object by these attentions,

how many resolves (*adhimokṣa*) are there?

Answer: nine resolves, namely: luminous and not-luminous, dull, sharp, limited, great, boundless, pure, and not-pure.

The luminous resolve: when the light-sign is well grasped, [the resolve] is accompanied by light. The not-luminous resolve: namely, when the light-sign is [Conjectural: not well grasped], [the resolve is] accompanied by darkness. The dull resolve: the one falling within the continuum of mild faculties. The sharp resolve: that falling within the continuum of sharp faculties. The limited resolve: [the one made] with limited faith, desire, concentration, and a limited object. Thus, from the limited-ness of the attention and of the object — [there is the] limited resolve.

The great resolve: the resolve that is accompanied by great faith and desire, or that resolves upon a great object — such a resolve is, from the greatness of attention and the greatness of the object, the great resolve. The boundless resolve: [the one] accompanied by boundless faith and desire, or that resolves upon an endless, boundless object. This resolve is, from the boundlessness of attention and of the object, the boundless resolve. The pure resolve: [the one] well cultivated, completed, arrived at its conclusion. The not-pure: [the one] not well cultivated, not completed, not arrived at its conclusion.

How many are the tasks (*karaṇīya*) of yoga?

Answer: four.

Which four?

Namely: cessation of the basis (*āśraya-nirodha*), transformation of the basis (*āśrayaparivarta*), full knowledge of the object, and delight in the object. Cessation of the basis: for one engaged in the cultivation of attention in practice, the basis attended by encumbrance is gradually brought to cessation, and the basis transforms into one accompanied by pliancy. This is the cessation of the basis, this is the transformation of the basis — [this is] a task of yoga. [As for] the full knowledge of the object and delight in the object: there is [a] full knowledge of the object and delight in the object led by cessation and transformation of the basis; [and] when making full knowledge of the object and delight in the object predominant, the basis ceases and is transformed. [Also,] there is [a] full knowledge of the object and delight in the object led by the purity of the basis: making the purity of the basis predominant, [a] perfectly pure knowledge of the object arises at the time of the completion of the task, and [a] delight [in the object likewise arises]. Hence one says: ‘four are the tasks of yoga.’

How many practitioners of yoga (*yogācāra*) are there?

Answer: three, namely: the beginner (*ādikarmika*), the experienced one (*kṛta-paricaya*), and one who has gone beyond attention (*atikrānta-manaskāra*).

The beginner practitioner of yoga [is twofold]: beginner with respect to attention, and beginner with respect to purification of defilements.

The beginner with respect to attention: the first-time practitioner, [applied] to one-pointedness, so long as he has not attained attention, [who] has not [yet] touched mental one-pointedness. The beginner with respect to purification of defilements: though he has attained attention, [he is one] who, wishing to liberate his mind from defilement, [is engaged in] the undertaking, the holding, [and] the practicing of the characteristic-knowing attention (*lakṣaṇa-pratisaṁvedī*). This is the beginner with respect to purification of defilements.

What is the experienced one?

Apart from the characteristic-knowing attention, when he has become practiced in the other six attentions, up to the practice-culminating attention, he is the experienced one. ‘One who has gone beyond attention’ is to be known with respect to the fruit-of-practice-culmination attention: he has gone beyond the attention of practice-cultivation, [and] is established in the fruit of cultivation. Therefore he is called ‘one who has gone beyond attention.’ Moreover, from [his taking up] desire for the wholesome dharma onward, while engaged and not yet producing the wholesome roots conducive to penetration (*nirvedha-bhāgīya*), for that long he is a beginner. When, however, he produces the penetration-conducive ones — namely the warmth (*ūṣmagata*), the summits (*mūrdhan*), truth-conforming patiences, [and] the supreme worldly dharmas — then he is the experienced one. When, however, he enters the fixed course of rightness (*samyaktva-nyāma*), and realizes the truths, he becomes one who does not depend on another, who is not led by another in the Teacher’s dispensation — then he is one who has gone beyond attention. Having passed beyond attention dependent on another, he stands in [attention] not dependent on another; therefore he is called ‘one who has gone beyond attention.’ There, what is the cultivation of yoga? Answer: twofold: cultivation of perception, and cultivation of the [dharmas] on the side of awakening.

There, what is cultivation of perception?

Namely: one engaged on the worldly path cultivates the perception of disadvantage in all the lower planes. Or, engaged for [Conjectural: nirvāṇa], in the realm of abandonment, in the realm of dispassion, in the realm of cessation, seeing [them] as calm, he cultivates the perception of abandonment, the perception of dispassion, the perception of cessation. Or, engaged for *śamatha*, he cultivates the perception of up-and-down on the *śamatha* side. Engaged in *vipaśyanā*, he cultivates the perception of [Conjectural: before-and-behind] on the *vipaśyanā* side. [Conjectural: Of these, the up-and-down perception:] he reviews this body, just as it is, just as it is set up, upward from the soles of the feet, downward from the hair of the head, full of various impurities. ‘In this body there are head-hairs, body-hairs…’ as before. [Conjectural: Of these, the before-and-behind perception:] as when, by someone, just the sign of the review is well and thoroughly grasped, well attended to, well drawn out, well penetrated. Namely: standing, he reviews someone seated; seated, one lying down; or, going behind, he reviews one going in front. This very thing illustrates *vipaśyanā*-mode review of the formations of the three times, dependently arisen. There, when it says ‘standing, he reviews someone seated,’ by this present attention, future knowable is reviewed. The present state of attention, having arisen, is called ‘standing.’ Future knowable state, from non-arising and from being on the verge of arising, is called ‘seated.’ When it further says, ‘seated, he reviews someone lying down,’ by this, by a present attention the review of the past knowable is illustrated. For the present state of attention, facing cessation, is called ‘seated.’ The past, from being ceased, knowable state, is called ‘lying down.’ When further it says, ‘one going in front he reviews,’ by this, by present attention, the review of immediately-preceding-ceased attention is illustrated. There, the attention which has arisen and immediately ceased: that ‘goes in front.’ There, the immediately-arisen attention, newly arising, grasping each immediately-ceased one in turn, ‘goes behind.’ There, cultivating *śamatha* and *vipaśyanā*, he cultivates the perception of light on both sides. This is the cultivation of perception.

There, what is the cultivation of the [dharmas] on the side of awakening?

The practice of the thirty-seven dharmas on the side of awakening, the familiarization, the resort, the making-much-of — this is called the cultivation of the [dharmas] on the side of awakening. Namely: the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of psychic power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven awakening-factors, and the noble eightfold path. Foundation of mindfulness in the body, in feelings, in mind, in dharmas. For unarisen evil unwholesome dharmas, that they may not arise, he generates desire, exerts himself, arouses energy. He uplifts the mind, settles it. For the right exertion. For the abandonment of arisen evil unwholesome dharmas. For the arising of unarisen wholesome dharmas. For the stability of arisen wholesome dharmas, for non-forgetting, for fullness of cultivation, for augmentation, growth, and expansion, he generates desire and exerts himself. He arouses energy. He uplifts the mind, settles it — this is right exertion. The bases of psychic power are those endowed with desire-concentration, energy-concentration, mind-concentration, and investigation-concentration, together with the formations of abandonment; then come the faculties of faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom; the powers of faith, [Lacuna] mindfulness, concentration, wisdom; and the awakening-factor of mindfulness. Right view. Right intention, right speech, action, livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.

Now, what is the body?

What is the contemplation of body in body (*kāye kāyānupaśyanā*)?

What is mindfulness (*smṛti*)?

What are the foundations of mindfulness?

Answer: the body is of thirty-five kinds — namely: internal and external; comprised by a sense-faculty, and not comprised by a sense-faculty; reckoned as a sentient being, and reckoned as not a sentient being; accompanied by encumbrance (*dauṣṭhulya*), and accompanied by pliancy (*praśrabdhi*); elemental body (*bhūta*) and derived body (*bhautika*); name-body (*nāma-kāya*) and form-body (*rūpa-kāya*); the body of a hell-being, of an animal, of a hungry ghost; of a human, of a deva; endowed with consciousness, and without consciousness; inner body and outer body; transformed and not transformed.

Female body, male body, eunuch body; friend’s body, foe’s body, indifferent body. Inferior body, middle body, superior body; child body, young body, old body. This much is the division of body. Contemplation (*anupaśyanā*), in turn, is threefold: with body as predominant, wisdom born of hearing, [Lacuna] or born of cultivation. By that wisdom one fully examines the whole body in every aspect rightly, ascertains, penetrates, [Conjectural: comprehends it]. Of these, mindfulness: with body as predominant,

of the dharmas grasped [by hearing], their meaning [reflected upon], and what is realized [by cultivation]: with respect to the letter, the meaning, and direct realization, that non-forgetfulness of mind which asks, ‘are these dharmas of mine well-grasped or not,’

‘well-marked or not by wisdom in each case,’ ‘well-touched by liberation in each case, or not’ — the establishing of mindfulness in these matters is the foundation of mindfulness. Moreover, the foundation of mindfulness serves for the guarding [done] by mindfulness, for non-defilement in regard to sense-objects, and for binding [it] to the object. Of these, the guarding by mindfulness is as said before: ‘there is established guarded mindfulness, mindfulness alert.’ Non-defilement in regard to sense-objects is, as said, ‘with mind guarded by mindfulness, proceeding in a state of evenness, he does not seize signs, does not seize secondary features,’ and so on at length, he guards the mind-faculty; with the mind-faculty he undertakes restraint. The binding to the object is, as said, the binding of mindfulness to the four objects (*ālambana*). Namely: pervasive object (*vyāpī*), conduct-purifying (*carita-viśodhana*), skill object (*kauśalya*), defilement-purifying (*kleśa-viśodhana*) — mindfulness well-established by these three modes is called the foundation of mindfulness.

Now, what is feeling (*vedanā*)?

Namely: pleasant, painful, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. The pleasant is bodily, and so too the painful and the neither-painful-nor-pleasant. As they are bodily, so they are mental. Pleasant with worldly attachments (*sāmiṣa*), and likewise the painful and the neither-painful-nor-pleasant. So too without worldly attachments (*nirāmiṣa*); so too dependent on greed (*gardhāśrita*) and dependent on renunciation (*naiṣkramyāśrita*) — whether the feeling is pleasant, painful, or neither-painful-nor-pleasant. Thus feeling is of twenty-one kinds, or of nine kinds.

Now, what is mind (*citta*)?

Namely: mind with lust, [Lacuna] with hatred, without hatred; with delusion, without delusion; contracted, distracted; slack, uplifted; agitated, not-agitated; calmed, not-calmed; concentrated, not-concentrated; well-cultivated, not-well-cultivated; well-released, not-well-released. Thus, in summary, the mind is of twenty kinds.

Now, what are dharmas?

Lust and the subduing of lust; hatred and the subduing of hatred; delusion and the subduing of delusion; contraction and distraction; slackness and uplift; agitation and non-agitation; calming and non-calming; well-concentrated and not-well-concentrated; well-cultivated path and not-well-cultivated path; being [Conjectural: well-released] and not-well-released. These twenty dharmas are to be understood as established on the dark and bright sides, on the side of defilement and on the side of purification. Of these, pleasant feeling is that which arises in dependence upon a contact to-be-felt-as-pleasant — the agreeable, the felt, what belongs to feeling. That which is associated with the five [sense-]consciousnesses is bodily. That which is associated with mind-consciousness is mental. As with ‘to-be-felt-as-pleasant,’ so too with ‘to-be-felt-as-painful’ and ‘to-be-felt-as-neither-painful-nor-pleasant’ — that which arises in dependence on contact: the disagreeable, the neither-agreeable-nor-disagreeable, the felt, what belongs to feeling — this is called painful or neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. That which is associated with the five [sense-]consciousnesses is bodily. That which is associated with mind-consciousness is mental. That which is favorable to nirvāṇa, penetrative, which, to ultimate completion, to ultimate purity, to the ultimate consummation of the holy life, leads — that is without worldly attachments.

That which has fallen into the realms, fallen into becoming — that is with worldly attachments. That which is connected with form and the formless [realms], or favorable to dispassion — that is dependent on renunciation. That which is connected with [Conjectural: the desire realm] and not favorable to dispassion — that is dependent on greed.

Mind with lust is the mind obsessed by lust with respect to a lust-arousing thing. Mind without lust is free from lust-obsession. Mind with hatred is the mind obsessed by hatred with respect to a hatred-arousing thing. Mind without hatred is free from hatred-obsession. Mind with delusion is what, in regard to a delusion-arousing thing, [Lacuna] — these six minds are to be understood as belonging to activity. Three are on the side of defilement. Three are antidotal to defilement. A contracted mind is one bound inwardly, by oneself, by way of *śamatha*. A distracted mind is one scattered outwardly upon the five strands of sense-pleasure. A slack mind is the one accompanied by sloth-and-torpor; an uplifted mind is one well-established on a gladdening object. An agitated mind is one obsessed by agitation through excessive uplifting; a [Conjectural: not-agitated] mind is one which, when in the time of uplifting or the time of contraction, attains equanimity. A calmed mind is one liberated from the hindrances; not-calmed, one not yet liberated. A concentrated mind is one liberated from the hindrances and entered into the basic dhyāna; not-well-concentrated, one not yet entered. A well-cultivated mind is that of one who, by long practice of this very concentration, attains [it] at will, attains [it] without difficulty, attains [it] quickly. A not-well-cultivated mind is to be understood by the opposite. A well-released mind is one completely and entirely released. A not-well-released mind is one not completely or entirely released — thus these fourteen [further] minds are to be understood as belonging to dwelling. Beginning from the plane of purification from hindrances, eight minds are to be known as belonging to dwelling: distracted, contracted, on up to calmed, not-calmed. Beginning from purification of defilement, six minds belonging to dwelling: on up to well-released mind, not-well-released. When, with hindrance present inwardly, he knows ‘I have a hindrance’; when not present, he knows ‘I have no hindrance’; and how the arising of an unarisen hindrance occurs, that too; how, having arisen, it departs, that too he knows. When an eye-fetter is present, on up to a mind-fetter, ‘I have an eye-fetter,’ on up to, ‘I have a mind-fetter’; when none is present, ‘I have no eye-fetter,’ on up to, ‘I have no mind-fetter’ — thus he knows. And how the arising of an unarisen mind-fetter occurs, that too he knows. And how the cessation of an arisen one occurs, that too he knows. When the mindfulness-awakening-factor (*smṛtisaṁbodhyaṁga*) is inwardly present, he knows, ‘I have the mindfulness-awakening-factor.’ When absent, he knows, ‘I have it not.’ And how the arising of an unarisen mindfulness-awakening-factor occurs, that too he knows. And how the stability of an arisen one occurs — its non-forgetting, its fullness of cultivation, its augmentation, growth, and expansion — that too he knows. As with the mindfulness-awakening-factor when present inwardly, so likewise the [remaining six] awakening-factors — [Conjectural: dharma-discrimination], energy (*vīrya*), [Lacuna] pliancy (*praśrabdhi*), concentration (*samādhi*), and equanimity (*upekṣā*) — are to be understood. The full knowledge of defiled dharmas by way of own-nature, disadvantage, and antidote — this is the body of the foundation of mindfulness in dharmas. As it is for the body — the contemplation of body in body, together with the mindfulness-and-foundation side — so for feelings, up to mind, and dharmas, it is to be understood as appropriate.

How, then, does he dwell contemplating the body in the body internally?

How externally? How internally-and-externally?

When inwardly, in himself, he sees the body in the body reckoned as a sentient being — thus inwardly he dwells contemplating body in body.

How externally? How internally-and-externally?

When externally he takes as object form reckoned as non-sentient — thus he dwells contemplating body in body externally. When externally he takes as object [Conjectural: another’s] form reckoned as sentient — thus he dwells contemplating body in body both internally-and-externally. Now, when inwardly he takes up subtle form reckoned as a sentient being, and takes as object the feelings, mind, and dharmas that have arisen [in dependence on it], internally — in feelings, in mind, in dharmas — he dwells contemplating dharmas in dharmas. When he takes up external form reckoned as non-sentient and takes as object the feelings, mind, and dharmas arising thereon, externally — in feelings, mind, dharmas — he dwells contemplating dharmas in dharmas. When externally he takes up outer sentient form and takes as object the feelings, mind, dharmas arising thereon, he dwells contemplating dharmas in dharmas both internally-and-externally.

Another mode: when he takes form comprised by a sense-faculty as object, internally he dwells contemplating body in body. When, not comprised by a sense-faculty, what belongs to form and is unappropriated is taken as object — externally he dwells contemplating body in body. When, not comprised by a sense-faculty, form, internal and appropriated, is taken as object — internally-and-externally he dwells contemplating body in body. Thus, taking up the threefold form just described as [the basis for] feelings, mind, and dharmas that have arisen — and taking them as object, he dwells contemplating accordingly. So one should understand. Another mode: when he takes as object form belonging to the concentrated plane and accompanied by pliancy — thus inwardly he dwells contemplating body in body. When he takes as object subtle form, inwardly, of the [Conjectural: unconcentrated] plane, accompanied by encumbrance — thus he dwells externally contemplating body in body. When he takes as object another’s form, accompanied by encumbrance or by pliancy — internally-and-externally he dwells contemplating body in body. So too the feelings, mind, dharmas arising in dependence on it are to be understood as appropriate. Another mode: when he takes inward elemental form as object, inwardly he dwells contemplating body in body. When outer elemental form is taken as object — externally he dwells contemplating body in body. And the derived form that has arisen in dependence on that elemental form, comprised in faculties and sense-objects — taking [that] form as object, internally-and-externally he dwells contemplating body in body. So too with the feelings, mind, dharmas arising in dependence — these are to be understood as appropriate. Another mode: when inwardly he takes the body endowed with consciousness as object, thus inwardly he dwells contemplating body in body. When consciousness-less form reckoned as a sentient being is taken as object — in states such as the blue-livid corpse and so on — externally he dwells contemplating body in body. And when he takes as object the past conscious state of form without consciousness, and the future unconscious state of form [Conjectural: endowed with consciousness], their like nature and sameness of nature — internally-and-externally he dwells contemplating body in body. So too the feelings, mind, dharmas arising in dependence are to be understood appropriately. Another mode: when one takes one’s own inner body — by way of head-hair, body-hair, nails, and so on — as object, [Conjectural: internally] one dwells contemplating body in body.

When one takes others’ inner body — by way of head-hair, body-hair, nails, and so on — as object, externally one dwells contemplating body in body; and when, [Conjectural: inwardly, the outer body, changed], by way of aspects such as blue-livid, and, externally, the outer body, changed and unchanged, are taken as object by way of aspects such as blue-livid, on the basis of like-naturedness — [Conjectural: internally-and-externally] one dwells contemplating body in body. The feelings, mind, and dharmas arising in dependence on that are likewise to be understood as appropriate. Thus modes of this sort, by way of the distinction into body, feelings, mind, and dharmas, are to be understood as many. Here only a few modes have been elucidated. Now, as antidote to the four perversions (*viparyāsa*), the Blessed One established the four foundations of mindfulness. As antidote to the perversion ‘[seeing] the pure in the unclean,’ the foundation of mindfulness on the body was established. Thus by the Blessed One, in the cultivation of the body-foundation-of-mindfulness, four charnel-ground reflections (*śivapathikā*) connected with the unattractive are taught, which, frequently engaged with and attended to, lead to the abandonment of the perversion ‘the pure in the unclean.’ As antidote to the perversion ‘[seeing] the pleasant in [Conjectural: the painful],’ the foundation of mindfulness on feeling is established. Dwelling contemplating feeling, he knows, as it actually is, ‘whatever is felt, that counts as suffering’; and thus, for him, the perversion ‘pleasant in the painful’ is abandoned. As antidote to the ‘permanent in the impermanent’ perversion, the foundation of mindfulness [on mind] is established. For him, perceiving, through the passing of those nights and days, moments, instants, and hours, the mind’s manifold variety of many kinds — by way of the distinction into minds with lust and so on — the ‘permanent in the impermanent’ perversion is abandoned. And as antidote to the ‘self in the not-self’ perversion, the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas is established. For him, on account of the existence of defilements such as self-view and so on, and on account of the non-existence of wholesome dharmas such as the view of non-self, there is the seeing of self in the aggregates and not for another. By specific characteristic and general characteristic, for one who dwells contemplating [Conjectural: dharmas in dharmas], seeing as it actually is, the perversion ‘self in non-self’ is abandoned. Another mode: for in the world this is how things commonly run. In the aggregates, not knowing the mere aggregates, the mere dharmas, as they actually are, [imagines a self] as resting on the body; and the one resting there [Conjectural: experiences] pleasure and pain and is defiled and purified by dharma and non-dharma — for the removal of confusion about the [supposed] self’s basis-of-resort, the foundation of mindfulness on the body is established. For the removal of confusion about that same self’s basis-of-experiencing, the foundation of mindfulness on feeling is established. And where they are confused by self-grasping in mind, mentality, and consciousness — for the removal of confusion about the self-entity, [Lacuna] the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas is established. Another mode: where one performs action, and for what purpose, and who performs the action, and by what one performs it — all of that, summed up into one, the four foundations of mindfulness are established. Namely: in the body one acts, for the sake of feeling, with the mind, by means of wholesome and unwholesome dharmas. Another mode: where one is defiled, where purified, from what, who, and by what one is defiled and purified — all this, gathered into one, the four foundations of mindfulness are established. Namely: in the body one is defiled and purified. Through the feelings the mind is defiled by dharmas, and purified. Now, what is the meaning of ‘foundation of mindfulness’? Answer: where one establishes mindfulness, and by what one establishes it, that is called the foundation of mindfulness. Where one establishes mindfulness — that is the object-foundation-of-mindfulness (*ālambana-smṛtyupasthāna*); by what one establishes mindfulness, that — the wisdom and mindfulness that uphold concentration — is the own-nature foundation of mindfulness (*svabhāva-smṛtyupasthāna*). And other mental and mental-factor dharmas associated with them are the conjunction-foundation-of-mindfulness (*saṁsarga-smṛtyupasthāna*). Moreover, the wholesome path arisen with body and feeling [Lacuna] as predominant, with flux or flux-less — that is the foundation of mindfulness. Further, it is born of hearing, born of reflection, or born of cultivation. Of these, the hearing-born and reflection-born are only with-flux. The cultivation-born may be either with flux or fluxless.

Thus, gaining experience in the four foundations of mindfulness, taking away the gross perversion, knowing both wholesome and unwholesome dharmas, immediately afterwards [he undertakes the four right exertions]: for the non-arising of unarisen evil unwholesome dharmas, for the abandonment of arisen ones, for the arising of unarisen wholesome dharmas, for the stability of arisen ones — in detail as before, on up to ‘he uplifts the mind, settles it.’

Now, what are evil unwholesome dharmas?

Whatever, in the realm of desire, is the defiled bodily action, verbal action, or mental action — comprised in the wrong-conduct of body, speech, and mind. And the defilements which engender these: those not yet assembled, not yet manifest, are the unarisen; those that have assembled and are manifest are the arisen. The wholesome dharmas — those that are antidotal to them, antidotal to misconduct, antidotal to hindrances, or antidotal to fetters — these too, unarisen and arisen, are to be understood in the same way as the evil unwholesome dharmas. When, for the non-arising of unarisen evil unwholesome dharmas, he generates eagerness, he sets his mind [on it]: ‘I will not, completely, entirely, in any way, let them arise.’ Thus he generates desire. Or of those that have arisen, assembled: ‘I will not tolerate them at all, I shall abandon them, shall dispel them.’ And when evil unwholesome [dharmas formerly] unarisen [Conjectural: have already arisen], he generates eagerness, sets his mind. He becomes one who wishes not to tolerate [them]. This is the desire for the abandonment of arisen ones. These evil unwholesome dharmas arise taking past, future, or present objects. They have [Conjectural: remote] objects or perceptible objects: those with past or future states as objects have [Conjectural: remote] objects; those with present objects have perceptible objects. Now, for the non-arising of unarisen evil unwholesome dharmas with remote objects, and for the abandonment of arisen ones, he exerts himself. And as for those with perceptible objects — for the non-arising of unarisen and the abandonment of arisen, he arouses energy. For only by firm rousing of energy is there non-arising of these, or abandonment. Moreover, for the non-arising of unarisen mild and middling [Conjectural: obsessions], and for the abandonment of arisen ones, he exerts himself. For the non-arising of unarisen strong [Conjectural: obsessions], and for the abandonment of arisen ones, he arouses energy. If he moves in a past object, he moves in such a way that no defilement arises in him from that object. If through forgetfulness one does arise, he does not tolerate it, he abandons it, puts an end to it. As with past objects, so future is to be understood. Thus it is said: for the non-arising of unarisen evil unwholesome dharmas and for the abandonment of arisen ones, he exerts himself. If he moves in a present object, he moves in such a way that no defilement arises through that object. If through forgetfulness it arises, the arisen he does not tolerate, he abandons, dispels, puts an end to it. Thus it is said: for the non-arising of unarisen evil unwholesome dharmas, and for the abandonment of arisen ones, he arouses energy. Some evil unwholesome dharmas arise through the power of thought, not through the power of the object; some arise through both — through the power of thought and the power of the object. Of these, the ones that arise through the power of thought are, as when dwelling, those based on past and future objects. And the ones that arise through the power of [Conjectural: thought] and the power of the object are, as when moving, those that arise through a present object. In that case too there is necessarily improper thought (*ayoniśaḥ saṁkalpa*). Now, for the non-arising of unarisen ones that arise through the power of thought, and for the abandonment of arisen ones, he exerts himself. For those that arise through the power of the object and the power of thought — for the non-arising of unarisen ones and the abandonment of arisen ones, he arouses energy. Now, ‘for the arising of unarisen wholesome dharmas, he generates desire’ means: for those wholesome dharmas not yet acquired, not yet manifest, he generates [Conjectural: eagerness] for their acquisition and manifestation. He sets his mind [on them]. Intense desire to obtain [them], intense desire to bring [them] to manifestation, is present in him. This is [what is meant by ‘desire’] for the arising of unarisen wholesome dharmas. ‘For the stability, non-forgetting, fullness of cultivation of arisen wholesome dharmas, he generates desire’ means: those wholesome dharmas which, having arisen, are acquired and manifest — with respect to non-loss of what has been acquired, with respect to non-falling-away from what has been acquired, [the text] says ‘for the stability’; with respect to its being clearly manifest, without dullness, [the text] says ‘for the non-forgetting’; with respect to the bringing of those very acquired and manifest wholesome dharmas to fullness, to completion by resort, [the text] says ‘for the fullness of cultivation.’ He generates eagerness, sets his mind. And intense desire-for-stability, desire-for-non-forgetting, and desire-for-fullness-of-cultivation are present. This is called the desire for stability, non-forgetting, and fullness of cultivation of arisen wholesome dharmas. Now, ‘he exerts himself’ means: for making present what has been acquired. ‘He arouses energy’: for acquiring what has not been acquired. Again, he exerts himself for the stability and non-forgetting of arisen ones; he arouses energy for the fullness of cultivation. Moreover, for the arising of unarisen mild and middling wholesome dharmas, and for the stability of arisen ones, for non-forgetting, he exerts himself. For the arising of unarisen strong wholesome dharmas, and for the arisen, on up to fullness of cultivation, he arouses energy. He uplifts the mind: when that mind, engaged in *śamatha*-cultivation and one-pointedness for the non-arising of unarisen evil unwholesome dharmas — in detail as before, on up to — for the arising of unarisen wholesome dharmas, and, for the arisen, on up to the fullness of cultivation, arouses energy, and so, inwardly contracted, tends to slackness, or he suspects slackness, and sees it so — then he takes hold of it by one or another uplifting sign that gladdens. He gladdens it: thus he uplifts the mind.

How does he settle it?

When again, at the time of uplifting, he sees the mind agitated, or suspects agitation, he again contracts it inwardly. He sets [the mind] toward *śamatha*. These four are the four right exertions (*samyakprahāṇa*). For the non-arising of unarisen dark-side dharmas, and for the abandonment of arisen ones — desire, striving, arousal of energy, uplifting the mind, settling it: these two right exertions are [for the dark side]. For the arising of unarisen bright-side dharmas, in detail; the [other] two right exertions are to be understood. Namely: of the dark side, the first is restraint-abandonment (*samvaraṇa-prahāṇa*), which is ‘for the abandonment of arisen evil unwholesome dharmas, he generates desire,’ in detail. The second is abandonment-of-abandonment (*prahāṇa-prahāṇa*): ‘for the non-arising of unarisen ones, he generates desire,’ in detail. For the arisen is to be restrained — the evil thing — and the unarisen, since it is abandoned by its not occurring, is thus to be abandoned by its not coming to manifest; on that account, the abandonment of what is abandoned is abandonment-of-abandonment. Then there is one cultivation-abandonment (*bhāvanā-prahāṇa*), as it says: ‘For the arising of unarisen wholesome dharmas,’ in detail on up to ‘he uplifts the mind, settles it.’ For wholesome dharmas, resorted to and cultivated, both come to be acquired when not previously acquired, and are made manifest when acquired. And there is one protection-abandonment (*anurakṣaṇā-prahāṇa*), as it is said: ‘for the stability of arisen wholesome dharmas,’ in detail on up to ‘he uplifts the mind, settles it.’ For wholesome dharmas once acquired and manifest, the avoidance of negligence and resort to heedfulness is for their stability, non-forgetting, and fullness of cultivation. Thus the [Conjectural: arisen] wholesome dharmas are protected — this is the detailed division of right exertions.

What, then, is their summary meaning?

Answer: for the relinquishing of the dark-side object and for the acquisition of the bright-side object, eagerness must be present beforehand. And for the abandonment of obsession (*paryavasthāna*) [it must be present]. there is excellence of intention and excellence of application — [this] is elucidated. Excellence of intention [is achieved] by generation of desire; excellence of application, by striving, arousing energy, uplifting the mind, and [Conjectural: settling it]. This much is the yogin’s task: for the abandonment of what is to be abandoned and for the attainment of what is to be attained, eagerness must arise beforehand; for abandoning obsession, and for abandoning latent tendencies (*anuśaya*), he must arouse energy; from time to time he should cultivate the signs of *śamatha*, uplifting, and equanimity. And for the abandonment of obsession and of the latent tendency, the wholesome dharmas antidotal to them are to be accumulated. All this is elucidated by the four right exertions — this is the summary meaning.

Now, the four concentrations [of the bases of psychic power], namely: concentration of desire (*chanda-samādhi*), concentration of energy (*vīrya-samādhi*), concentration of mind (*citta-samādhi*), and concentration of investigation (*mīmāṁsā-samādhi*). The concentration acquired by making desire predominant is the concentration-of-desire. The concentration acquired by making energy, mind, or investigation predominant is [Lacuna] the concentration of investigation. When he generates desire alone, and, with desire arisen, with respect to those evil unwholesome dharmas in their own-nature, cause, disadvantage, and antidote, rightly contemplates them, he establishes one-pointed mindfulness. Likewise, with respect to the wholesome dharmas — in their own-nature, cause, [Conjectural: benefit], and way-out — he rightly contemplates. He establishes one-pointed mindfulness. He touches one-pointedness that abounds in that mode, by the method of distancing the manifestation of [Conjectural: obsession], but he does not [Conjectural: eradicate] even the latent tendency (*anuśaya*) of evil unwholesome dharmas. This too is called the concentration with desire as predominant. When, in a past, future, or present object — serving as a basis for evil unwholesome dharmas and for mild, middling, or strong defilement-[Conjectural: obsession] — for the non-arising of unarisen or the abandonment of arisen [dharmas], he exerts himself and arouses energy, moving in that object, and with respect to that object, by way of own-nature, cause, and antidote, rightly contemplating, establishing one-pointed mindfulness — then by the frequent dwelling-mode the one-pointedness of mind that arises by the method of distancing [Conjectural: obsession], but [Conjectural: does not] eradicate the latent tendency either of evil unwholesome dharmas. This is the energy-predominant concentration. Again, for one uplifting a slack mind, concentrating the uplifted mind, and from time to time regarding it with equanimity, [for one] rightly contemplating the evil unwholesome dharmas [Conjectural: and] the dharmas that serve as bases for evil unwholesome [dharmas], and the wholesome dharmas and the dharmas that serve as bases for wholesome and unwholesome [dharmas], in own-nature, cause, disadvantage, benefit, antidote, and way-out; establishing one-pointed mindfulness by frequent dwelling, the one-pointedness of mind that arises, in detail on up to: this is the mind-predominant concentration. Now, the dharmas that serve as bases for evil unwholesome dharmas for one of unwise attention become the very same dharmas that serve as bases for wholesome [dharmas] for one of wise attention. When, in him, the [Conjectural: obsessions] have been distanced, and when, as the antidote to [Conjectural: obsession], the dharmas headed by concentration have [Conjectural: arisen], those evil unwholesome dharmas [Conjectural: do not occur]. He thinks thus:

‘Are there evil unwholesome dharmas present that I do not perceive, or are there none?

If non-existent — let me investigate.’ Making investigative attention (*mīmāṁsā-manaskāra*) predominant, he investigates rightly whether they are abandoned or not. And by frequent dwelling there he touches one-pointedness of mind, by which he becomes free of overestimation. ‘From mere obsession my mind is liberated, but not completely from all latent tendencies; and the concentration-headed wholesome dharmas antidotal to those obsessions have been acquired and cultivated, but [the dharmas] antidotal to the latent tendencies have not.’ Thus he knows as it actually is. This is called the investigation-concentration. Making the fourfold concentration predominant, when the obsessions are distanced, for the complete eradication of the latent tendencies of evil unwholesome dharmas, and for the accomplishment of the wholesome dharmas antidotal to them, he generates desire; he exerts himself, and so on — in detail he applies the four right exertions. And thus for him so applied, eight formations of abandonment (*prahāṇa-saṁskāra*) arise, which serve for his eradication of latent tendency and for the fullness of concentration. Namely: desire — ‘when shall I fulfill concentration and abandon the latent tendencies of evil unwholesome dharmas?’ Exertion: on up to his application not being laid down in cultivation of the antidote. Faith: for one who dwells with application not laid down, trust in further attainment, conviction. Pliancy: the gladness preceded by faith and heedfulness, the joy, and, for one whose mind is joyful, the gradual tranquillization of the encumbrance on the side of evil unwholesome dharmas. Mindfulness: of nine modes, the upholder of nine-mode mental abiding (*navākārā cittasthiti*) on the *śamatha* side. Full awareness (*saṁprajanya*): the wisdom that is on the *vipaśyanā* side. Volition: the mental conditioning (*cittābhisaṁskāra*) — for him investigating what is abandoned and what is not, the mental conditioning, favorable to *śamatha-vipaśyanā*, sets up bodily and verbal action. Equanimity: for one moving among past, future, and present dharmas that serve as bases for evil unwholesome [dharmas], the mind’s [Conjectural: non-defilement and evenness]. By these two causes he ascertains the abandonment of latent tendencies and knows it — namely, by volition with its object out of sight and by equanimity with its object [Conjectural: in sight]. These eight are the formations of abandonment. And this eightfold yoga of formations of abandonment is for the eradication of latent tendencies. Of these, the desire is [that] desire; what is striving, this is [that] energy; the faith is [that] faith; and pliancy, mindfulness, full awareness, volition, and equanimity — [Conjectural: this is the means]. Summing all this up: the previously stated concentrations of desire [etc.] and these formations of abandonment — when the latent tendencies are abandoned and concentration is complete — are all called ‘the base of psychic power (*ṛddhi-pāda*) endowed with desire-concentration and formations of abandonment’; and ‘the base of psychic power endowed with energy-, mind-, or investigation-concentration and formations of abandonment’.

For what reason is it called ‘base of psychic power’?

Answer: namely, one whose foot is present is able to step forward and back, to stride. Just so, one for whom these dharmas are present, and for whom this concentration is present, complete — he, with mind thus purified, cleansed, unblemished, free of secondary defilement, made straight, made workable, established, having attained imperturbability, is able to step forward and back for the attainment and touching of supramundane dharmas. For this is the supreme psychic-power, the supreme prosperity — namely, the supramundane dharmas. Therefore they are called bases of psychic power.

Thus established in concentration and depending on concentration, he exerts himself in the training in higher mind and in the training in higher wisdom. While he is so exerting himself, with respect to others’ attainment — that of the Teacher and the disciples — the trust, serenity, and faith (*śraddhā*), in the sense of [Conjectural: predominance], is called the faculty of faith (*śraddhendriya*).

And in what does its predominance consist?

Answer: in predominance for the arising of energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom that lead to the arising of supramundane dharma. Those too, energy and so on, have predominance for the arising of supramundane dharma, and predominance for the attainment [of the next], up to wisdom, [which has] for the arising of supramundane dharma predominance. These are the five faculties (*paṁcendriya*) beginning with faith. And when, recognizing the attainment of distinction of the later over the earlier, he accordingly has trust, serenity, and faith toward the attainment of the still higher supramundane dharma — that, in the sense of being uncrushable, is called the power of faith (*śraddhā-bala*). By what cannot it

be crushed?

That faith cannot be taken away (*asaṁhāryā*) — by a deva, a māra, a brahma, or by anyone else in the world legitimately, nor by an obsession of defilement; therefore it is called uncrushable. Headed and led by it, those — energy and so on — are also called powers. Endowed with those powers, the mighty one overcomes all the māra’s armies and applies himself to the destruction of the fluxes. Therefore they are called powers. Now, the faculty of faith and the power of faith are to be seen in the four serenities of clear knowing (*avetya-prasāda*).

Why so?

Because the serenity of clear knowing of one who has entered the fixed course of rightness (*samyaktvanyāma*) has that [faith-faculty/power] as its cause, condition, and source. Therefore, by cause-effect connection, that [serenity] is its predominant-effect — so it is said. ‘There it is to be seen,’ said the Blessed One — but not that it is its very body or its very characteristic. The faculty of energy is to be seen in the four right exertions.

Which right exertions?

The right exertions which are preparatory for the abandonment of the defilements abandonable by seeing (*darśana-prahātavya-kleśa*) — those right exertions are here intended; for they lead to the abandonment, even unto its absolute end, of evil unwholesome dharmas.

The faculty of mindfulness is to be seen in the four foundations of mindfulness — these four foundations of mindfulness lead to the abandonment of all perversions without distinction. The faculty of concentration is to be seen in the four dhyānas, the dhyānas which are preparatory for [Conjectural: non-returning]; and the faculty of wisdom is to be seen in the four noble truths — in the truth-knowledge which leads to the realization of the four noble truths. The five faculties are for the attainment of the fruit of asceticism (*śrāmaṇya-phala*). Likewise the [five] powers should be understood. From his resort to, cultivation of, and making-much-of those faculties and powers, he produces the wholesome roots conducive to penetration (*nirvedhabhāgīya*). [They are of three grades:] mild, middling, and strong. Namely: warmth (*ūṣmagata*), summits (*mūrdhan*), patiences in conformity with truth, and supreme worldly dharmas. Now, just as some person wishing to make a fire-mass with fire, needing fire, places the upper stick on the lower fire-stick, makes effort, exerts himself, strives — and thus, making effort, exerting, striving, first warmth is born in the lower stick; that same warmth, increasing, rises upward; increasing further in greater measure, it brings down a flameless fire; and immediately upon the falling of the fire, a flame is born. As by the flame arisen, born, come to be, he makes the fire-mass — so by the effort of the rubbing-stick, the resort to the five faculties is to be seen. As in the lower stick first there is warmth, so the warmths [are to be seen]. They are forerunners, becoming signs for the arising of the fire-like fluxless dharmas that burn the defilements. As that warmth rises upward, so the summits are to be seen. As the appearance of smoke, so the truth-conformity patiences are to be seen.

As the falling of a flameless fire, so the supreme worldly dharmas are to be seen. As immediately after, the flame’s arising — so the supramundane, fluxless dharmas are to be seen, which arise immediately after the five faculties subsumed under supreme worldly dharma. Which are they? Answer: the seven awakening-factors (*saptabodhyaṁga*). That awakening as it actually is of the person who has entered the fixed course of rightness (*samyaktvanyāma*) — these are its factors. For that awakening as it actually is is taken up by seven factors: three on the *śamatha* side, three on the *vipaśyanā* side, and one belonging to both. Therefore they are called awakening-factors. [Conjectural: Dharma-discrimination], energy (*vīrya*), and joy (*prīti*) — these three are on the *vipaśyanā* side. Pliancy (*praśrabdhi*), concentration (*samādhi*), and equanimity (*upekṣā*) — these three are on the *śamatha* side. Mindfulness (*smṛti*), belonging to both, is called all-pervading. At that moment, by the first attainment of the awakening-factors, he becomes a trainee (*śaikṣa*), one progressing [on the path]. His defilements to be abandoned by seeing (*darśana-prahātavya*) are abandoned. Those abandonable by cultivation (*bhāvanā-prahātavya*) remain. For their abandonment he cultivates the three-aggregate noble eightfold path. Of these, right view, right intention, and right effort make up the wisdom-aggregate (*prajñāskandha*); [Lacuna] right action and right livelihood make up the virtue-aggregate (*śīla-skandha*). His right mindfulness and right concentration make up the concentration-aggregate (*samādhi-skandha*).

For what reason is it called the noble eightfold path?

Answer: for the noble trainee who has seen the track, this is the path, this is the practice, comprised in eight factors.

Without omission, for the abandonment of all defilements and for the realization of liberation — therefore it is called the noble eightfold path. At the time of [attaining] the awakening-factors, realization of reality

is acquired; and having acquired it, by that very wisdom one makes a delineation

of that realization just as it has been attained — these two, summed up, are called right view. Making that right view predominant, what he wills as the intention of renunciation, the intention of non-malevolence, the intention of non-cruelty — this is called right intention. If his mind moves in applied thoughts (*vitarka*), he thinks such applied thoughts [accordingly]. If his mind moves to speech, making right view predominant, he speaks the dharma-talk born of that wholesome intention. That becomes for him right speech. If he needs robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, [or other] requisites, he undertakes their seeking. in going forward and returning, he dwells with full awareness; in looking ahead and looking around, in bending and stretching, in wearing the saṅghāṭi and robes, and carrying the bowl; in eating, drinking, chewing, tasting; or, back in the dwelling, with respect to the robes etc. already sought — in walking, standing, sitting, up to the dispelling of sleep-fatigue — he dwells with full awareness. This for him is called right action. He seeks robes [and] up to medicine and requisites by means of the dharma. up to ‘free of the dharma of wrong [Damaged]’ — that for him is right livelihood. Those subsumed under the abstinences (*virati*), [Lacuna] right action and right livelihood — these have been obtained along with the awakening-factors by virtue of the prior attainment of attention. And [they are] called ‘virtues [Conjectural: dear to the noble ones]’. For what reason? For a long time those virtues had been esteemed, dear, beloved, and agreeable to the noble, the good, the rightly-gone: ‘would that I might attain the restraint of non-doing of misconduct of speech, body, and wrong livelihood — the restraint.’ What had long been for him desired, dear, beloved, agreeable — that is now attained by him at that moment. Therefore they are called ‘[Conjectural: dear to the noble ones]’. For when he has attained the virtues [Conjectural: dear to the noble ones], he does not knowingly speak false speech, does not deliberately deprive a living being of life, does not take what is not given, does not engage in wrong conduct in sense-pleasures, and does not seek robes and so on by non-dharma. Thus, making those virtues [Conjectural: dear to the noble ones] predominant while cultivating the path, the bodily action and the livelihood — these too are called right speech, action, and livelihood. For him who, with right view and right intention, speech, action, and livelihood as his support, is applied to cultivation, whatever desire, energy, effort, going forth, advance, stamina, undertaking, uplifting of the mind, and continuity — this is called right effort. Making the four foundations of mindfulness predominant, the mindfulness subsumed under non-perversion, of nine modes, the upholder of the nine-mode mental abiding — this is called right mindfulness and right concentration. Summing all this up, the noble eightfold path stands in the task of conduct and the task of dwelling. Of these, right speech, right action, and right livelihood are in the task of conduct; the task of dwelling is again twofold: *śamatha* and *vipaśyanā*. Right view, right intention, and right effort are *vipaśyanā*. Right mindfulness and right concentration are *śamatha*. Thus, depending on the purified right speech, action, and livelihood, he cultivates *śamatha* and *vipaśyanā* from time to time, realizes the abandonment of the fetters without remainder, and attains the supreme fruit, arhatship. And the cultivation-path (*bhāvanā-mārga*) is protracted: it abandons defilements by practice over a long time. The path of seeing (*darśana-mārga*), a thing bound up with mere knowledge, abandons defilements by the mere arising of knowledge. For this reason speech, action, and livelihood are established in the cultivation-path. Thus, this sequence of practice — familiarization with the thirty-seven dharmas on the side of awakening — is called the cultivation of the [dharmas] on the side of awakening.

Now, what is the fruit of cultivation?

Answer: the four fruits of asceticism — the fruit of stream-entry, the fruit of once-returning, the fruit of non-returning, and the supreme fruit, arhatship.

What is asceticism (*śrāmaṇya*)?

What is its fruit?

Answer: asceticism is the path; the abandonment of defilements is the fruit. Moreover, the path arisen later is the fruit of the path arisen earlier — middling or superior.

For what reason are four [fruits] established?

Answer: as antidotes for the abandonment of four kinds of defilement. Namely: from the abandonment of the baseless defilements (*nirvastuka*) that cause going to the lower realms, and from the arising of antidote, the fruit of stream-entry is established — But [that] it is established from the abandonment of the three fetters [is because] the Blessed One [said it] in respect of three positions — the householder’s, that of the ill-stated dharma-vinaya, [Lacuna] — since the three fetters are [Conjectural: obstructive] to the arising of the path. In the householder, [the fetter is] identity-view (*satkāya-dṛṣṭi*), by which from the very start he does not engage in the path — terror-bringing identity-view. In the ill-stated dharma-vinaya, [the fetter is] clinging-to-virtue-and-vow (*śīla-vrata-parāmarśa*). Even one who has set out, it makes him practice wrongly, by which the noble path does not arise. In the well-stated dharma-vinaya, [the obstacle is] from doubt (*vicikitsā*); he has set out and is not practising wrongly, but, even with his own training, as long as he has no seeing as it actually is of the knowable entity, so long doubts and indecisions are obstructive to the arising of the path. Thus the delineation of the fruit of stream-entry.

For this stream-enterer, at most seven existences remain. And this is the continuity of his birth. When he abandons the defilements that bind to birth — those subsumed under deva-existence and human-existence — by the abandonment of which he produces only one further deva-existence and one human-existence, at that moment the fruit of once-returning (*sakṛd-āgāmin*) is established. When, however, he produces only a deva-existence, having abandoned the defilement that brings to a return-birth here — at that moment the fruit of non-returning (*anāgāmin*) is established.

From the abandonment of all the defilements conducive to existence and rebirth, the supreme fruit, arhatship, is established. Moreover, the fruit of once-returning: from the abandonment of the three fetters and, of lust, hatred and delusion, from their attenuation, it was established by the Blessed One. From the abandonment of the five lower fetters (*avarabhāgīya*), the fruit of non-returning. From the complete abandonment of all defilements, the fruit of arhatship — this is called the fruit of cultivation. Now, in those persons of lust-, hatred-, delusion-, conceit-, or applied-thought conduct, the conduct should first be purified by means of a conduct-purifying object. Afterwards they attain mental abiding. For them, that object is fixed; they must necessarily apply themselves to that object. But for one of even-balanced conduct (*sama-bhāga-carita*), wherever there is delight, with that he should apply himself, simply for mental abiding, not for conduct-purification. As with the even-balanced, so with the one of slight dust (*manda-rajaska*) is to be understood. Their distinction is this: one of lust-conduct etc., applying himself, becomes one who attains [mental abiding] only after a long time; the even-balanced, not in too long a time; the one of slight dust, however, swiftly, very swiftly attains mental abiding. The marks of persons of lust-conduct etc. were stated before.

What are the marks of the person of even-balanced conduct and of the one of slight dust?

Answer: in the person of even-balanced conduct, all those marks are present which belong to persons of lust-conduct etc., but those [marks] of lust and the rest are not intense, not pre-eminent. Thus they come to be on a par with those of the lust-conducted and the rest; when the conditions are present they are not distinguished. Now, the marks of the person of slight dust: he is unobstructed, originally pure, and equipped with the requisites. He is full of serenity, intelligent, of merit, and endowed with virtues. There are three obstacles (*āvaraṇa*): the obstacle of action (*karma-āvaraṇa*), the obstacle of defilement (*kleśa-āvaraṇa*), and the obstacle of result (*vipāka-āvaraṇa*). The obstacle of action consists in the five actions of immediate retribution (*ānantarya*), and whatever other action is intentional, heavy, which, when its result has ripened, for the arising of the path makes an obstruction. The obstacle of defilement consists in intense-defilement-state and long-defilement-state, which in this very life cannot be purified by the conduct-purifying or the object-purifying [object]. The obstacle of result: a place of rebirth where the noble path does not operate, does not [Conjectural: grow]; there he brings forth a result; Or [it occurs] where the noble path can function, Born and present there, he is deaf-mute, [Conjectural: speaking by hand signs], incapable of understanding the meaning of dharmas well- or ill-cultivated. Here, original purity is virtue thoroughly pure and upright view. Virtue thoroughly pure is to be understood by ten causes. [Conjectural: Uprightness of view] is born from conjunction with faith, from conjunction with conviction, from [Conjectural: freedom from deceit and guile], and from practice that leads out, free of doubt and indecision, with the meaning of the dharma well-considered. The view that is conjoined with faith is not carried away from this Dharma-Vinaya; and, conjoined with conviction, in the Buddhas’ and the Buddha-disciples’ [Conjectural: inconceivable power and inconceivable places of rebirth,] in the deep teaching and in the undeclared matters, he has conviction. He is not alarmed, does not fall into alarm. Free of guile and pretense, the view by which he becomes straight, of straight nature, he practices as instructed. He reveals himself as he actually is. Or, the meaning of dharmas with regard to impermanence, suffering, emptiness and selflessness is well-considered, well-weighed, well-examined; by which cause he is without doubt, without indecision. Gone past the forked road, he goes on to distinction. Thus this four-mode view as taught is called the straightness of view. Being equipped with the requisites: the requisites in detail were set out before; in brief, they are again fourfold: merit-requisite, knowledge-requisite, of the previous [life], and of the present life. The merit-requisite is that by which conforming requisites manifest, auspicious. And he gains good friends. And he becomes without obstacle when applied. The knowledge-requisite is that by which he becomes intelligent, capable of understanding the meaning of dharmas well- or ill-stated. He becomes a recipient of conforming dharma-teaching, meaning-teaching, and admonition. The [requisite] of the previous [life] is that by which now the faculties become mature, by accumulation of prior wholesome roots. The [requisite] of the present life is, namely, wholesome desire for the dharma. Thus, for one whose faculties are matured, the restraint of virtue and the restraint of the senses [follow], in detail as before. Now, fullness of serenity: he does not doubt the Teacher, does not waver. He has serene faith, he is convinced. As toward the Teacher, so toward the dharma and the training, in detail as before. Intelligence is that by which he quickly grasps a dharma, and even after a long time does not [Conjectural: forget] the dharma and its meaning, but swiftly penetrates dharma and its meaning. Merit-doing is that by which he becomes handsome, well-favored to see, inspiring serene faith, long-lived, of acceptable speech, of great standing, well known, of great merit, recipient of robes etc., honored and revered by kings and others. ‘Endowed with virtues’ — the virtues to be understood are few-desires (*alpecchatā*) and so on. As stated [before] under the ornament of the recluse, with these he is endowed by nature. These and the like are to be understood as the marks of the person of slight dust.

[Now,] six person-designations: namely, recluse (*śramaṇa*), brāhmaṇa, brahma-farer (*brahmacārin*), monk (*bhikṣu*), ascetic (*yati*), and renunciant (*pravrajita*). Of recluses, four: victor over the path (*mārga-jina*), teacher of the path (*mārga-deśika*), one who lives by the path (*mārga-jīvin*), and defiler of the path (*mārga-dūṣin*). The well-gone one (*sugata*) is the victor over the path. He who is a Dharma-speaker is the teacher of the path. He who has entered upon practice lives by the path. He who has entered upon wrong practice is the defiler of the path. Sugata is so called as one who has attained the destruction of lust, hatred, [and] delusion without remainder. Dharma-speaker: one who teaches the Dharma for the subduing of lust, hatred, and delusion. The rightly-practicing one: one who has entered upon practice for the subduing of lust, hatred, and delusion. The wrongly-practicing one: of bad virtue, of evil dharma. Moreover, trainees and non-trainees are called victors over the path through their conquest of the defilements to be abandoned by seeing and by cultivation. Here the Tathāgata, the bodhisattva practicing for future awakening, and the disciples — bearers of the sūtra, bearers of the vinaya, bearers of the mātṛkā — who hold the conventional Dharma-Vinaya, who set Dharma-guidance in motion — these are called teachers of the path. Now, the good worldlings who have entered upon practice for their own benefit — scrupulous, desiring training, applied for the attainment of the not-yet-attained, for the reaching of the not-yet-reached, for the direct realization of the not-yet-realized; fit and capable, [and so on] up through [their being capable of] the direct realization of the not-yet-realized. These are called those who live by the path. In them there is warmth for the arising of this noble wisdom-faculty; they are not dead, but are said to be alive. Therefore they are called those who live by the path. Now, that person who is of bad virtue, of evil dharma, [and so on] up through [the formula] ‘non-celibate, claiming to be celibate,’ he is called defiler of the path — by him the path is defiled from the root, from the beginning, by which he becomes unfit, incapable, a non-vessel for the arising of the path, even though the teaching of the path exists, even though realization is present. Therefore he is called defiler of the path. And this was said by the Blessed One with this intent: ‘Here is the [Conjectural: first] recluse, here [the second, and so on] up to the fourth; empty are the other doctrines of recluses and brāhmaṇas. Where the noble eightfold path is known, there is the first recluse, [and] there [are recluses] as far as the fourth.’

[Now,] of brāhmaṇas, three: namely, brāhmaṇa by birth (*jāti*), brāhmaṇa by designation (*saṁjñā*), and brāhmaṇa by practice (*pratipatti*). Now, the *jātibrāhmaṇa* (brāhmaṇa by birth): this one who is a *jātibrāhmaṇa* — family-born, womb-born, mother-arisen, [duly] produced from mother and from father. Now, the *saṁjñābrāhmaṇa* (brāhmaṇa by designation) — in the world this becomes [his] name: notion, designation, convention, usage. The brāhmaṇa by practice: the one whose task is finally done, by whom the evil unwholesome dharmas have been removed. As was said: ‘There is no [further] task for the brāhmaṇa; the brāhmaṇa is one whose task is done.’ [Now,] of brahma-farers, three: namely, the abstinence-undertaker, the in-between-abandoner (*antara-prahāyin*), [and] the absolute abandoner (*atyanta-prahāyin*). The abstinence-undertaker: one who is restrained from [Conjectural: non-]celibacy, the dharma of coupling, having undertaken the training. The in-between-abandoner: one who, a worldling, is free of lust for sense-pleasures by the worldly path. The absolute abandoner: namely, the non-returner, or again, the arhat.

[Now,] of monks, five: ‘he begs (*bhikṣati*), therefore he is a monk (*bhikṣu*)’; monk by self-claim; monk by designation; monk because his defilements are broken (*bhinnakleśa*); monk ordained by the fourth-formal-motion procedure (*jñapticaturtha-karman*).

[Now,] of ascetics, three: ascetic by restraint from evil conduct — the one restrained from unwholesome bodily and verbal action; ascetic by restraint of [the] senses — the one with guarded sense-doors, of guarded mindfulness, [with] alert mindfulness, [as elaborated] above; ascetic by restraint of defilements — the one for whom the defilements to be abandoned by seeing are abandoned, and [as for] each arisen [Lacuna] applied thought, malevolent applied thought and harmful applied thought; covetousness, malevolence, wrong view, false view, anger, resentment, concealment, spite, and the like — states leading to lower realms, hellish, going to bad destinations, making one a non-recluse — as they arise, he does not allow [them], [but] abandons [them], purifies [the mind of them], puts an end to [them]. This is twofold restraint of defilements: restraint of obsession (*paryavasthāna*) and restraint of both [obsession and latent tendency].

[Now,] of renunciants, two: [those in the] well-stated Dharma-Vinaya and [those in the] ill-stated Dharma-Vinaya. [Those in] the well-stated Dharma-Vinaya: monk, nun, probationer, male novice, female novice. Moreover, he [is one who] renounces from himself the evil unwholesome dharmas — [such a one] is called renunciant in the ultimate sense. [Those in the] ill-stated Dharma-Vinaya: namely the tīrthika, the wanderer (*parivrājaka*), or the Jain, or [Conjectural: the ash-smeared mendicant], or another such kind. Thus [the six designations] — recluse, brāhmaṇa, brahma-farer, monk, ascetic, renunciant — [Damaged] The distinction of time: a path long cultivated, and a path not long cultivated.

How are these four distinctions the grounds [of the eight persons]?

Or [Damaged] — knower of means, skilful: that is the meaning. Application on the side of continuity, continuous, alert — so it is called. Long cultivated [Damaged] by the threefold distinction, the distinction of yoga, of application and of time, is the establishment of the [Conjectural: eight] persons. The person of unripe faculties, even if a knower of means, continuous and experienced, does not become an achiever of [Conjectural: what is proper], of the dharma, of what is skilful. There, one of ripe faculties and [Damaged]. One of ripe faculties, a knower of means, does not become one of quick higher knowledge. There, ripe faculties, knower of means, not continuous, not experienced — he is not one whose own benefit is accomplished, task-done. And who is of ripe faculties, knower of means, continuous, experienced — he is one who succeeds. He is of quick higher knowledge. And he has accomplished his own task; he is task-done.

Now, the four māras and the many māra-actions are to be understood by the yogin engaged in yoga, and, having been fully known, they should be avoided. The four māras: namely, the aggregate-māra (*skandha-māra*), the defilement-māra (*kleśa-māra*), the death-māra (*maraṇa-māra*), and the deva-son-māra (*devaputra-māra*). The five aggregates of clinging are the aggregate-māra. The defilements ranging in the three realms [are the defilement-māra]. The death, the dying, of these and those beings from this and that group of beings is the death-māra. And the deva-son born in the realm of desire who, for one engaged on the [Conjectural: wholesome] side in passing beyond the aggregates, the defilements and death, [Conjectural: having attained mastery,] brings obstruction in the making of distraction — this is the deva-son-māra. Now: where one dies, and that which is death, and that by which one is made to yield to death, [and that which] turns [one] aside by an obstructive thing — by reference to this, the four māras are established. Here, one dies when the five aggregates of clinging have been born and are growing; one generates defilements and, born again in the future, dies; and the falling, the falling-away of beings, the cessation of the life-faculty, the dying: this is death in its own nature. And the deva-son-māra creates obstacle for one applied to the surmounting of death, by which he is unable to surmount the death-nature,

or only surmounts it after a long time. Now, the worldling free of lust by the worldly path is not under Māra’s control —

whether staying here or reborn there, [he is] not under [his] control; but one who is not free of lust [is]. [Of these,] the one [Conjectural: not] free of lust is as if in [his] hand, to be dealt with as he wishes. Or again, the one free of lust [by the worldly path] is bound by Māra’s bonds, not released from māra’s snares, since he must [eventually] return to this realm [of desire]. Now, māra-actions: When anyone’s desire for the dharma has arisen, connected with renunciation, and, making greed for sense-pleasures predominant, he [Conjectural: turns back from it] — this is to be known as a māra-action. For one dwelling with guarded sense-doors, whose mind, in lust-arousing forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, dharmas, in sign-seizing and feature-seizing, leaps forth — this is to be known as māra-action. Likewise for one dwelling with knowledge of right measure in food, whose mind leaps forth in delight-and-lust toward exquisite tastes; or toward irregularity in eating. Likewise for one dwelling applied to wakefulness in the early and late night, whose mind leaps forth to sleeping-pleasure, lying-down-pleasure, side-lying-pleasure — this is to be known as māra-action. Likewise for one dwelling with full awareness, in setting forth and returning etc., seeing a youthful, beautiful-complexioned, lust-arousing woman, with mind unwisely seizing the sign, leaping forth; or seeing varied worldly spectacles, mind leaps forth; in much-business, much-task, mind leaps forth — namely in delight in mixing with householders and renunciants, in being of one resolve with evil friends, and, [Conjectural: having seen them,] in approval, the mind leaps forth. This is to be known as māra-action.

Likewise toward the Buddha, dharma, saṅgha, suffering, origin, cessation, path, in this world, in the next world, doubts and indecisions arise — this is to be known as māra-action. Gone to the forest, or to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, he sees a great alarming fearsomeness, terror-causing, hair-raising. In the guise of a brāhmaṇa, or of a human, or of a non-human, someone approaches, and unwisely, [Conjectural: dissuades him] from the bright side, and incites him into the dark side. This is to be known as māra-action. When the mind leaps forth toward gain and honor, to stinginess, great-desire, discontent, anger, grudge, deceit, cajoling talk, etc., to dharmas counter to the ornament of the recluse — mind leaps forth. This is to be known as māra-action. Such are the kinds of māra-actions to be understood — those of the four māras as appropriate.

Now, [Conjectural: by three] causes the undertaking of one rightly applied becomes fruitless: namely, [Conjectural: non-]perfecting of the faculties, [Conjectural: non-]conforming instruction, and weakness of concentration. If the faculties are not perfected, though the instruction is conforming and the concentration is [Conjectural: strong], thus his undertaking is fruitless. If the faculties [Conjectural: are] perfected, and the instruction is not conforming, though the concentration is strong — thus the undertaking is fruitless. If the faculties are perfected, and the instruction is conforming, but the concentration is weak — thus the undertaking is fruitless. [Whereas:] if the faculties are perfected, and the instruction is conforming, and the concentration is strong — thus the undertaking is fruitful. By [absence of] these three causes one is fruitless; by [presence of] those same three causes, [one is] fruitful.
