# Closing Material: Rebirth, Lokottara Path, Arhatship

*Śrāvakabhūmi · sbh4.3*

Thus, through the frequent cultivation of these twelve perceptions, the manifestation, as appropriate, of the five higher knowledges and of the noble qualities [Conjectural: not shared with ordinary persons] is to be known accordingly. In the first *dhyāna* — when cultivated in mild, middling, or strong degree — one attains rebirth in the company of the Brahmakāyika, Brahmapurohita, and Mahābrahmā gods accordingly. In the second *dhyāna* — when cultivated in mild, middling, or strong degree — one attains rebirth in the company of the Parīttābha, [Conjectural: Apramāṇābha], and Ābhāsvara gods accordingly. In the third *dhyāna* — when cultivated in mild, middling, or strong degree — one attains rebirth in the company of the Parīttaśubha, [Restored: Apramāṇaśubha], and [Restored: Śubhakṛtsna] gods accordingly. In the fourth *dhyāna* — when cultivated in mild, middling, or strong degree — one attains rebirth in the company of the Anabhraka, Puṇyaprasava, and Bṛhatphala gods accordingly. If, further, [the practitioner is] a non-returner (*anāgāmī*), he cultivates, interspersed with the uncontaminated fourth *dhyāna*, the contaminated one. When that is cultivated in mild, middling, strong, stronger, or strongest degree, he is reborn accordingly in the company of the gods of the five Pure Abodes. Namely: among the [Restored: Avṛha], Atapa, [Restored: Sudṛśa], Sudarśana, and Akaniṣṭha [gods]. When the sphere of infinite space, the sphere of infinite consciousness, the sphere of nothingness, and the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception are cultivated in mild, middling, or strong degree, one attains rebirth in the company of the gods belonging to those spheres. And those gods are formless; therefore there is no distinction among them made by difference of place, but there is a specificity made by [mode of] abiding. When the non-perception attainment (*asaṁjñisamāpatti*) is cultivated, one attains rebirth in the company of the non-perception beings among the gods.

Now, what are the signs of one without lust (*vītarāga*)?

It is said: He has steady bodily action and stable faculties. His posture is not quickly abandoned. He maintains a single posture for a long time without distress. nor does he [Conjectural: quickly] long for another posture. He speaks little, and speaks calmly. He does not delight in crowds, does not delight in company; his speech proceeds with steadiness. Having seen forms with the eye, he is comprehending-of-form. He is not comprehending-of-lust-toward-form. Likewise he is comprehending of sound, smell, taste, tangible — but not comprehending-of-lust toward [sound, and so on] up to the tangible. He is confident. His intellect is deep; he is embraced by abundant pliancy of mind and body.

He is without covetousness, unshakeable. He is patient; sense-desire-thoughts and other evil thoughts do not disturb his mind. These are the signs of one without lust — to be known thus. This much is the analysis of the mundane-path travel.

Now, [if the practitioner] wishes to go by the supramundane path, the seven attentions arise for him in sequence, in connection with the four noble truths: beginning with the characteristic-knowing attention (*lakṣaṇapratisaṁvedī*) and ending with the fruit-of-practice-culmination (*prayoganiṣṭhāphala*), up through the attainment of arhatship. Here the practitioner who has taken up, [Conjectural: by hearing], the four noble truths with their summary and exposition — or who has well-cultivated attention, or who has attained a *maula dhyāna* or a formless attainment — comprehends the characteristic of the truth of suffering through four aspects: namely, the aspect of impermanence, the aspect of suffering, [Restored: the aspect of emptiness,] and the aspect of not-self. He comprehends the truth of arising through four aspects: by cause (*hetu*), by arising (*samudaya*), by origination (*prabhava*), and by condition (*pratyaya*). He comprehends the characteristic of the truth of cessation through four aspects: namely, by cessation (*nirodha*), by calm (*śānta*), by sublime (*praṇīta*), and by escape (*niḥsaraṇa*). He comprehends the characteristic of the truth of the path through four aspects: namely, by path (*mārga*), by method (*nyāya*), by practice (*pratipatti*), and by deliverance (*nairyāṇika*). This is for him the characteristic-knowing attention.

Examining the truth of suffering through ten aspects, he enters into [its] four aspects. Through what ten? Namely: by the aspect of transformation (*vipariṇāma*), [Conjectural: by the aspect of destruction,] by the aspect of separation (*viyoga*), by the aspect of being-at-hand (*sannihita*), by the aspect of nature-of-things (*dharmatā*), by the aspect of fetter-and-bondage (*saṁyojanabandhana*), by the aspect of unwished-for (*aniṣṭa*), by the aspect of insecurity (*ayogakṣema*), [Conjectural: by the aspect of non-apprehension], and by the aspect of non-autonomy (*asvātantrya*). These ten aspects he examines by proof-establishing reasoning (*upapattisādhanayukti*). Here, first, scripture (*āgama*): as the Blessed One said, 'All formations are impermanent.' Those formations, in summary, are the sentient world and the container world.

The Blessed One said, concerning the sentient world: 'I see, monks, with the divine eye, purified and surpassing the human, beings passing away and arising' — in detail, up through, 'at the breaking up of the body, in the good destination, in the heavenly world, among the gods.' Thus by this account the Blessed One, possessed of vision, who sees directly, has explained the impermanence of the sentient world. And the Blessed One said, 'There is a time, monks, when, by the passing of a long time, gradually, up to the appearing of seven suns in the world.' as in the Sūtra of the Seven-Suns Simile, up to: 'of this great earth and of Sumeru, king of mountains, [burning and blazing, the flame reaches] even up to the Brahmā world; and of the container world, burnt and consumed, not even soot is perceived. Not even a remnant of ash is perceived.' By this account the Blessed One has explained the impermanence of the container world. Relying thus far on trustworthy testimony, this yogi attains certainty in the impermanence of all formations through the predominance of faith.

Having thus attained certainty, by the predominance of faith he investigates again and again impermanence as directly perceived, [Restored: not hidden], and not dependent on another.

And how, again, does he investigate?

It is said: He establishes a twofold entity. It is said: Inward and outward. There, the inward entity is the six sense-bases (*ṣaḍāyatana*); the outward entity is sixteenfold. Namely: the earth-entity, that is, villages, towns, houses, marketplaces, and so on. The park-entity: grasses, plants, trees. The mountain-entity: varied mountain formations. [Conjectural: The water-entity:] springs, lakes, ponds, rivers, and streams. The field-entity; the treasury-and-store-entity; [Restored: the food, drink, vehicle,] clothing, ornament, dance-song-music, perfume-garland-unguent, utensil, light, and attendance-of-men-and-women entities — these are the sixteen entities. Having thus established the inward and outward entity, he investigates first, with respect to the inward entity, by attention governed by direct perception, transformation-impermanence through the aspect of transformation. There, the transformation of the inward entity is fifteenfold. There are eight transformation-causes.

What is the fifteenfold transformation?

Of the inward entity, namely: [transformation] made by stage of life, by color, by shape, by prosperity and adversity, by completeness-or-deficiency of limbs, by exertion, by assault from another, by cold and heat, by posture, by [Restored: contact], by defilement, by [Restored: illness], by death, by [the corpse-states beginning with] the discolored, and by complete non-appearance and exhaustion — [this is] the transformation. There are eight transformation-causes.

What [are they]?

It is said: Namely: passage of time, assault by another, use, seasonal change, fire-burning, water-flooding, wind-drying, and encounter with a different condition.

There, passage of time means: of material things, even when kept in their own place, after a lapse of time decrepitude is observed — agedness, alteration of form.

There, assault by another means: as when another, with various weapons and various kinds of assault, brings about various alterations in various forms. There, use means: as when the respective owners, using various material [things], with use as the governing factor, bring about alteration. There, seasonal change means: as in winter, the paleness, the falling-away of grasses, plants, and trees arises. In summer and rains again, fullness and greenness; similarly the abundance of fruit, abundance of flower, abundance of leaf — and the loss of these. There, fire-burning means: as when a fire let loose goes on burning villages, towns, kingdoms, and capitals. There, water-flooding means: as when a great mass of water that has arisen goes on flooding villages, towns, kingdoms, and capitals.

There, wind-drying means: as when, by a great mass of wind, moist regions of the earth dry up very quickly; likewise wet [Conjectural: clothes] and wet kinds of crops. The supervening of a different condition means: for one experiencing a pleasant feeling in dependence on a contact to be felt as pleasant, the supervening of a contact to be felt as [Conjectural: painful]; likewise for one experiencing a painful feeling, the supervening of a contact to be felt as [Restored: pleasant]; for one experiencing a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, the supervening of a contact to be felt as pleasant or as painful. Likewise, for one with lust, the supervening of an occasion for aversion, from whose supervening the obsession of lust departs and the obsession of aversion arises. Likewise for the hostile and the deluded, the supervening of an occasion for the arising of a dissimilar defilement is to be understood. Likewise, when eye-consciousness is present, the supervening of a sound-object, or of a smell-, taste-, tangible- or dharma-sign — by which other object dissimilar [Restored: consciousnesses] arise. These are the eight transformation-causes. Whatever transformation there is — of dharmas with form or without form — all of it [comes about] by these eight; nothing beyond these, nothing more.

How does one investigate the transformation of the inward entity made by stage of life?

Here he has seen, in himself or in others, the stage of youth up through the stage of old age; having seen the formation-continuum as dissimilar earlier and later, broken, transformed, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations of mine.' [Conjectural: For] this earlier-and-later alteration is observed directly.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by color?

Here, in himself or in others, the earlier good color, good complexion, and [Restored: clear] skin-color [has been seen]; and later the bad color, bad complexion, harshness, harsh coloration he sees. Having seen, and turning back again, at another time he sees that same good color, [Restored: good complexion,] and clear skin-color. It occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations.' Of these, this color-alteration of such a kind is observed directly.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by shape?

As with color, so shape is to be known by emaciation or stoutness. [Conjectural: How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence of the inward thing made by prosperity and adversity?] Prosperity and adversity: namely, prosperity in relatives, prosperity in possessions, or prosperity in morality and view. The reverse of this is adversity.

How does one investigate the impermanence of transformation of limbs and minor limbs?

Here he has first seen, in himself or in another, the limbs [Conjectural: intact]; at another time he sees them deficient — by a king, a thief, a human, or a non-human. Having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations of mine' — as before.

Likewise [he sees], of himself or of others, a fatigued body, a wearied body — from running, swimming, leaping, climbing, or doing various [Conjectural: actions] rapidly — at another time he sees [him] free of fatigue and weariness. It occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations' — as before. Thus he investigates the transformation-impermanence made by exertion.

Likewise he sees, in himself or in others, alteration of the body by another's assault: namely, of one struck with creepers, with whips, with canes, or with straps; likewise by various contacts with gadflies, mosquitoes, and crawling things. Or again at another time he does not see that alteration. Having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations' — as before. Thus he investigates the transformation-impermanence made by another's assault.

Likewise he sees himself or another, when the cold season has set in, listless in body, shrunken in body, beset by the grip of cold, overcome by longing for warmth; or again, when the hot season has set in, brisk in body, sweating in body, heated in body, [Conjectural: dry-mouthed], overcome by thirst, longing for the touch of cold. Having seen, and turning back again, in the cold season he sees [him] in the aspects stated before. And having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations' — as before. Thus he investigates the transformation-impermanence made by [Conjectural: cold and heat]. Again, he sees himself or another [Conjectural: being harmed] by one or another of the four postures — walking, standing, sitting, lying; and at times he sees [him] benefited by that very same [posture]. Having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations' — as before. Thus he investigates the transformation-impermanence made by posture.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by contact?

Touched by contact to be felt as pleasant, experiencing the pleasant feeling arisen in dependence on contact to be felt as pleasant — he distinguishes for himself the state of pleasant feeling. As the state of pleasant feeling, so the states of painful and of neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. Having seen, [Conjectural: in the before-and-after of] these feelings, their ever-newness, their never growing old, [Conjectural: their coming and going], their lasting only a while, their brief presence, their becoming otherwise, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations' — as before.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by defilement?

He fully understands the mind arisen with lust. [Likewise the mind] without lust, with hatred, without hatred. [Likewise] with delusion, without delusion, defiled by one or another secondary-defilement — he fully understands as defiled. Or the undefiled, [he understands] again as undefiled. Having seen, in the before-and-after, the mind-continuum [Restored: rent], changed and unchanged, by these defilements and secondary defilements, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations.' For the defilement-made transformation of these is observed directly.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by illness?

Here he has at some time seen himself and others free of disease, happy, strong. At another time he sees himself or another afflicted, suffering, gravely ill, touched by bodily feelings that are painful, sharp — in detail as before. And again at another time he sees [them] free of disease, happy, strong. Having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations' — as before.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by death?

Here he sees [someone] alive, sustained, staying, getting along; at another time he sees him dead, deceased, a corpse empty of consciousness. And having seen, it occurs to him thus — in detail as before.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by the discolored and the rest?

On one occasion he sees that same corpse in the discolored state, on one occasion in the festering state — in detail, up to the bone-skeleton state. Having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations' — in detail as before.

How does one investigate the transformation-impermanence made by [Conjectural: complete non-appearance] and exhaustion?

At another time he sees even that bone-skeleton state wholly destroyed, shattered, crumbled, wholly gone from the range of sight. And having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations.' For of these, earlier and later, this kind of alteration is observed directly — transformation. Thus, by attention governed by direct perception, he investigates the transformation-impermanence of the inward entity through fifteen aspects. Having investigated it, he investigates the transformation-impermanence of the sixteenfold outward entity. Those regions of earth which he earlier saw unbuilt upon — afterwards he sees them built upon with the kinds of buildings — houses, shops, almshouses, temples, monasteries — [Conjectural: new], well made, well plastered. At another time he sees them decrepit: decayed, cut and torn, crumbled, damaged and fallen, holed, burnt by fire or swept away by water. And having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations.' For of these, earlier and later, this kind of alteration — transformation — is observed directly. Thus he investigates transformation-impermanence with respect to the earth. Likewise he sees grasses, plants, trees, gardens, and parks with abundant leaf, flower, and fruit, green, pleasing, delightful. At another time he sees them dried up, bereft of leaf, flower, and fruit, or burnt by the burning of fire. Likewise the mountains: on one occasion he sees them with rocks in full, on another with rocks tumbled down, with fallen peaks, fallen pinnacles, uneven, burnt by fire, washed over by water; likewise springs, lakes, ponds, rivers, streams, wells and the like — on one occasion he sees them with abundant water, on another with diminished water, or wholly dried up, become barren, hollow. Likewise he sees enterprises on one occasion prospering, on another failing — namely enterprises of farming, of shipping, of lawful trade, and of the various crafts; likewise of treasuries and stores of many and various kinds he sees on one occasion the accumulation, on another the depletion. Likewise food and drink: on one occasion he sees them in the unprepared state, on one occasion in the prepared state, on one occasion soaked with saliva, on one occasion up to the state of feces and urine. Likewise various vehicles he sees on one occasion well decorated, well adorned, new; on one occasion devoid of ornament, devoid of decoration, decrepit. Likewise of clothes he sees on one occasion newness, on one occasion oldness, wearing out; on one occasion cleanness, on one occasion dirtiness. Likewise of ornaments he sees on one occasion the unmade state, on one occasion the made state, on one occasion soundness, on one occasion the broken and shattered state, worn away. Likewise of dance, song and music he sees the rise and the breaking-off of the various present performances. Likewise of perfumes, garlands and unguents he sees freshness, fragrance, and unfadedness; at another time he sees them not very fragrant, foul-smelling, faded, dried up. Likewise of vessels and equipment he sees the unmade state, the made state, soundness, and brokenness. Likewise he sees the arising and passing of light and [Conjectural: darkness]. Likewise he sees the rise and fall of the attendance of men and women, its instability. And having seen, it occurs to him: 'Impermanent indeed are these formations.' For of these outward formations — the six things possessed and the ten requisites of the body — this kind of alteration, transformation, is observed directly. And everywhere this [refrain] is to be understood as abbreviated.

By these eight causes of transformation expounded before, as appropriate to the inward and outward entity, he investigates impermanence through the aspect of transformation by attention governed by direct perception. As that transformation-impermanence has been directly seen and experienced by him, and he has become, with regard to it, independent of others, not to be led by another — recollecting it just so, he investigates, and becomes certain. Therefore it is called attention governed by direct perception. Having thus investigated transformation-impermanence by attention governed by direct perception, for those formations that have form whose momentary arising, breaking and dissolving, though existing and present, is not perceived — there, relying on attention governed by direct perception, he makes an inference; and he makes the inference thus: of these formations, arisen, broken and dissolved in a moment, this earlier-and-later alteration is fitting, but not for those that persist just as they were; therefore the formations are momentary: with those conditions present, they arise thus and thus, and once arisen, they perish of their own accord, without depending on a cause of destruction. And those causes of transformation operate for arising-otherwise; they are causes of an altered arising, not of destruction.

Why?

Because, for formations that have perished together with that very cause of destruction, a dissimilar continuation is observed, and not a total non-continuation. Or again, those formations of which a total non-continuation is observed — as, of [Conjectural: water] being boiled, at the end there is total exhaustion; and of the container world burnt by fire not even ash is found, and not even a remaining shadow is found; of these too, through the progressive exhaustion of the causes, at the end there is total non-existence. And it is not done by fire alone. Therefore these eight causes of transformation are as stated; destruction, however, happens of itself. Having thus attained certainty, by inferential attention, that formations arise, break and dissolve in a moment, he makes a further inference about the continuation of formations in another world, which is not directly perceived.

And he makes the inference thus: There are beings who are found to be [Conjectural: good-looking] and ill-looking, of high family, of [Conjectural: wealthy] family and of poor family, of little influence, long-lived, whose word is heeded and whose word is not heeded, of sharp faculties. This variety of beings is fitting only if there is variety of action, not otherwise. Whatever varied action, wholesome and unwholesome, was really done before by whichever beings, done and accumulated — by that cause, by that condition, this variety of their individual existence has come about.

And it is not fitting that this is caused by Īśvara's creation. If it were caused by Īśvara's creation, then either it would have Īśvara alone as its condition, or Īśvara would create by means of something else. If it had Īśvara alone as its condition, then there would be simultaneity of Īśvara and these formations. If Īśvara is earlier and the formations later, the formations do not have Īśvara as their condition. Or if Īśvara's resolve is the cause of creation, and not Īśvara himself —

then is that desire caused or uncaused?

If caused, and caused by Īśvara alone, that does not fit, being equivalent to the previous fault. Or if it has another cause, then desire, effort, and resolve have as their cause other dharmas apart from Īśvara; and likewise all formations will have dharmas alone as their cause. What use is Īśvara, imagined in vain? By such inferential attention of this kind, he attains certainty about the continuation of formations in another world. Thus, by the governing power of three kinds of attention — governed by faith, governed by direct perception, governed by inference — he investigates impermanence. Of the fivefold impermanence listed earlier, which is accompanied by cultivation in five aspects, the transformation-aspect and the destruction-aspect have been expounded.

What is impermanence in the aspect of separation?

It is said: It is to be known with respect to the inward and to the outward.

With respect to the inward: as when someone was formerly master of others, not a slave, not a servant, not doing another's work; at another time, abandoning the status of master and of non-slave, he comes to the status of slave of others. He is separated from the status of master. Likewise possessions, existing and present, untransformed, undestroyed, are carried off by a king, or by thieves, or by enemies, or by heirs — thus impermanence is to be known. Here, impermanence in the nature-of-things aspect: as of that very transformation-impermanence and destruction-impermanence which, not yet come together in the present time, will come to be [Conjectural: in the future] — he penetrates their nature: 'These formations are of such nature, of such kind, in the future.' Impermanence in the at-hand aspect: in these [formations], right here, he regards transformation-impermanence, destruction-impermanence and separation-impermanence as come together and [Conjectural: present]. Thus, having attained certainty in the fivefold impermanence of inward and outward formations by these five aspects as appropriate, through abundance of attention and through the governing power of proof-establishing and of cultivation, immediately he enters the suffering-aspect. It occurs to him: 'These formations are impermanent; their impermanence is fitting because they are subject to birth.' Thus these very formations are subject to birth; and birth is suffering. As birth, so aging, illness, death, association with the unpleasant, separation from the pleasant, and frustration of desire are to be known. Thus, first, by the unwished-for aspect he enters the suffering-aspect. He enters the suffering-aspect in the aggregates conducive to pleasant feeling, contaminated and with grasping, by the fetter-and-bondage aspect. For these serve him as the basis of the craving-fetter; and the craving-fetter is the basis of the bondage of birth, aging, illness, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection and despair, and of the bondage of lust, hatred and delusion. Here, by the insecurity aspect, he enters the suffering-aspect in the aggregates conducive to neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. For the aggregates conducive to neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, with grasping, accompanied by encumbrance, [Conjectural: followed by seeds], are not freed from suffering of suffering and suffering of change, impermanent, subject to cessation. Thus this yogi, in the formations conducive to pleasant feeling and in pleasant feeling, has entered suffering of change — namely by the fetter-and-bondage aspect. In the formations conducive to painful feeling and in painful feeling he has entered suffering of suffering — namely by the unwished-for aspect. In the formations conducive to neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling and in neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling he has entered suffering of conditioning — namely by the insecurity aspect. It occurs to him: Taking as governing the fetter-and-bondage aspect, the unwished-for aspect, and the [Conjectural: insecurity] aspect, [he sees that] whatever is felt among the three feelings is suffering here; thus, by attention preceded by the impermanence-aspect, he has entered the suffering-aspect. It occurs to him: 'Only faculties are apprehended, only sense-objects, only the experience born of them, only mind; [Conjectural: 'I' and 'mine'] are only name, only view, only designation; nothing beyond this, nothing more than this.' 'This being so, there are only aggregates; in these aggregates there is nothing permanent, stable, eternal, [Conjectural: standing as master] — no self, no being, who would be born, or [Conjectural: age], or die, or who here and there would experience the fruit and ripening of actions done.' 'Thus indeed these formations are empty, devoid of self.' Thus by the non-apprehension aspect (*anupalambhākāra*) he enters the empty-aspect (*śūnyākāra*). It occurs to him: 'Again, these formations, endowed with their own characteristic, with the characteristic of impermanence and with the characteristic of suffering — they too, being dependently arisen, are not autonomous; what is not autonomous is not self.' Thus by the non-autonomy aspect (*asvatantrākāra*) he enters the not-self aspect. Thus the yogi has comprehended the characteristic of the truth of suffering by four aspects taken in ten modes.

Here, the impermanence-aspect is comprised by five aspects: namely by the transformation-aspect, [Conjectural: by the destruction-aspect,] by the separation-aspect, by the at-hand aspect, by the nature-of-things aspect. The suffering-aspect is comprised by three aspects: by the fetter-and-bondage aspect, by the unwished-for aspect, by the insecurity aspect. The empty-aspect is comprised by one aspect, namely by the non-apprehension aspect. The not-self aspect is comprised by one aspect, namely by the non-autonomy aspect. Having thus, by ten aspects, entered the four aspects and comprehended the characteristic of suffering, [he asks]: 'What is the cause of this suffering, what its arising, its origination, its condition? By the abandoning of what would there be the abandoning of this suffering?' Thus by these four aspects he comprehends the characteristic of the truth of arising. Craving, because it [Conjectural: projects] suffering, is [regarded] as cause; because, having projected it, it brings it about — bringing about its arising — as arising; because it is the origination of the suffering produced, as origination; and again, because it takes hold of the seeds of suffering in the future and, in sequence, brings about the arising of suffering, as condition. Another way of putting it: because of the coming-about of existence that has grasping as its cause; because it is the origination of birth, which is preceded by existence; and because of the production of aging, illness, death, sorrow and the rest, which have birth as their condition — as cause, as arising, as origination, as condition, as appropriate, is to be understood. Another way of putting it: The latent tendency of defilement which is the basis is the cause of the production of re-existence; and of the obsession born of it, [the rest] is to be understood as appropriate. Another way of putting it: The latent tendency of defilement which is the basis — the latent tendency of craving and so on — is the cause of the production of re-existence in the future; and of the obsession born of it, as appropriate, [it is] arising, origination, and condition. Here, because it brings about the craving for re-existence, as arising; and that craving for re-existence is the origination of the craving accompanied by delight and lust; and that craving accompanied by delight and lust, once arisen, is the condition for the craving that delights here and there; thus, in dependence on his craving in its latent state and in its threefold obsessive state, in the future the production, the manifestation, of re-existence comes about. Therefore it is said: 'as cause, as arising, as origination, and as condition.' Thus this yogi comprehends the characteristic of the truth of arising by these four aspects. Having comprehended the characteristic of the truth of arising, he regards the cessation of this truth of arising — its remainderless stilling — as cessation; because of the remainderless stilling of the truth of suffering, [Conjectural: as calm]; because it is foremost, best, [Conjectural: unsurpassed], as sublime; because of its permanence and its being the escape, as escape. Thus by four aspects he comprehends the characteristic of the truth of cessation. Having comprehended it, he regards the path — in the sense of seeking what is to be known, in the sense of seeking the actual, in the sense of proceeding through the four [Conjectural: gates], and in the sense of the one way to the attainment of nirvāṇa — as path, as method, as practice, and as deliverance. Thus by four aspects he comprehends the characteristic of the truth of the path. This is called his characteristic-knowing [attention], inwardly and personally, with regard to the four noble truths.

Having thus examined and investigated the personally present aggregates through conditionality, he extends [the analysis] by inference to the aggregates in dissimilar realms that are not directly present: 'They too are of such nature; they too fall under such a method; whatever is conditioned, everywhere, in every way, is of such nature; and its cessation is calm, and the path for its abandoning is deliverance-leading.' When he has truth-knowledge regarding the personally present aggregates just described, and inferential knowledge regarding those not directly present, in dissimilar realms, this [knowledge] stands as the seed for the arising of dharma-knowledge and subsequent knowledge. And this characteristic-knowing attention is to be understood as mixed with hearing and reflection. When this yogi has thus attained certainty in those truths by right investigation, by consequence and the rest, by the sixteen aspects in the four noble truths, namely by proof-establishing reasoning, or by *yāvadbhāvikatā* — then, passing beyond the attention made of hearing and reflection, [Damaged] he resolves exclusively in the mode of cultivation. This is his resolve-born attention, which has the truths as its object and is exclusively concentrated. As a consequence of that, with regard to two truths — the truth of suffering and the truth of arising — he attains unlimited knowledge, by which he resolves upon the impermanent as unlimited: 'impermanent, impermanent'.

Thus [he resolves on] the unlimitedness of suffering, the unlimitedness of the emptiness aspect, the unlimitedness of defilement, the unlimitedness of going to bad destinations, the unlimitedness of prosperity and the unlimitedness of adversity, and the unlimitedness of [Conjectural: old age,] illness, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection and despair. There, 'unlimited' [means]: there is no end to *saṁsāra*; of these dharmas there is no end, no limit. As long as *saṁsāra* goes on, these dharmas are; with the remainderless cessation of *saṁsāra* they cease, and there is no other cessation. Thus, with respect to all becomings, destinies, deaths and births, by the aspect of non-aspiration, by the aspect of non-reliance, and with regard to repugnance — resolving with regard to repugnance, he cultivates the resolve-born attention. Thus, from all becomings and births having stirred the mind and alarmed it, with deepest aspiration he directs it to *nirvāṇa* by one aspect or another. For a long time his mind has delighted in sights, delighted in sounds, smells, tastes and tangibles; it has been fed and built up by sights, sounds, smells, tastes and tangibles. Hence, even when with deepest aspiration he directs it to *nirvāṇa*, it does not leap forward, does not settle into it, does not stand firm, is not released; [Conjectural: it recoils] — the mind. Because the calm element is not yet its aim, and out of anxiety, he again and again stirs and alarms that mind; from the truth of suffering and from the truth of arising he stirs and alarms it, and again and again with deepest aspiration directs it to *nirvāṇa*. Still it does not leap forward. Why? Because the gross 'I am' conceit obstructs direct realization. Having entered into attention, it proceeds intermittently: 'I have transmigrated, I shall transmigrate; I shall attain final *nirvāṇa*; I cultivate wholesome dharmas for final *nirvāṇa*; I see suffering as suffering, arising as arising, cessation as cessation; I see the path as path; I see the empty as empty, the wishless as wishless, the signless as signless; these dharmas are mine.' For that cause and reason the mind does not leap forward, even when with deepest aspiration he directs it to *nirvāṇa*. Quickly penetrating with wisdom that the 'I am' conceit is the obstruction, he drops the attention that has been running on of its own momentum, turns away from the outward object of knowledge, and begins the investigation of the truths that is entered by attention and follows attention. He sees each arisen mind, as it ceases, being observed by the mind that arises immediately after, in the manner of a continuous stream. Thus with mind he takes mind as object and holds it fast, so that the obstructing 'I am' conceit which has entered with attention has [Conjectural: no] opportunity to come into being again.

The yogi thus engaged sees, in the earlier and the later, the difference from moment to moment of that mind-continuum, its ever-newness, its liability to decline, its lasting only a while, its brief presence — this is [his seeing] of its impermanence. That he sees the mind-continuum as entered into the aggregates of grasping — this is of its suffering. That therein the mind apprehends no [other] dharma — this here is of its emptiness. That he sees the same mind-continuum as dependently arisen and not autonomous — this is of its not-self. Thus, first, he has entered the truth of suffering. It occurs to him: 'This mind-continuum of mine too has craving as its cause, arises from craving, originates from craving, has craving as its condition.' 'And the cessation of this mind-continuum too — that is calm.' 'And the path leading to its cessation — that is delivering.' Thus, by investigating with attention what was uninvestigated, with subtle wisdom [Conjectural: he has entered] those noble truths. Through his familiarization and cultivation of this, there arises for him the knowledge in which the object and the apprehending [mind] are evenly balanced. By it, when the gross 'I am' conceit that obstructs delight in *nirvāṇa* becomes active, it is abandoned. And as he directs it to *nirvāṇa* with deepest aspiration, the mind leaps forward; it does not recoil — the mind — out of anxiety; and with deepest aspiration he takes delight. For him so become, the knowledge in which the object and the apprehending [mind] are evenly balanced, accompanied by mild patience, is called warmth (*ūṣmagata*). That embraced by middling patience is called [Conjectural: summit]. That comprised by strong patience is called [Conjectural: truth-conforming] patience.

Having thus abandoned the obstructing 'I am' conceit and taken up delight in *nirvāṇa* with deepest aspiration, that effort of his [Conjectural: of examining the mind] again and again — that effort he relinquishes, and settles the non-conceptual mind in effortlessness. At that time his mind appears as if ceased, and it is not ceased; it appears as if without object, and it is [Conjectural: not] without object. His mind, calmed, appears as if gone away, and it is not gone away. Nor, again, at that time is it as one overcome by [Conjectural: sweet] sleep that the mind appears as if ceased; and it is not ceased. That which, for some dull and deluded ones, becomes a conceit of direct realization — this mind, on the contrary, is for direct realization itself: 'Before long, now, entry into the certainty of rightness will take place.' When such a state of mind comes about, that last-of-all non-conceptual mind of his, immediately after which he directs inward effort to the previously investigated [Conjectural: truths] — those are the supreme worldly dharmas. After that, only supramundane mind arises for him, not mundane. This is the boundary, this the limit, of mundane formations; therefore they are called the supreme worldly dharmas. Immediately after them, the previously investigated truths come [before him]; immediately after [that] effort, in the previous order, in the investigated truths in sequence, perceived and unperceived, non-conceptual

certain knowledge and direct knowledge arise. By their arising, the encumbrance lodged in the basis that belongs to the side of the defilements of the three realms to be abandoned by seeing is abandoned. By its abandoning, if previously he was already free of lust for sense-pleasures, then upon the direct realization of the truths, at that time he is called a non-returner. His signs are to be understood as those very ones stated earlier for the one free of lust. But this is the distinction: he is to be spontaneously born, attaining final *nirvāṇa* there, not returning to this world. If, however, he is for the most part free of lust, upon the direct realization [Conjectural: of the truths] he becomes a once-returner. If, however, he is not free of lust, then, with that encumbrance further tranquillized, he becomes a stream-enterer. Knowledge has come together with the object of knowledge through direct perception; therefore it is called direct realization (*abhisamaya*). As when a kṣatriya, with a kṣatriya, having come face to face, is then said to have come to meet him (*abhisamayāgata*) — so brahmins and the rest are to be understood. These are his signs: four knowledges have been attained by him. As he examines in [Conjectural: all] his attentions, in conduct and in abiding, [he attains] knowledge of mere dharmas, knowledge of non-annihilation, knowledge of non-eternity, and knowledge of dependently arisen formations as like an illusion. And even as he moves among sense-objects, if a very intense obsession of defilement arises through loss of mindfulness, even that goes away very quickly, as soon as he attends to it. Likewise, he is not one who goes to bad destinations; he does not [Conjectural: deliberately] transgress a training rule, nor does he deprive of life a being gone to the animal realm. He does not fall back, renouncing the training. He is incapable of committing the five actions of immediate retribution (*ānantarya*). He does not hold that pleasure and pain are self-made, nor other-made, nor both self-made and other-made, nor arisen without cause, neither self-made nor other-made. He does not seek another teacher outside this [teaching], nor another worthy of offerings. He is not one who looks to the [Conjectural: face] of other śramaṇas and brahmins, nor one who studies their [Conjectural: face]; rather, he has himself seen the dharma, attained the dharma, plunged into the dharma; crossed over doubt, crossed over uncertainty; independent of others, not to be led by another; in the Teacher's teaching he has attained confidence in the dharmas. He does not believe in purity through omens and auspicious rites. Nor does he bring about an eighth existence. He is endowed with the four confidences (*avetyaprasāda*). For him, up to the supreme worldly dharmas, [there is] the resolve-born attention; for one who has directly realized the truths, until the defilements to be abandoned by seeing are abandoned, [there is] the seclusion attention — for their abandoning. After that, as he cultivates and practises the path as attained, through the abandoning of the strong and middling defilements of the desire realm he becomes a once-returner. Of him too, whatever are the signs of the stream-enterer, all are to be understood. And this is the distinction: with respect to sense-objects, in situations for lower defilements, even one that would occasion a strong obsession, he produces only a [Conjectural: weak] obsession of defilement and quickly removes it. Having come once to this world, he makes an end of suffering. The non-returner: the signs of the non-returner are just as stated before. There, the whole path of cultivation [proceeds] by examining again and again, with the examination attention, what has and has not been abandoned, [Conjectural: brought about by practising] the path as attained.

There, what is the nature of cultivation?

What is its action?

What is its distinction by kind?

The practice of these wholesome conditioned dharmas, mundane or supramundane, by an attention belonging to the concentrated plane — their familiarization, their continuous and careful performance, and the mind-continuum's becoming imbued with them — this is called the nature of cultivation. There, cultivation has eight kinds of action: cultivating, he attains some dharmas; some dharmas he familiarizes himself with through cultivation; some dharmas he purifies, some he dispels, some he fully understands; some dharmas he abandons, some he realizes directly, some he distances. There, those superior wholesome dharmas not yet attained — he attains them. Some dharmas attained by cultivation and present — he familiarizes himself with them. There, those that have been attained but are not present: when cultivated through dharmas of their own kind, they are brought into presence in the future and arise purer and more cleansed. There, those defiled dharmas that become active through loss of mindfulness — by the power of the practice of wholesome dharmas he [Conjectural: does not tolerate] them, abandons them, dispels them, makes an end of them. Or, dharmas to be abandoned which have not yet arisen: he fully understands them as a disease and finds fault with them; he fully understands them as a dart, as a boil, as misfortune, as impermanent, as suffering, as empty and as not-self, and finds fault with them. From his practice of full understanding the immediacy path (*ānantaryamārga*) arises for the abandoning of defilements, by which he abandons [them]. And when they are abandoned, he directly realizes liberation. And as he ascends to higher and higher planes, so the abandoned dharmas of the lower planes become remote, up to reaching the end. This is to be understood as the eightfold action of cultivation.

There, an elevenfold distinction by kind is to be understood for cultivation, namely: *śamatha* cultivation and *vipaśyanā* cultivation, as before; there, mundane-path cultivation, supramundane-path cultivation, mild, middling and strong cultivation, preparation-path cultivation, and immediacy-path, liberation-path and distinction-path cultivation. There, *śamatha* cultivation is through the nine-aspect mental abiding, as before. *Vipaśyanā* cultivation, as before. There, mundane-path cultivation is that by which, through seeing the grossness of the lower planes and the calm of the higher planes, [he goes] up to dispassion toward the sphere of nothingness. There, supramundane-path cultivation is [that of one] attending to suffering as suffering, up to attending to the path as path, [and] that which, by the uncontaminated path beginning with right view, [goes] up to dispassion toward the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. There, mild-path cultivation is that by which he abandons the gross, strong defilements. There, middling-path cultivation is that by which he abandons the middling defilements. There, strong-path cultivation is that by which he abandons the mild type of defilement, which is to be abandoned last of all. There, preparation-path cultivation is that by which he begins the preparation for the abandoning of defilements. There, immediacy-path cultivation is that by which he abandons [them]. There, liberation-path cultivation is that by which, immediately after [they are] abandoned, he directly realizes liberation from the defilements. There, distinction-path cultivation is that [by which he proceeds] thereafter, as long as he does not undertake the preparation to be undertaken for the defilements of another plane, or, having reached the end, undertakes none. This is to be understood as the elevenfold distinction by kind of cultivation. For him, thus engaged in cultivation, examining from time to time whether the defilements have been abandoned or not, from time to time stirring the mind with dharmas that should stir it, and from time to time gladdening it with dharmas that should gladden it — this is his delight-gathering attention. From his familiarization, cultivation and repeated practice of that delight-gathering attention, there arises, for the abandoning without remainder of the defilements to be abandoned by cultivation, the last-of-all concentration of a trainee: the diamond-like concentration (*vajropama samādhi*). From its arising, all the defilements to be abandoned by cultivation are abandoned.

For what reason is it called diamond-like?

Just as a diamond, among all other gems — gems, pearls, beryl, conch, stone, coral and the rest — is the most solid and the firmest of all, and cuts the others, but is not cut by other gems, just so this concentration is the foremost of all trainee concentrations, the best, the most solid of all; it overcomes all defilements and is not overcome by defilements arising again. Therefore it is called diamond-like. Immediately after the diamond-like concentration, through the uprooting of the seeds of encumbrance belonging to the side of all the defilements, the mind is [Conjectural: liberated] absolutely, and he attains the purification of the lineage. In the destruction of all encumbrance and defilement, [Conjectural: the knowledge of destruction] arises; and from the destruction of the cause, the knowledge of non-arising arises, for the total non-appearance of suffering in the future. At that time he becomes an arhat: his contaminations destroyed, what was to be done done, his task accomplished, his own goal attained, the fetters of existence destroyed, his mind well liberated through right knowledge. He is endowed with the ten dharmas of a non-trainee: the non-trainee's right view and right intention, up to the non-trainee's right liberation and right knowledge. And he has mastery over his own mind in abiding and in attention: at whatever time he wishes, whether the noble abiding, the divine or the brahmā abiding, in that very one he abides; whichever dharma he wishes to attend to, wholesome and connected with the goal, mundane or supramundane, that very one he attends to.
