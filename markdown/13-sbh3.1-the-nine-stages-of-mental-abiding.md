# The Nine Stages of Mental Abiding

*Śrāvakabhūmi · sbh3.1*

One-pointedness (*ekāgratā*) has both the side of śamatha (calm-abiding) and the side of vipaśyanā (insight). The one-pointedness in the nine-fold continuity or abiding of mind belongs to the śamatha side; the one-pointedness in the four-fold support of wisdom belongs to the vipaśyanā side.

What is the nine-fold mental abiding?

Here a monk places (*sthāpayati*) the mind inwardly, places it continuously (*saṁsthāpayati*), places it again (*avasthāpayati*), places it closely (*upasthāpayati*), tames it (*damayati*), pacifies it (*śamayati*), fully pacifies it (*vyupaśamayati*), makes it single-pointed (*ekotīkaroti*), and concentrates it (*samādhatte*).

How does he place [the mind]?

Withdrawing it from all outer objects, he ties it inwardly toward non-distraction. That first binding [of the mind] against distraction — this is placement.

How does he place it continuously?

That mind first bound is [Conjectural: unsteady], gross, unsettled, and not fully settled. On that very same object, through the practice of strengthening and the practice of clarification, with deliberate restraint — making it subtle and gathering it together — he places it continuously.

How does he place it again?

If, while he is placing the mind, it becomes distracted outwardly through loss of mindfulness, he withdraws it again in just the same way: thus he places it again. [Lacuna]

How does he tame [the mind]?

By those signs which distract his mind — namely the signs of [Conjectural: forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and tangibles], and the signs of passion, hatred, delusion, and of women and men — with regard to these, he has previously grasped the perception of danger; taking that as governing, he does not allow the mind to spread out toward those signs. Thus he tames it.

How does he pacify [the mind]?

By those thoughts — sensual thoughts and the like — and by those secondary defilements — the hindrance of sensual desire and the like — there is agitation of the mind. With regard to these, he has previously grasped the perception of danger; taking that as governing, toward those thoughts and secondary defilements he does not allow [the mind] to spread out — thus he pacifies it.

How does he fully pacify?

When, through loss of mindfulness, both of these become active, he does not tolerate the thoughts and secondary defilements as they arise, one after another; he abandons them. Thus he fully pacifies.

How does he make [the mind] single-pointed?

With effort, he establishes an unbroken, uninterrupted flow of samādhi — thus he makes it single-pointed.

How does he concentrate?

Through familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice, a path proceeding effortlessly, proceeding of its own accord, is what he attains, by which, without effort, without exertion, the flow of his mind's samādhi proceeds in non-distraction. Thus he concentrates it.

The nine-fold mental abiding is accomplished through the six kinds of power — namely the powers of hearing and thinking (*śruta-cintā-bala*), the power of mindfulness (*smṛti-bala*) [Conjectural: and the power of clear comprehension], the power of diligence (*vīrya-bala*), and the power of familiarity (*paricaya-bala*).

First, through the powers of hearing and thinking: taking what he has heard and what he has reflected on as governing, from the very beginning he places the mind on the object; and on that very object, by sustained engagement, he places it continuously. Returning [to it] through the power of mindfulness, he places again the mind bound there and placing it closely. Then through the power of clear comprehension (*saṁprajanya-bala*), not allowing the mind to spread toward distracting signs, thoughts, and secondary defilements, he tames and pacifies. Through the power of diligence, he does not tolerate the occurrence of both of these; [Conjectural: he fully pacifies] and makes [the mind] single-pointed. Through the power of familiarity, he concentrates.

In the nine-fold mental abiding, four [kinds of] attention should be known: forceful attention, interrupted attention, uninterrupted attention, and effortless attention.

In one who is placing and placing continuously, the attention is forceful (*bala-vāhana*). In one who is placing again, placing closely, taming, pacifying, or fully pacifying, the attention is interrupted (*sacchidra-vāhana*). In one who is making single-pointed, the attention is uninterrupted (*niścchidra-vāhana*). In one who is concentrating, the attention is effortless (*anābhoga-vāhana*).

Thus these [four] attentions in mental abiding belong to the śamatha side. But for one who, having thus attained inward śamatha of mind, applies himself to vipaśyanā — for him these very same four attentions belong to the vipaśyanā side.
