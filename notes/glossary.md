# Translation glossary

Controlled English renderings of recurring Sanskrit technical terms. Each entry states the policy, gives concrete examples, and lists adjacent terms that should not be conflated.

## *aśubha* / *aśubhatā* — rendered contextually

The Sanskrit *aśubha* (literally "non-beautiful, inauspicious"; negation of *śubha* "beautiful, auspicious") functions across three semantic registers in the Śrāvakabhūmi's *aśubha-bhāvanā* (principally sbh2.2 and sbh3.2). No single English word covers all three cleanly, so this translation uses three, each bound to *aśubha* via this glossary.

| Context | English | Triggers |
|---|---|---|
| Body-substance contemplation | **impurity / the impure** | 32-parts list (hair, nails, teeth, organs, fluids); references to excrement, urine, saliva, mucus, blood, pus; *pratyaśubhatā* taken **inwardly** |
| Cemetery / decaying body | **repulsive / repulsiveness** | Corpses in stages of decay (discolored, festering, bursting, swollen, gnawed, bloody-skeletal, dispersed, bone, skeleton); *pratyaśubhatā* taken **outwardly** |
| Metaphorical and doctrinal extensions | **unattractive / unattractiveness** | *duḥkhāśubhatā* (of suffering); *kleśāśubhatā* (of defilement); *prabhaṅgurāśubhatā* (of perishability); relative *aśubha* (e.g., jhāna as *aśubha* relative to nirvāṇa); chapter/category headings; any non-corporeal use |

### Rule of thumb

- If the referent is a body part, bodily fluid, or anything that can be excreted, dripped, or extracted — **impurity**.
- If the referent is a corpse or part of a decay process — **repulsiveness**.
- If the referent is abstract (suffering, defilement, perishability, relative ranking) — **unattractiveness**.
- For master-category labels and chapter-level taxonomy headings, default to **unattractiveness** as the neutral umbrella term.

### Heading vs taxonomy wording

Use **"unattractiveness"** for the technical taxonomy noun where the abstract quality itself must be named. In running prose, especially after the heading has established the topic, use **"mode"** to avoid repeating "unattractiveness" mechanically through the sixfold list.

For public-facing page titles or section headings, **"the unattractive"** or **"contemplation of the unattractive"** may read better than bare "unattractiveness." This is an English-style distinction, not a Sanskrit distinction:

- **"unattractiveness"** = the abstract quality/category in the taxonomy
- **"mode"** = a readable shorthand for one member of the sixfold *aśubhatā* taxonomy once the topic has been introduced
- **"the unattractive"** = the meditative object-domain as encountered by the practitioner
- **"contemplation of the unattractive"** = the practice frame, especially when introducing *aśubha-bhāvanā*

Do not use "impurity" as the umbrella heading for sbh2.2 unless the whole chapter is being restricted to body-substance contemplation; the chapter's sixfold taxonomy is broader than bodily impurity.

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *pratyaśubhatā* (inherent unattractiveness, the first member of the sixfold taxonomy) | "inherent mode" / "inherent unattractiveness" |
| `sbh2.2` page title | "The Unattractive" or "Contemplation of the Unattractive" |
| `sbh2.2:1.1` definition question | "What is the unattractive?" |
| `sbh2.2:1.3-1.9` sixfold list | "The unattractive has six modes: the inherent mode, the suffering mode..." |
| *pratyaśubhatā taken inwardly* (32 parts) | "inward inherent impurity" |
| *pratyaśubhatā taken outwardly* (cemetery stages) | "outward inherent repulsiveness" |
| *duḥkhāśubhatā* | "unattractiveness of suffering" |
| *adhamāśubhatā* | "unattractiveness of inferiority" |
| *āpekṣikī aśubhatā* (jhāna as *aśubha* relative to nirvāṇa) | "relative unattractiveness" / "relative mode" |
| *kleśāśubhatā* | "unattractiveness of defilement" |
| *prabhaṅgurāśubhatā* | "unattractiveness of perishability" |

### Why the split

*Aśubha* is etymologically closer to "the non-beautiful / the unattractive" than to either "the foul" or "the impure." The Ñāṇamoli–Bodhi–Deleanu English-Buddhist-Studies convention has settled on "foulness," but that translation imports English connotations of filth and contamination that *aśubha* does not strictly entail. The earlier Wayman/Edgerton tradition used "impurity," which more strictly translates *aśuci* (a different Sanskrit term).

For a Yogācāra meditation manual specifically, *aśubha-bhāvanā* operates across three registers that English vocabulary splits: gross body-impurity (32 parts), gross decay-revulsion (cemetery), and metaphorical/doctrinal unattractiveness. Forcing a single English word across all three produces unidiomatic readings in at least one register; this glossary's split keeps each register idiomatic while binding all three back to the unified Sanskrit term.

### Adjacent terms (not to be conflated)

- *aśuci* — "the unclean" / "uncleanness" (strict cognate of "impurity"; appears in *viparyāsa* contexts as *aśucau śuci-grāha*, "taking the unclean as clean")
- *jugupsanīya* — "disgusting"
- *pratikūla* — "repugnant," "averse to" (often in dietary contexts: *āhāre pratikūla-saṁjñā*, "perception of food as repugnant")
- *durgandha* — "foul-smelling," "stinking"
- *pūti* — "putrid"

A reader who encounters "impurity," "repulsive," or "unattractive" in the English can confirm against the parallel Sanskrit that the underlying term is *aśubha*; encountering "unclean," "disgusting," "repugnant," "foul-smelling," or "putrid" indicates one of the adjacent terms above.

## *ālambana* — "object"

The technical term for the object of cognition or meditation — what the mind takes as its referent. Literal etymology: "support, basis" (the object on which the mind "rests"). Rendered consistently as **"object"**.

In compound forms ending in *-vastu* (e.g., *ālambana-vastu*), the *-vastu* is technical Sanskrit lexical filler meaning "thing / entity" and does not need to be calqued. *Ālambana-vastu* is rendered simply as **"object"**, or **"object referent"** where precision is needed to distinguish the cognitive referent from the act of cognition.

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *ālambana* | object |
| *ālambana-vastu* | object (or "object referent") |
| *ālambana-pariśuddhi* | purity of object |
| *bāhyālambana* | outer object |
| *ālambana-nimitta* | object-sign |
| *ālambana-smṛtyupasthāna* | object-foundation (of mindfulness) |

### Why not "support"

"Support" is the literal etymology but obscures the term for English readers: "the support of the meditation" parses as the *practice's* support rather than the *cognition's* object. "Object" is unambiguous in meditation context.

## *abhinirhāra* — "manifestation" / "bringing forth"

The Sanskrit *abhinirhāra* means "producing, bringing forth, manifesting" — the practitioner's deliberate production of specific dharmas, psychic states, or higher knowledges. Rendered as **"manifestation"** in nominal contexts and **"bringing forth"** when the verbal force is dominant.

**Avoid "projection"**, which carries unintended English connotations (defense mechanism, displacement). *Abhinirhāra* does not connote distortion; it connotes deliberate cultivation-of-result.

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *abhijñā-abhinirhāra* | manifestation of higher knowledges |
| *ṛddhi-abhinirhāra* | manifestation of psychic power |
| (verbal) *...abhinirharati* | brings forth... |

## *āyatana* — context-dependent

Sanskrit *āyatana* (from *ā- + yat-* "to stretch toward, to reach to") literally "locus, abode, resting-place, sphere of activity." Polysemous in the Yogācāra corpus across several distinct technical registers; rendered contextually rather than uniformly.

| Context | English | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Geographical locus** — the region where dharma transmission is alive | **"region"** | *ārya-āyatana* "noble region" (sbh1.1:1.7); paralleled by *madhya-janapada* "Middle Country" (1.8) |
| **Doctrinal locus** — the proper object/domain in which faith or clarity lodges | **"sphere"** | *āyatana-gata prasāda* "clarity directed to its sphere" (sbh1.1:1.13); the *āyatana* there is the dharma-vinaya itself (1.16) |
| **Abhidharmic sense-base** — the twelve *āyatanas* (six internal + six external) of perception | **"sense-base"** | The twelve-fold list in Abhidharma analysis; appears in the *skandha-āyatana-dhātu* triad and in later chapters' analytical lists |
| **Meditation locus** — the object-domain or stationing-place of meditation | **"basis"** / **"object-basis"** | Compounds like *ālambana-āyatana*, *karmasthāna-āyatana* (meditation supports) |

### Why split

A single English rendering for *āyatana* across these contexts would either over-technicalize all uses (e.g., "sense-base" everywhere collapses geographical and doctrinal senses) or generalize them into mush ("sphere" everywhere reads vague and masks the abhidharmic technical sense). The contextual split keeps each use idiomatic while binding all back to the unified Sanskrit lexeme. Where two senses appear in close proximity (e.g., the two *āyatana*s in the *ātmasampat* list at sbh1.1), inline comments flag the distinction so the parallel reader can see the same word doing different work.

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *ārya-āyatana* (sbh1.1:1.7) | noble region |
| *āyatana-gata prasāda* (sbh1.1:1.13) | clarity directed to its sphere |
| *tad āyatanaṁ tathāgata-pravedito dharma-vinayaḥ* (sbh1.1:1.16) | "that sphere is the dharma-vinaya proclaimed by the Tathāgata" |
| *ṣaḍ-āyatana* (the six sense-bases in dependent origination) | the six sense-bases |
| *bāhya-āyatana* / *adhyātma-āyatana* | external sense-base / internal sense-base |
| *ālambana-āyatana* | object-basis (of meditation) |
| *kāyāyatana*, *cakṣur-āyatana*, etc. (in abhidharmic lists) | body sense-base, eye sense-base |

### Adjacent terms (not to be conflated)

- *janapada* — **"country"** / **"region"**, with *madhya-janapada* as **"Middle Country"** (the Indian-Buddhist concept of the dharma-active heartland) and *pratyanta-janapada* as **"peripheral country"**
- *gati* — **"resort" / "path/destination"** (in *yatra gatiḥ satpuruṣāṇām* at sbh1.1:1.8, "wherever the resort of holy persons lies")
- *ālambana* — **"object"** (the cognitive referent the mind takes; see entry above)
- *vastu* — **"object" / "thing" / "referent"** (broader category-term; see entry on *kṣaya-vastu*)
- *dhātu* — **"element"** (the eighteen *dhātus* extend the twelve *āyatanas* by adding the six consciousnesses)
- *bhūmi* — **"stage" / "ground"** (the work's structural divisions: *gotrabhūmi*, *avatārabhūmi*, etc.; a different "locus" word)

## *audārika* / *audārikatā* — "gross" / "grossness"

The Sanskrit *audārika* (adjective) and *audārikatā* (noun) name the gross-perceptual quality of lower planes and unrefined mental states, contrasted with *śānta* ("calm") for higher planes. Rendered as **"gross"** (adj.) and **"grossness"** (n.).

This is an opinionated meditation-manual choice. The more technical English equivalent is "coarse / coarseness," and that rendering has precedent in academic Buddhist Studies. But the modern meditation-teaching tradition (Pa-Auk, Mahāsi, contemporary jhāna manuals, *The Mind Illuminated*, etc.) consistently uses **"gross"** for this distinction, and this translation follows that convention to keep the practice-language familiar to readers from those traditions.

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *audārika* (adj.) | gross |
| *audārikatā* (n.) | grossness |
| *audārika-śānta* (the contrast pair) | gross / calm |
| *svabhāva-audārika* | grossness in essential nature |
| *saṁkhyā-audārika* | grossness in number/quantity |
| *audārika-lakṣaṇa* | gross characteristic |

### Adjacent terms (not to be conflated)

- *śānta* — **"calm"** (the paired term in the gross/calm contrast that drives the laukika-mārga)
- *dauṣṭhulya* — **"encumbrance"** (see dedicated entry below); a different opposition pair from gross/calm.

## *bhūtatā* / *tathatā* / *tathābhūtatā*

Three closely related Yogācāra terms commonly conflated in English. Distinguished here:

- ***bhūtatā*** — **"actuality"**. The object's having-become / actual-character; that it *is* what it is. From *bhū-* "to be / become" + *-tā* abstract.
- ***tathatā*** — **"suchness"**. The canonical Yogācāra term for ultimate reality / how-things-ultimately-are. The proper object of the four *yukti*.
- ***tathābhūtatā*** — **"such-actuality"**. Compound; the actuality-of-things-as-they-are. Treated as the *dharmatā-yukti* object at sbh3.2:5.37, where it appears alongside *prasiddha-dharmatā*, *acintya-dharmatā*, *avasthita-dharmatā* as synonyms for "the way things are."

### Why the distinction matters

In sbh2.1:7.7–7.10, *yathāvad-bhāvikatā* (the "as-it-actually-is-ness" of the meditation object) is defined as the dual of *bhūtatā* + *tathatā*:

> The actuality [*bhūtatā*] of the object, and its suchness [*tathatā*], endowed with the four reasonings — its reasoning-endowed-ness.

The two-term structure is doctrinally load-bearing: the object has both an *actuality* (its specific character, what it *is*) and a *suchness* (its ultimate-reality character, accessed through *yukti*). Collapsing them as two English synonyms for the same thing — as the previous "thusness [tathatā]" / "suchness" rendering did — flattens the Yogācāra epistemology.

### Adjacent terms (not to be conflated)

- ***yathābhūta*** (adv./adj.) — **"as-it-actually-is"**. A different syntactic category from the abstract nouns above; modifies verbs of seeing/knowing (*yathābhūta-jñāna-darśana* "knowing-and-seeing as-it-actually-is"). Already consistently rendered.
- ***dharmatā*** — "nature of things" / "nature-of-things" (the term that anchors *dharmatā-yukti*; appears in compounds like *prasiddha-dharmatā* "established nature of things")

## *dauṣṭhulya* — "encumbrance"

Yogācāra technical term for the body-mind baggage the meditator brings to practice — the residual unwholesome weight that persists beneath surface-level *kleśa*-abandonment, seed-like in its latent potential to re-manifest defilements. Rendered as **"encumbrance"**.

The opposite is *praśrabdhi* (pliancy, tranquillity): as *dauṣṭhulya* dissolves, *praśrabdhi* arises. The pair *dauṣṭhulya-sahagata* ↔ *praśrabdhi-sahagata* is the diagnostic for the term's meaning.

### Why "encumbrance" and not "hindrance"

"Hindrance" is the established English rendering for *nivaraṇa* (the five hindrances) and is also used for *āvaraṇa* in this translation. Adding *dauṣṭhulya* → "hindrance" would conflate three distinct Yogācāra technical categories under a single English term.

The English word that most closely matches *dauṣṭhulya*'s semantics — the body-mind *baggage* the meditator brings to practice — is "baggage" itself, but compounds like "body-baggage" break down. **"Encumbrance"** is the closest formal synonym to "baggage" that compounds cleanly: from Latin *in- + cumbrus* "a load," literally "something carried that weighs you down."

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *dauṣṭhulya* | encumbrance |
| *kāya-dauṣṭhulya* | body-encumbrance |
| *citta-dauṣṭhulya* | mind-encumbrance |
| *dauṣṭhulya-sahagata* | accompanied by encumbrance |
| *dauṣṭhulya-bīja* | encumbrance-seed |
| *dauṣṭhulya-sanniśraya* | encumbrance-substrate |
| *dauṣṭhulya-apagama* | dissipation of encumbrance |
| *dauṣṭhulya-pratipraśrabdhi* | tranquillization of encumbrance |

### Adjacent terms (not to be conflated)

- *nivaraṇa* — **"hindrance"** (the five hindrances: sense-desire, ill-will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-regret, doubt; doctrinally distinct list)
- *anuśaya* — **"latent tendencies"** (related but distinct: seed-form proclivities of *kleśa*; some prior renderings of *dauṣṭhulya* mistakenly used "latent corruption" / "latent tendencies" — corrected 2026-05-15)
- *praśrabdhi* — **"pliancy" / "tranquillity"** (the antonym; the dissolution of *dauṣṭhulya*)
- *āvaraṇa* — **"obstruction"** / **"hindrance"** in some contexts; another distinct category

## *kṣaya-vastu* — "cessation event"

Sanskrit *kṣaya* "destruction, exhaustion" + *vastu* "substance, object, referent." In the *samāhita-bhūmi* (concentration-plane) context of sbh3.0:2.25, where the practitioner cultivates a *sabhāga-ālambana* ("conformable-object") as a representation (*pratirūpaka*) of the *kṣaya-vastu*, the term names the **experiential cessation event** that the meditation is preparing for: the moment when the mind becomes truly still and habitual subconscious-to-conscious cognitive activity drops away.

Rendered as **"cessation event"** (with bracketed Sanskrit `[kṣaya-vastu]` to flag the interpretive choice). The samādhi-image is "conformable" because it serves as a foretaste of cessation — the meditator stabilizes the image, becomes familiar with the still-mind quality it points toward, and eventually the actual event arrives.

### Why not "object of destruction" / "thing-of-destruction"

"Object of destruction" misreads the construction. *Kṣaya-vastu* is not what gets destroyed (defilements would be the *object* of destruction); it is the *referent* (vastu) of the term *kṣaya* — i.e., the cessation event itself. The earlier rendering "thing-of-destruction" both mistranslates the relationship and reads as wooden English calque.

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *kṣaya-vastu* | cessation event |
| *kṣayasya vastunaḥ pratirūpakam* | likeness of the cessation event |
| *sabhāga-ālambana* | conformable-object (the meditation-image that resembles the cessation event) |

### Adjacent terms (not to be conflated)

- *pratirūpaka* — **"likeness" / "representation"**. Names the representation-relationship between the meditation-image and its referent. Compare [sbh2.1:14.8](sbh2.1.html#sbh2.1:14.8) *tat-pratirūpakam asyotpadyate* "a representation of it arises."
- *nirodha* — **"cessation"** (related but distinct technical term, more commonly used for *nirodha-samāpatti* / cessation-attainment as a meditative state-class; *kṣaya* tends to emphasize the destruction-of-defilements pole).
- *kleśa-kṣaya* / *āsrava-kṣaya* — "destruction of defilements" / "destruction of fluxes." Standard compounds where *kṣaya* carries its primary "destruction" meaning. *Kṣaya-vastu* at sbh3.0:2.25 is the cessation-event-referent of that destruction.

## *manuṣyatva* — retained untranslated

Sanskrit *manuṣya* "human" + *-tva* abstract suffix; literally "human-ness." In the Śrāvakabhūmi's *ātmasampat* list (the five endowments-from-self at sbh1.1:1.2), Asaṅga defines *manuṣyatva* not as bare humanness but as a **compound condition**: rebirth among humans **plus** male body with the male sex-faculty (*puruṣendriya*).

Rendered consistently as ***manuṣyatva*** (italicized, Sanskrit retained) in the running translation, with a parenthetical gloss "(human-male birth)" on the first occurrence and on chapter-initial reintroductions.

### Why retain the Sanskrit

Translating *manuṣyatva* as "being human" creates a tautological collision with the male-faculty clause: "what is being human? being reborn human and being male" reads as restricting humanness to males rather than naming a technical compound condition. Retaining the Sanskrit makes the compound visible: *manuṣyatva* is a technical category-label, not a definition of *Homo sapiens*. The reader can see *puruṣa* (male, social-biological) sitting next to *puruṣendriya* (male sex-faculty, abhidharmic) in the parallel Sanskrit pane and understand the structural move.

### Why the male-faculty condition

The Śrāvakabhūmi is a *bhikṣu* (monk) manual. *Manuṣyatva* as the first *ātmasampat* condition names male rebirth specifically because the work is about preconditions for the monk's path; the *bhikṣuṇī* (nun) lineage has its own Vinaya and its own *bhūmi*-style treatment elsewhere in the Yogācāra and broader Buddhist scholastic corpus. The framing parallels Abhidharmakośa usage and is a stock list, not Asaṅga's editorial position.

### Worked examples

| Sanskrit | English |
|---|---|
| *manuṣyatva* | ***manuṣyatva*** (gloss "human-male birth" on first use) |
| *manuṣyāṇāṁ sabhāgatāyāṁ pratyājāta* | "reborn in the community of humans" |
| *puruṣa* | "male" (social-biological category) |
| *puruṣendriya* | "male faculty" (the abhidharmic *indriya*) |
| *puruṣaḥ puruṣendriyeṇa samanvāgataḥ* | "is male, endowed with the male faculty" |

### Adjacent terms (not to be conflated)

- *manuṣya* — "human" (the gender-neutral noun; pluralized in *manuṣyāṇāṁ sabhāgatāyāṁ* "community of humans" at 1.4, where the term is genuinely gender-neutral)
- *puruṣa* — "male" / "man" (the social-biological category; appears in the *ātmasampat* clause and in compounds like *satpuruṣa* "true person, holy person" where the gendered force is conventional rather than restrictive)
- *strī* / *strīndriya* — "female" / "female faculty" (the paired *indriya* not listed here because the manual is *bhikṣu*-scoped)
- *ātmabhāva* — "individual existence" (the body-and-mind continuum that *ātmasampat* qualifies; see comment at 1.22)
- *ātmasampat* — "endowment-from-self" (the umbrella category; five members listed at 1.2)

## *saṁskāra* — "formations," contextually "body-conditioner"

The Sanskrit *saṁskāra* is polysemous. Default rendering: **"formations"** for the technical/aggregate sense. In *kāya-saṁskāra* (the breath as conditioner-of-body in the third ānāpāna tetrad), rendered as **"body-conditioner"** to keep the practice meaning explicit.

### When to use which

| Compound / Sense | English |
|---|---|
| *saṁskāra-skandha* (4th aggregate) | formations / formations-aggregate |
| *saṁskāra* (2nd link of dependent origination) | formation |
| *saṁskāra-duḥkhatā* | suffering of conditioning |
| *kāya-saṁskāra* (3rd ānāpāna tetrad) | **body-conditioner** |
| *abhisaṁskāra* / *anabhisaṁskāra* (9-stage śamatha) | with effort / without effort |

### Why "body-conditioner" for *kāya-saṁskāra*

In MN 118 and the Yogācāra ānāpāna treatment, breath is named *kāya-saṁskāra* because it is what conditions and regulates the body. Rendering this as "body-formation" obscures the function; "body-conditioner [i.e., breath]" makes the agentive role explicit, which matters in practice (the third tetrad is about tranquillizing the breath *as the way of* tranquillizing the body).

## Apparatus markers

Bracketed markers at the start of an English segment signal source-state uncertainty. Default (unmarked) segments are treated as secure translations of intact source. The marker convention is **source state first, translator's response second** — `[Damaged]` names the source state; `/Conjectural` is added when the translator's response goes beyond showing the gap to actively reconstructing the reading.

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `[Damaged]` | Sanskrit source is unusable as printed — lacuna, OCR/extraction garbling, or otherwise unrecoverable. English shows the gap fragmentarily. |
| `[Damaged/Conjectural]` | Source is damaged, but enough context (or parallel material) supports a coherent English reconstruction. The smooth English is a best-guess restoration, not a secure reading. |
| `[Conjectural]` | Source is intact but lexically or syntactically uncertain (e.g., hapax legomena, ambiguous compounds). English is the translator's best reading among several possibilities. |

These markers always appear as a bracketed prefix at the start of the segment. Use is conservative: secure passages stay unmarked; markers appear only when the source genuinely warrants doubt.

The system distinguishes along two axes:

- **Source state**: *Damaged* (unusable — lacunose or garbled) vs unmarked (sound).
- **Translation state**: *Conjectural* (reconstructed or interpretive) vs unmarked (fragmentary if source is damaged, secure if source is sound).

The most important distinction is between `[Damaged]` (visibly fragmentary English — the reader sees the damage) and `[Damaged/Conjectural]` (coherent reconstructed English over damaged source — would otherwise read as secure without the marker).

The lacunose-vs-garbled subdivision of "Damaged" is a translator-side distinction handled in comment notes when relevant, not in the reader-facing marker.
