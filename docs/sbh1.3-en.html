The stage of renunciation (naiṣkramya), the longest section of the first yogasthāna. The text defines it as going to dispassion by the worldly path, through the dhyānas and the formless attainments (1.6–14), and by the supramundane path, through knowledge of the four truths and cultivation of the path (1.15–22), together with the requisites (saṁbhāra) for both (1.5). The body of the section (2.1–80.104) treats the requisites one by one, from restraint of virtue to the ornament of the recluse; summary verses and the colophon of the first yogasthāna close it (81.1–86.1).

The Stage of Renunciation

What is the stage of renunciation?

Answer: the going to dispassion by the worldly path, the going to dispassion by the supramundane path, and the requisites for these two — gathered into one, this is called the stage of renunciation.

What is going to dispassion by the worldly path?

When someone here sees grossness in the realm of desire, and sees calm in the seclusion-born joy and pleasure of the first dhyāna, both as attainment and as rebirth: so seeing, abiding much in that, he attains dispassion [Conjectural: from the realm of desire], and attains the first dhyāna. So, above each dhyāna, he becomes a seer of grossness in all the lower planes and a seer of calm in all the upper planes; so seeing, abiding much in that, he attains dispassion all the way up to the sphere of nothingness, And he attains the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. The going to dispassion by the worldly path goes no higher, no further than this.

What is the going to dispassion by the supramundane path?

When someone here, one who frequents good persons, learned in the noble dharmas, knows suffering as suffering as it actually is, origin as origin, cessation as cessation, path as path, endowed with the trainee’s knowing-and-vision; thereafter, further cultivating the path, he disjoins and releases himself from the dharmas of the three realms — those to be abandoned by [the path of] vision and [the path of] cultivation — and thus passes beyond the three realms. This is called going to dispassion by the supramundane path.

What, then, are the requisites?

Namely: endowment-from-self, endowment-from-others, wholesome dharma-desire, restraint of virtue, [Lacuna] knowing the right measure in food, application to wakefulness in the early and late watches of the night, dwelling with full awareness, association with good friends, hearing-and-reflection on the true dharma, [practice] without obstacle, generosity, the ornament of the recluse — these dharmas are called the requisites for going to worldly and supramundane dispassion. Of these, endowment-from-self, endowment-from-others, and wholesome dharma-desire — their division is to be understood as before, namely as stated [above] regarding the lesser supporting condition for the perfecting of the seed.

What is restraint of virtue?

When someone here dwells endowed with virtue, [and so on] up to ‘having undertaken them, he trains in the precepts of training’. And how does he dwell endowed with virtue, [and so on] up to ‘having undertaken them, he trains in the precepts of training’?

How does he dwell endowed with virtue?

When, in the precepts undertaken, his bodily action is unimpaired and his [Conjectural: verbal] action unimpaired, his conduct unbroken, his conduct unrent — thus is he endowed with virtue.

How is he restrained by the restraint of the Prātimokṣa?

The sevenfold leading-out virtue is called the restraint of the Prātimokṣa. These, by the division of assemblies, become many restraints. Here, however, taking the monastic (bhikṣu) restraint specifically, [the text] says ‘restrained by the restraint of the Prātimokṣa.’

How is he endowed with proper conduct?

When, with respect to deportment, duties-to-be-performed, or application to the wholesome side, he is endowed with conduct that conforms to the world and does not transgress the world, conforms to the Vinaya and does not transgress the Vinaya. [First,] conduct grounded in deportment.

How does he avoid transgressing the world and avoid transgressing the Vinaya [in this respect]?

He walks where one should walk, and walks as one should walk — [walking] in such a way that he is not blamed by the world, by the good, by the properly engaged, by good persons, by his co-religionists, by the Vinaya-holders, by those trained in Vinaya; he is not censurable, not in a position to be reproached. As with walking, so with standing, sitting, and lying down — [the same applies]. [Second,] conduct grounded in duties-to-be-performed. How does he avoid transgressing the world, [how does he avoid] transgressing the Vinaya? — [These] are called the duties-to-be-performed: putting on the robe, disposing of excrement and urine, [the use of] water and the tooth-stick, entering the village, taking and bringing back alms-food, eating, the cleaning and putting away of the bowl, the washing of the feet, the laying out of the lodging — and, in summary, bowl-work and robe-work. Whatever else is of this kind is called a duty-to-be-performed. These, as appropriate — wherever they are to be performed and however they are to be performed — he performs there and in that manner, such that he is not criticized by worldlings, not censured; by the Vinaya-holders and those trained in Vinaya, he is not blameworthy, not censured; by the properly engaged co-religionists [likewise]. Thus conduct grounded in duties-to-be-performed is conduct that does not transgress the world and does not transgress the Vinaya.

[Third,] conduct grounded in application to the wholesome side.

How is this non-transgressive of the world and non-transgressive of the Vinaya?

The wholesome side is said to be: recitation, respectful acts toward and attendance on teachers, looking after the sick, mutually arousing compassionate mind, engagement in giving consent (chandadāna), inquiry and hearing of Dharma by one adroit and not lazy, performing bodily service for wise co-religionists, rousing others to the wholesome side, teaching Dharma, entering retreat, sitting cross-legged — these and other such dharmas are called application to the wholesome side. He, accordingly — as has been proclaimed, wherever it is to be performed — performs it in just that way, such that he is not blameworthy, not censured by worldlings, by Vinaya-holders, by those trained in Vinaya, by the properly engaged, by good persons, or by his co-religionists. This is called conduct grounded in application to the wholesome side, [conduct] non-transgressive of world and Vinaya. Conduct complete in these modes is called completeness of conduct; Thus he is endowed with proper conduct.

And how is he endowed with proper resort?

Five are the non-resorts for a monk.

What five?

A cowherd settlement, a prostitute’s house, a tavern, the royal household, and fifth, the households of caṇḍālas and kaṭhinas. He who avoids these non-resorts rejected by the Tathāgata, and who at the right time frequents elsewhere — the blameless resort — is endowed with proper resort.

How does he see danger in the slightest fault?

‘Slightest fault’ is said [of this]: the minor and lesser precepts of training, in which transgression and rising-out are recognized — transgression of those is the slightest fault. Again, since rising-out from such transgression is accomplished with little difficulty, it is therefore called slightest.

How, then, does he see danger [in it]?

He reflects: ‘On account of this transgression I might become unfit for attaining what has not yet been attained, for realizing what has not yet been realized, for directly realizing what has not yet been directly realized; I might go to the lower realms, might become one bound for the lower realms; I would reproach myself, or the Teacher would reproach me, or the deities, or wise co-religionists would censure me in accordance with Dharma; and in all directions and intermediate directions an evil report, repute, word and verse about me would arise’ — he sees danger in these [Conjectural: disagreeable] dharmas, present and future, that have that [transgression] as their cause. By this he does not [Conjectural: deliberately] transgress even those minor and lesser precepts of training, even for the sake of life. If at some time, on some occasion, through forgetfulness he transgresses, he quickly, very quickly makes amends according to Dharma and rises out [of the transgression]. Thus he sees danger in the slightest fault.

How does he train by undertaking the precepts of training?

Answer: Previously, when receiving the restraint of the Prātimokṣa by the formal act of a motion and three announcements at his full ordination, he heard the formulation of only a few precepts; the remaining precepts, over [Conjectural: two] hundred, set forth in the Prātimokṣa-sūtra, he took on by pledge alone, [saying] ‘I shall acquire them in all respects.’ Having heard from his preceptor and instructor, from those he talks with, converses with, is acquainted with and is dear to, and from the half-monthly recitation of the Prātimokṣa-sūtra, and thereby, from the undertaking of all the trainings, the restraint of the Prātimokṣa is obtained. Thereafter, the precepts of training in which he is skilled he does not transgress, and if he has transgressed he makes amends according to Dharma. But as for those precepts of training in which he is [Conjectural: not yet] skilled, his understanding not yet developed: those precepts previously undertaken by way of pledge he now undertakes through developed skill [in their actual content], [doing so] on the basis previously proclaimed, [namely] from the preceptor or instructor, as before. And having thus undertaken [them] through developed skill, he trains as instructed, neither lessening nor adding. In the precepts of training taught by those who stand in a teacher’s place, he grasps both meaning and letter undistortedly. Thus he trains by undertaking the precepts of training. — Such, in this much, is the detailed division of the restraint of virtue.

What, then, is the summary meaning?

This summary meaning has just three marks. [That is,] the aggregate of virtue is elucidated by the Blessed One under three marks: the mark of non-loss, the mark of own-nature, and the mark of own-nature-with-its-qualities. How so? — In that he says ‘he dwells endowed with virtue,’ by this the non-loss mark of the restraint of virtue is declared.

In that he says ‘restrained by the restraint of the Prātimokṣa,’ by this the own-nature mark is declared. Again, in that he says ‘endowed with proper conduct and proper resort,’ by this — with reference to others — the quality-mark of the restraint of the Prātimokṣa as undertaken is declared. For when others observe his fullness of conduct and resort, those without confidence gain confidence, and those already confident become still more so; those with confidence perform acts grounded in confidence. And they do not defile their minds, they do not send forth blame. Otherwise, for one endowed with virtue, conduct and resort, this quality and benefit dependent on others would [not] be; and in the reverse case there would be fault. Again, in that he says ‘seeing danger in the slightest faults, having undertaken them, he trains in the precepts of training,’ by this the mark of the quality-and-benefit dependent on oneself is declared. For what reason? [Although] this person, endowed with proper conduct and resort, would gain the quality-and-benefit dependent on others, yet, having ruined virtue, by that cause, by that condition he is reborn in the lower realms, or becomes unfit for the attainment of the not-yet-attained, [as] above. Now since he sees danger in the slightest fault — still more in the major [faults] — and trains by undertaking the precepts of training, by that cause, by that condition, at the breaking up of the body he is reborn in a happy destiny, or becomes fit for the attainment of the not-yet-attained, [as] above. For this reason [the foregoing] is called the quality-and-benefit of the restraint of virtue dependent on oneself.

Another mode [of summary]: in brief, the Blessed One has elucidated [under three rubrics] undertaken-virtue, leading-out virtue, and the cultivation of virtue. In that he says, ‘he dwells endowed with virtue,’ by this undertaken-virtue is declared. Again, in that he says, ‘restrained by the restraint of the Prātimokṣa,’ by this leading-out virtue (nairyāṇika) is declared. For the virtue subsumed under the restraint of the Prātimokṣa is called the training in higher virtue (adhiśīla). And on the basis of the training in higher virtue, he cultivates the training in higher mind (adhicitta) and the training in higher wisdom (adhiprajñā) — and thus, for the destruction of all suffering, he is led out. That is, on the basis of higher virtue, taking it as forerunner — therefore the restraint of the Prātimokṣa is called leading-out virtue. Again, in that he says, ‘endowed with proper conduct and resort, seeing danger in the slightest fault, having undertaken them, he trains in the precepts of training’ — by this the cultivation of virtue is declared. By these modes that virtue of the restraint of the Prātimokṣa is to be cultivated, and so cultivated, it becomes well cultivated. Thus this single restraint of virtue is to be understood as set forth by a six-mode teaching.

This restraint of virtue is to be understood as deficient by ten modes, and conversely, by ten causes, [as] perfected.

By what ten causes is it deficient?

It is ill-grasped from the very beginning; it is too slack; it is too overreaching; it is gripped by heedlessness and indolence; it is wrongly resolved; it is gripped by deficiency of conduct; it is gripped by deficiency of livelihood; it is fallen into the two extremes; it is not leading out; and it is fallen from the undertaking. Here, how is virtue ill-grasped from the very beginning? When someone here goes forth under pressure from a king, or under pressure from thieves, or distressed by debt, or distressed by fear, or in fear for his livelihood — not for the sake of recluseship, not for the sake of brahminhood (brāhmaṇya), not for the calming of self, not for the restraint of self, not for the final nirvāṇa of self — thus from the very beginning virtue is ill-grasped.

How is it excessively slack?

When someone here is shameless, with weak remorse, loose, doing loosely what is taught in the training — this is excessive slackness.

How is it too overreaching?

When someone here is an ill-grasper, feeling regret in the wrong place — feeling regret in cases where [Conjectural: no regret] is called for; in the wrong place he produces toward others a contemptuous mind or [Conjectural: a malicious] one, and admonishes them. Thus it is too overreaching.

How is it gripped by heedlessness and indolence?

When someone here, with respect to the past, has fallen into transgression, and some of it, through forgetfulness, he has not atoned for according to Dharma; as with the past, so with the future and the present — whatever transgression he has fallen into, and some of it, through forgetfulness, is not atoned for according to Dharma; and he does not, in advance, undertake intense effort for the sake of non-transgression in the future, [resolving] ‘Let me act in such a way that, acting and dwelling [so], I would not commit transgression’ — yet so he acts, so he dwells, such that he commits transgression. Endowed with this heedlessness connected with the past limit, the future limit, and the middle limit — along with heedlessness about what should have been done earlier — he holds dear the pleasure of sleep, lying down, and resting on the side; he becomes unskilled, lazy, lacking energy [to rise up]; he does not perform bodily service for his wise co-religionists. Thus engaged, he is gripped by heedlessness and indolence.

How is it wrongly resolved?

When someone here lives the holy life having resolved: ‘By this virtue, observance, austerity, holy life, I shall become a god or some [other] kind of divine being,’ desirous of gain and honor. He seeks gain and honor from others; he yearns for gain and honor. Thus it is wrongly resolved.

How is it gripped by deficiency of conduct?

When someone here, having undertaken either deportment, or duties-to-be-performed, or application to the wholesome side, transgresses the world and transgresses the Vinaya, [as detailed] above — thus it is gripped by deficiency of conduct.

How is it gripped by deficiency of livelihood?

When someone here has great desires, is discontented, [Conjectural: hard to feed, and hard to support], and seeks robes by non-Dharma, not by Dharma — and seeks alms-food, lodging, medicine, [and other] requisites by non-Dharma, not by Dharma; and for the sake of robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, [and other] requisites — for the sake of generating regard [for himself in others] — he displays to others a contrived, deliberately composed deportment: non-restless faculties, non-fickle faculties, calm faculties, by which others, having generated regard for his qualities, think [that things] should be given, [things] should be done — namely, robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, [and other] requisites. He becomes clamorous, talkative, brazen, affected, parading his name and clan; he is much-learned, a Dharma-holder, and from a gain-motive alone he speaks Dharma to others — what was spoken by the disciples — or his own qualities, real or somewhat exaggerated, he himself proclaims. Or he has others say it, or shows it by hints; or, wanting a robe or some other recluse’s requisite, much or finer, though not in want he displays a shabby robe, [thinking]: ‘Faithful brāhmaṇas and householders, noticing his want of a robe, will think that a plentiful and fine robe should be given.’ As with the robe, so with any other recluse’s requisite — when he does not obtain it at will from faithful brāhmaṇas and householders, or when [their] possessions are lacking or absent — by importunity he begs, pressing them and speaking harshly. Or, having obtained an inferior thing when possessions are present, he derogates that gain, depreciates it, and in front of the giver-patron speaks thus: ‘Ho, son of good family, there are sons and daughters of good family who, compared with you, are lower in clan and poorer, and yet give thus and so, fine and pleasing gifts. Why do you, higher in clan than they and [Conjectural: richer], give what is not pleasing, not fine, and meager?’ He who by such modes — relying on hypocrisy (kuhanā), on flattery (lapanā), on insinuation (naimittikatā), on extortion (naiṣpeṣikatā), on gain-seeking-by-gain (lābhena lābhaṁ niścikīrṣā) — seeks robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, [and other] requisites from others, he [does so] by non-Dharma — and what is by non-Dharma is for him wrong livelihood. Thus that virtue is gripped by deficiency of livelihood.

How is it fallen into the two extremes?

When someone here is bent on the pleasure of sense-desires, supremely attached to them. He uses the robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, [and other] requisites — obtained either by Dharma or by non-Dharma — without seeing the danger, without knowing the escape. This is called one extreme. And again, here someone is bent on self-mortification; in manifold ways he scorches and torments himself. He undertakes severe vows: one whose resort is thorns, an ash-taker, one whose resort is the pestle, one whose resort is the plank, one who stays in a squat, applied to the yoga of exertion while squatting, a fire-attendant — up to three times [a day] he tends the fire; a water-immerser — up to three times [a day] he immerses in water; standing on one foot, he turns with the sun. Such, or any other such kind of application to self-mortification — this is called the second extreme. Thus it is fallen into the two extremes.

How is it not leading out?

When someone here clings to virtue or observance by [wrong] view: ‘By this very virtue or observance there will be purification, release, leading out.’ All the virtue of those outside — even well-guarded, even well-purified — with such purity is [still] called not leading out. Thus it is not leading out.

How is it fallen from the undertaking?

When someone here is wholly, entirely shameless, indifferent to recluseship, and he is of evil virtue, of evil dharma, inwardly rotten, oozing, become as refuse, of conch-sound conduct, not a recluse but claiming to be a recluse, not a celibate but claiming to be celibate — thus it is fallen from the undertaking. By these ten causes virtue is deficient — this is called by the Blessed One the deficiency of virtue. Moreover, the Blessed One also spoke of [Conjectural: the calamity] of virtue. And that is to be understood from two of these causes: not-leading-out and falling from the undertaking; by the other causes only deficiency of virtue is to be understood. From these very causes set on the dark side, by their reverse — by the bright-side causes — the fullness of virtue is to be understood, and [likewise] the purification of virtue. In some places, again, the Blessed One speaks of virtue by the word ‘root.’ As said: ‘He is one whose root is well-established, delighting in the calming of the mind, [beings are] bound and unbound through view, the evil and the noble’ — so the verse.

In some places it is spoken of by the word ‘ornament.’ As stated: ‘A monk or nun endowed with the ornament of virtue abandons the unwholesome, cultivates the wholesome.’ In some places it is spoken of by the word ‘ointment.’ Where he says: ‘A monk or nun endowed with the ointment of virtue,’ [the rest] as before. In some places it is spoken of by the word ‘scent.’ ‘There is, [Conjectural: Ānanda], that kind of scent whose scent wafts downwind, against the wind, and both with and against the wind.’ In some places it is spoken of by the word ‘good conduct’ (sucarita). Where he says: ‘The fruit of good bodily conduct is to be desired in the visible-here-and-now and in the after-state; likewise of good verbal conduct.’ In some places it is spoken of by the word ‘restraint.’ Where he says: ‘The giver, the donor, becomes endowed with virtue, established in restraint, grounded in scriptural learning and view, seeing the fruit.’ And it is said: ‘He dwells endowed with virtue, restrained by the restraint of the Prātimokṣa,’ [as elaborated above] in detail.

For what reason did the Blessed One speak of virtue by the word ‘root’?

Because the meaning of ‘root’ is the meaning of establishment, the meaning of basis. This very virtue is the establishment, the basis, for the arising and attainment of all worldly and supramundane pure, blameless, supreme, distinguished, pleasant-fruited dharmas; therefore it is spoken of by the word ‘root.’ Just as the earth is the establishment, the basis, for the arising of grasses, shrubs, herbs, and trees, just so [is] virtue, [as detailed] above.

For what reason is virtue declared by the word ‘ornament’?

Answer: Those other adornments — necklaces, bracelets, armlets, rings, garlands of gold and silver — only as long as one is young, youthful, with dark hair, possessing the freshness of youth, do those adornments, when put on him, [actually] adorn. But not so for one aged, old, weak, octogenarian or nonagenarian, broken-toothed, gray-haired — on such a one those adornments, put on, appear on the contrary as if mocking [him]. In sickness, in loss of wealth, in loss of kin — when these befall — he does not shine [in such adornments]. Virtue, however, is an adornment for all [people], at all times. Therefore it is spoken of by the word ‘ornament.’

For what reason is virtue spoken of by the word ‘ointment’?

Here, the great wholesome, blameless undertaking of virtue removes the bodily and mental burning caused by every undertaking of bad conduct (dauḥśīlya) — as for one scorched by heat in the great-summer-burning time, sandal ointment or camphor ointment [removes the burning]. For this reason virtue is spoken of by the word ‘ointment.’

For what reason is virtue called ‘scent’?

For a virtuous individual, in all directions and intermediate directions, fair fame, repute, voice, [and] eulogy go forth — just as, of various kinds of root-scents, sap-scents, or flower-scents, when wafted in the directions, an agreeable scent goes forth. For this reason virtue is spoken of by the word ‘scent.’

For what reason is virtue called ‘good conduct’?

‘This conduct leads to ease, this conduct leads to heaven, leads to good destinies.’ Therefore it is called good conduct.

For what reason is virtue called ‘restraint’?

This dharma has cessation [of unwholesome dharmas] as its own-nature, has the mark of cessation, has abstention as its own-nature. Therefore it is called by the word ‘restraint.’ Of this restraint of virtue, the threefold review is the cause of purification.

What threefold?

Namely: review of bodily action, review of verbal action, review of mental action. Reviewing these [three kinds of] action, he purifies the restraint of virtue. Whatever action he intends to perform with the body, that he reviews [thus]: ‘Is this bodily action of mine harmful to myself or others, unwholesome, giving rise to suffering, with painful result? Or is this bodily action of mine non-harmful to myself and others, wholesome, giving rise to ease, with pleasant result?’ If, reviewing thus, he knows: ‘this bodily action of mine is harmful to myself or another, unwholesome, [Conjectural: giving rise to suffering, with painful result],’ then, having so reviewed, he does not engage in it; that action he does not do, does not perform. If, again, he knows: ‘this bodily action of mine is non-harmful, wholesome,’ [the rest] as before, that he does — that bodily action he does not withhold, but performs. Even an action that, with reference to past time, has been done by him through the body — that too he repeatedly reviews: ‘Was this [action] of mine harmful?’ [the rest] as before. If, reviewing thus, he knows: ‘this action of mine is harmful,’ [the rest] as before, in the presence of wise co-religionists he withholds, he performs. Even an action that, with reference to past time, has been done by him through the body — that too he repeatedly reviews: ‘Was this [action] of mine harmful?’ [the rest] as before. If, reviewing thus, he knows: ‘this action of mine is harmful,’ [the rest] as before, in the presence of wise co-religionists he confesses, [and] makes amends according to Dharma. If, on the other hand, reviewing thus, he knows: ‘this bodily action of mine is non-harmful,’ [the rest] as before, by that very joy and gladness he dwells, day and night, much in training. Thus that bodily action of his is well reviewed and well purified, namely, in past, future, and present times. As with bodily action, so verbal action is to be understood. Mind arises dependent on past formations; mind arises dependent on future and present formations. That mind he repeatedly reviews: ‘Is this mind of mine harmful?’ [the rest] as before, up through: he does not produce [it]; he withholds, does not perform, that mental action. Conversely, on the bright side, he produces [it], does not withhold, [but] performs that mental action. Thus that mental action is by him reviewed [and] well purified, namely, in past, future, and present times. For what reason? In past time, in future time, and in present time, whatever recluses or brahmins, having reviewed and purified bodily, verbal, and mental action, have lived extensively [in this way] — all of them have done so by such review and purification, as was said by the Blessed One concerning the Venerable Rāhula: ‘Bodily action, then verbal action, and also mental action, Rāhula — repeatedly review [them], remembering the instruction of the Buddhas.

This is the work of the recluse — train in this, Rāhula. For one training therein, there is only good, not evil.’

Here, that he investigates ‘bodily action, verbal action, mental action — is this harmful for me?’ [the rest] as before — this is the review. And that some [actions] he withholds and confesses, some he performs, and by that very joy and gladness — training day and night — he dwells much [in training] — this is called purification. Of the restraint of virtue thus purified, ten benefits are to be understood.

What ten?

[1] Here a virtuous individual dwells reviewing his own purity of virtue and gains non-regret; for one free of regret there is gladness; for one of gladdened mind joy arises; with a joyful mind the body becomes pliant; with pliant body he feels pleasure; for one at ease the mind is concentrated; with concentrated mind he knows as it actually is, sees as it actually is; knowing-and-seeing as it actually is, he becomes disenchanted; disenchanted, he becomes dispassionate; dispassionate, he is liberated; for one liberated, the knowing-and-seeing of liberation arises; [and so on] up through: he attains final nirvāṇa in the nirvāṇa-element without remainder. [Thus] a virtuous individual, through the power of the purity of virtue, gains non-regret, and by gradual progression [proceeds] up to the going to nirvāṇa — this is the first benefit of virtue. [2] Further, a virtuous individual, when the time of death is present, [reflects]: ‘good has been done by me, well [done] [Lacuna]; good conduct of body, speech, [and] mind [has been done by me]; bad conduct of body [etc.] has not been done by me,’ [the rest] as before. ‘Such is the destiny of those who have done merit, done wholesome deeds, made a shelter against fear — to that destiny shall I, after death, go.’ He gains this second [kind of] non-regret, [which leads] to a happy destiny. For a non-regretful individual, death is good, the time of death is good, the after-state is good. This is the second benefit of virtue.

[3] Further, of the virtuous individual, fair praise, fame, renown, [and] report go forth. This is the third benefit of virtue.

[4] Further, the virtuous individual sleeps comfortably and [Conjectural: wakes] comfortably, with body and mind free from burning. This is the fourth benefit of virtue. [5] Further, the virtuous individual, even when asleep, is guarded by the deities. This is the fifth benefit of virtue.

[6] Further, the virtuous individual is not apprehensive of harm from others, is not afraid, not of terrified mind. This is the sixth benefit of virtue.

[7] Further, the virtuous individual — even when killers, enemies, [Damaged] have found an opening — is guarded; knowing always ‘this individual is such [a virtuous one],’ they fall either into friendship with him or into a neutral disposition. This is the seventh benefit of virtue.

[8] Further, even when [Damaged], yakṣas, resident spirits, [and] non-humans have found an opening, he is guarded — namely, by making that very virtue predominant. This is the eighth benefit of virtue.

[9] Further, the virtuous individual, by Dharma [and] with little hardship, gains from others robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, [and other] requisites — that is, on account of virtue, he is honored [and] esteemed by kings, royal ministers, townsmen, country-folk, the wealthy, the guild-masters, [and] caravan leaders. This is the ninth benefit of virtue.

[10] Further, [as] above, all his aspirations succeed. If he wishes, [saying]: ‘In the realm of desire, may I be born in the company of kṣatriya families of great wealth, or brāhmaṇa families of great wealth, or householder families of great wealth, or among the deities of the Four Great Kings, the Trāyastriṁśa, Yāma, Tuṣita, Nirmāṇarati, or Paranirmitavaśavartin’ — that [comes about] through the purity of his virtue, [Damaged]. ‘And may I, entering the dhyānas, dwell at ease in the visible-here-and-now, and may I be born in the company of the form-realm deities and dwell [there]’ — this aspiration of the virtuous, dispassionate one succeeds. If he wishes, ‘[Damaged] the liberations (vimokṣa) [Damaged] may I attain and dwell [in them]’; or ‘in the company of the formless-realm deities may I be born,’ [the rest] as before.

That [comes about] through the purity of his virtue and because he is everywhere dispassionate. This is to be understood as the tenth benefit of virtue.

What is restraint of the senses?

When someone here dwells with guarded sense-doors, of guarded mindfulness, of alert mindfulness — [as elaborated] in detail. How, then, does he dwell with guarded sense-doors? He is of guarded mindfulness, of alert mindfulness — [as elaborated] in detail, up to: he guards the mind-faculty; over the mind-faculty he thus undertakes [restraint]. Thus he dwells with guarded sense-doors.

How, then, is his mindfulness guarded?

When someone here, taking the very [practice of] guarded sense-doors as predominant, holds firmly to what has been heard, reflected upon, or cultivated. And by that, he attains a mindfulness whose predominant ground is hearing, reflection, and cultivation. Of that mindfulness once attained — so that it is not forgotten, is realized, is not lost — he applies effort and practice, from time to time, on that same teaching he heard, and he applies effort and practice in reflecting on it and cultivating it. He does not let his application drop. Thus, of the mindfulness born from hearing, reflection, and cultivation, the guarding is performed from time to time through the practice of those very three. Thus he becomes one of guarded mindfulness.

How does he have alert mindfulness?

He becomes one constantly active in that very mindfulness. And one acting toward going-forth. Of these, constant action (nityakāritā) is what is called continual action (sātatyakāritā). Action toward going-forth (niryāṇakāritā) is what is called earnest action (satkṛtyakāritā). He who acts thus — continually and in earnest — is called one of alert mindfulness. As he has guarded mindfulness, so he does not lose that mindfulness; as he has alert mindfulness, so in that very unlost mindfulness he gains strength, by which he becomes able and fit for the overcoming of forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, dharmas.

How is his mind guarded by mindfulness?

Depending on the eye and forms, there arises eye-consciousness; immediately after eye-consciousness there arises discriminative mind-consciousness; by that discriminative mind-consciousness, one is drawn toward pleasing forms and feels ill will toward displeasing forms. Taking that very [mindfulness] as predominant, he guards [the mind] against the unwise discrimination that produces defilement, so that defilement does not arise. Likewise for ear, nose, tongue, [and] body; and depending on mind and dharmas, mind-consciousness arises. And that mind-consciousness, accompanied by unwise discrimination, produces defilement, by which one is drawn toward pleasing dharmas and feels ill will toward displeasing dharmas. He guards that mental state against the unwise discrimination that produces defilement — and thus defilement does not arise in him. Thus he becomes one whose mind is guarded by mindfulness.

How is he one who maintains a balanced state?

‘Balanced state’ here means: equanimity, wholesome or indeterminate. Having guarded that mental state from the unwise discrimination that produces defilement, he settles it in wholesome equanimity or in indeterminate equanimity. Therefore he is called one who maintains a balanced state. Thus he becomes one who maintains a balanced state.

And how, again, does he guard the mental state from the unwise discrimination that produces defilement?

He does not become a seizer of signs. In those forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, and dharmas he does not become a seizer of secondary features — that on account of which evil unwholesome dharmas would flow into his mind. If, even so, through lapse of mindfulness or through the strength of his defilements, even while he avoids sign-seizing and secondary-feature-seizing, there do arise evil unwholesome dharmas, [and,] ill-grasped, they do flow into the mind — he applies himself to their restraint. By these two modes, the mental state is guarded from the unwise discrimination that produces defilement.

And how, having guarded the mental state by these two modes, does he settle it in wholesome or indeterminate equanimity?

By two modes again.

Which two?

As it is said: ‘he guards the eye-faculty, undertakes restraint over the eye-faculty,’ and just as ‘he guards the eye-faculty, undertakes restraint over the eye-faculty,’ so for the ear, nose, tongue, and body — and he guards the mind-faculty, undertakes restraint over the mind-faculty. By these two modes, he settles that mental state in wholesome or indeterminate equanimity.

How, with respect to the forms cognizable by the eye, does he not become a seizer of signs?

‘Seizing of sign’ here means: of the form which is the range of eye-consciousness, becoming a seizer of that range by means of eye-consciousness. That is what it means to become a seizer of signs. Namely, in forms cognizable by the eye, and likewise in dharmas cognizable by ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. If, then, he avoids that range — [the range] of eye-consciousness too — he does not become a seizer of signs in forms cognizable by the eye, and likewise in dharmas cognizable by ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind-consciousness.

How does he not become a seizer of secondary features?

[That is, with respect to] forms cognizable by the eye. ‘Seizing of secondary feature’ here means: in those very forms cognizable by the eye, the range of the discriminative mind-consciousness that arises immediately along with eye-consciousness — when [that range tends] toward lust, toward hatred, or toward delusion — he avoids that range, does not produce, with that object, that mind-consciousness — and thus he does not become a seizer of secondary features in forms cognizable by the eye, or in dharmas cognizable by ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. Another kind of sign-seizing and secondary-feature-seizing: Here, sign-seizing is when, having brought to presence the attention born from them, he sees with the eye the forms that have come into view. And the seizing of secondary features [proceeds as follows]: Those same forms that have come into the eye’s view: having brought to presence the attention born from them, he sees them, but [has] previously heard from another, by report: ‘There are such-and-such eye-cognizable forms’ — and the names, words, and letters that follow upon those [reports], making them predominant, relying on them, established in them, this person discriminates the eye-cognizable forms in accord with what he has heard. This is called seizing of secondary features. As with eye-cognizable forms, so it is to be understood with dharmas cognizable by ear, nose, tongue, body, mind. This sign-seizing and secondary-feature-seizing: there is [that] on account of which, with respect to which, and under whose predominance his evil unwholesome dharmas flow into the mind, and there is [that] on account of which, with respect to which, and under whose predominance evil unwholesome dharmas [Conjectural: do not] flow into the mind. Of these, the sign-seizing, the secondary-feature-seizing, the unwise seizing — that which is the cause, the occasion, the predominant condition for his evil unwholesome dharmas to flow into the mind: that very form of sign-seizing and secondary-feature-seizing he avoids.

What are evil unwholesome dharmas?

Lust, and the bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct produced by lust; hatred and delusion, and the bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct produced by [Conjectural: hatred and] delusion — these are called evil unwholesome dharmas.

How do they ‘flow into the mind’?

With whatever object mind, mentation, [and] consciousness arise — they go [there], they follow along. Associated with mind, mentation, [and] consciousness that have that object, the bodily-, verbal-, and mental-misconduct-producing lust, hatred, and delusion arise, go, follow along. Therefore they are said to ‘flow into the mind.’

Thus, the defilement that would arise — through sign-seizing or secondary-feature-seizing — in eye-cognizable forms (and so on up to mind-cognizable dharmas) does not arise for one who avoids sign-seizing and secondary-feature-seizing. If, even so, through lapse of mindfulness or through the strength of his defilements, evil unwholesome dharmas arise even while he is dwelling alone — taking previously seen eye-cognizable forms as predominant, or taking previously experienced dharmas cognizable by ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind as predominant — once they have arisen he [Conjectural: does not accommodate] them, abandons them, purifies them, and puts an end to them. Therefore he is said to apply himself to their restraint. On forms toward which the eye is [properly] to be directed, and on dharmas cognizable by ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind toward which the mind is [properly] to be directed, to those he directs [his faculties], in such a way that he is not defiled. Thus the mind-faculty is guarded by him from that defilement. Therefore it is said: ‘he guards the mind-faculty.’ On forms cognizable by the eye toward which the eye-faculty is not to be directed, and on dharmas cognizable by ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind toward which the mind-faculty is not to be directed — to those he does not direct [his faculties] at all, in any way. Therefore it is said: ‘he undertakes restraint over the eye-faculty,’ and so on up through ‘he undertakes restraint over the mind-faculty.’ This much is the detailed exposition of sense-restraint, to be understood. In summary: by what one restrains, [Lacuna] from what one restrains, how one restrains, and what the restraint itself is — all this gathered into one is called restraint of the senses.

By what does he restrain?

By guarded mindfulness — by that he restrains.

What does he restrain?

He restrains the eye-faculty; he restrains the ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-faculty. This is what he restrains.

From what does he restrain?

From pleasing and displeasing forms, sounds, and so on up through dharmas — from these he restrains.

How does he restrain?

He does not become a seizer of signs, does not become a seizer of secondary features — that on account of which evil unwholesome dharmas flow into the mind, to their restraint he applies himself; he guards the faculty, undertakes restraint over the faculty. Thus he restrains.

And what is the restraint itself?

That by which he is one whose mind is guarded by mindfulness, and one who maintains a balanced state — this is called restraint. A further summary meaning: the means of restraint, the object to be restrained, and the restraint itself — these gathered into one are called restraint of the senses.

What is the means of restraint?

What is said as: ‘he becomes one of guarded mindfulness, alert mindfulness; seeing forms with the eye, he does not become a seizer of signs, does not become a seizer of secondary features — up through: cognizing dharmas with the mind, he does not become a seizer of signs, does not become a seizer of secondary features’ — ‘that on account of which evil unwholesome dharmas would flow into the mind, to their restraint he applies himself; he guards the faculty, undertakes restraint over the faculty.’ This is called the means of restraint.

What is the object to be restrained?

Eye-and-form, and so on up through mind-and-dharmas — this is called the object to be restrained.

And what is the restraint itself?

What is said as: ‘he is one whose mind is guarded by mindfulness, one who maintains a balanced state’ — this is called restraint. This restraint of the senses is, in brief, of two kinds: that grounded in the power of reflection (pratisaṁkhyāna-bala), and that grounded in the power of cultivation (bhāvanā-bala). Of these, the one grounded in the power of reflection is that by which he sees danger in the sense-objects, but does not remove that danger, does not abandon it. The one grounded in the power of cultivation is that by which he sees danger in the sense-objects and also removes that danger, abandons it. By sense-restraint grounded in the power of reflection, the obsession of defilement that takes sense-objects as its object does not arise, does not come to manifest — but he does not abandon, does not eradicate, the latent tendency settled in the basis. By sense-restraint grounded in the power of cultivation, the obsession of defilement that takes sense-objects as its object does not arise, does not come to manifest — and at all times he also abandons the latent tendency settled in the basis, eradicates it. This is the distinction; this is the import. This is the difference between sense-restraint grounded in the power of reflection and that grounded in the power of cultivation. The sense-restraint grounded in the power of reflection belongs to the path of requisites (saṁbhāra-mārga). The sense-restraint grounded in the power of cultivation is to be understood as falling within the plane of dispassion.

What is knowing the right measure in food?

When someone here takes food with [proper] reflection — not for frivolity, not for adornment, not for embellishment, [as elaborated] above.

How does he take food with [proper] reflection?

‘Reflection’ here refers to that wisdom by which he sees the danger in morsel-made food and, having found it loathsome through seeing the danger, eats.

What, again, is that danger-seeing?

[It is the danger seen] in that very morsel-made food, as connected with consumption, with transformation, or with seeking.

What is the danger connected with consumption [in food]?

When someone here, at the time of taking food endowed with color, endowed with smell, endowed with taste, very fine — when his morsel-food, as soon as it is put into the mouth, is ground by the mill of the teeth, soaked by the spreading saliva, and wrapped in saliva — at the time when it has rolled down through the throat-tube, it wholly, entirely loses that former, original agreeableness, and undergoes an extreme alteration, and in that transformation underway, it appears like vomit. And [as for] that very state — if the consumer-individual were to attend to its appearance, to recollect it, his desire to enjoy [it] would in no way remain, [not] even with respect to other, as yet untransformed fine food. What more is to be said of that [later] state? By these manifold modes, by this sequence, with food-consumption as the predominant [factor], this fine appearance vanishes, and the danger appears, counted among the unclean. This is called the consumption-connected danger, namely, in food.

What is the transformation-connected danger in food?

When he has taken that food and is satisfied, [and] when it undergoes transformation — in the middle watch of the night or in the last watch — it then [produces] blood, flesh, sinew, bone, skin and so on, of many kinds and many sorts: the impure substances in this body it increases and generates. And in part, having undergone further transformation, it descends to the lower [part of the body], which he has to wash off every day. And whatever it touches — hand or foot or any other limb — becomes outwardly loathsome to himself and to others. And on that account [of food] arise in his body many bodily afflictions, namely: tumor, boil, ringworm, scabies, itch, leprosy, skin-disease, pallor, fever, cough, swelling, consumption, epilepsy, ague (āṭakkara), jaundice, blood [disorder], bile [disorder], fistula — these and other such bodily afflictions arise in the body. Or what was eaten goes bad, on account of which cholera appears in his body. This is called the danger of transformation, namely, in food.

What, then, is the seeking-connected danger in food?

The seeking-connected danger is of many kinds: caused by gathering, caused by guarding, caused by loss of affection, caused by insatiability, caused by lack of autonomy, and caused by misconduct. What is the danger in food caused by gathering? When someone here, for the sake of food, on account of food, struck by cold in the cold, struck by heat in the heat, makes effort, strives, exerts himself — by farming, by cattle-tending, by trade, or by writing, calculating, accounting, [and] seals, [and] by manifold arts and crafts, for the attainment or increase of food not yet attained. As with food, so with food’s cause. If, for him exerting himself, striving and struggling thus, those undertakings fail, he on that account grieves, [is] exhausted, laments, beats his chest, cries out, [Conjectural: falls into a daze]: ‘ alas, my exertion is vain and fruitless.’ This is the danger arising from gathering — namely, in food. If he succeeds, then by reason of guarding [his gains] he becomes intensely anxious: ‘May my possessions not be taken by kings or by thieves; may they not be burned by fire; may they not be washed away by water; may my ill-stored deposits not perish; may my poorly applied [investments] not collapse; may unloved heirs not obtain them; or [lest] a firebrand of the family (kulāṁgāra) arise in the family, who by misconduct would bring those possessions to ruin’ — this is the danger arising from guarding, namely, in food.

What is the danger caused by loss of affection?

When, by reason of food, on account of food, the mother speaks ill of the son, the son of the mother, the father of the son, the son of the father, brother of sister, sister of brother, companion of companion — much more one person against another person. They are mutually opposed, fallen into dispute; likewise eminent brāhmaṇas, kṣatriyas, [and] householders of great wealth, on account of food — thus opposed and fallen into dispute — strike each other with the hand, [strike each other] with clod, with stick, with weapon. This is called the danger caused by loss of affection. What is the danger caused by insatiability? When consecrated kṣatriya kings, discontented with their own villages, market-towns, kingdoms, [and] capitals, resort to battle-arrays advancing on each other from both sides — with conches sounded, drums beaten, various arrows shot — there they meet together with frenzied horses, with frenzied elephants, with chariots, [and] with foot-soldiers they clash. By arrows or by spears their limbs are cut off, [and] they go to their death; or deadly pain. This is called the danger caused by insatiability — or whatever else is of such kind.

What is the danger caused by lack of autonomy (asvātantrya)? When the king’s men storm fortified cities, they are doused with boiling oil, with boiling fat, with boiling cow-dung slurry, with molten copper, with molten iron — [or] by arrows and spears their limbs are cut off, [and] they go to their death; or deadly pain. This is called the danger caused by lack of autonomy — or whatever else is of such kind.

What is the danger caused by misconduct?

When by someone, on account of food, much bodily misconduct has been done [and] accumulated — and likewise verbal and mental [misconduct] — and when he becomes bodily afflicted, suffering, gravely ill, his prior bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct, like the shadow of mountains or mountain peaks in the evening, hangs over him, bears down on him, looms over him. It occurs to him: ‘Evil has been done by me; merit has not been done by me, [whether] by body, speech, [or] mind. The destiny of those who have done evil — to that destiny shall I, after death, go.’ Remorseful, he dies, and [Conjectural: having died], he is reborn in the lower realms — namely in the hells, the animal-realm, [or] the ghost-realm. This is called the danger caused by misconduct. Thus it occurs to him: ‘This food, in its seeking, is dangerous; in its consumption too, [is] dangerous; and once consumed, [its] transformation is dangerous.’

Yet there is for this food some slight benefit. What, then, is that?

‘This body sustained by food stands depending on food, not without food’ — this is the benefit. ‘Thus this food-sustained body, standing for a long time, stands [even] up to a hundred years, or somewhat longer when well looked after; and there is also its [possible] cessation in the meanwhile.’ Those who undertake practice merely for body-maintenance are not well-practiced; those who are content with mere body-maintenance are not truly content, and they do not experience the full, blameless benefit of food. But those who are neither content with mere body-maintenance nor practice merely for body-maintenance, but, relying on that very body-maintenance, undertake practice for the fulfillment of the holy life — they are well-practiced, and they experience the full, blameless benefit. ‘It would [Conjectural: not] be fitting for me to dwell content with only this slight, inferior share of food’s benefit. It would not be fitting for me to fall into the class of fools, into Dharma-commonality with fools.’ Having thus known the wholly complete danger in food, with [proper] reflection he becomes a seer of danger and seeker of escape — for the sake of escape from food itself, [Conjectural: as with the perception of a son’s flesh]. It occurs to him: ‘These donor-patrons, having gathered their possessions with difficulty, undergoing great seeking-caused danger, as though pressing out their own skin, flesh, and blood, give [food] to us. These [patrons] give out of compassion, seeking a distinguished fruit. For this alms-food thus received by us, the fitting mode of use would be this: that, having [Conjectural: settled] myself accordingly, I should consume it so that their acts of service become very fruitful, of great benefit, great glory, [and] great extension; and that, like the moon, I should approach their families — withdrawn in body, withdrawn in mind, possessed of moral shame, non-brazen, non-self-aggrandizing, non-deprecating-of-others, with mind well-clear and glad at my own gain, and equally with clear and glad mind at another’s gain — and with such a mind I would again approach their families. How could this be proper for one gone forth [into the homeless life], [going] among others’ families: ‘let others give me food’ — honoringly, not without honor; plentifully, not scantily; finely, not crudely; swiftly, not slowly. [I should resolve:] if, while I am walking thus and approaching families, others do not give, on their account I should not sully myself with a mind of resentment or a mind of hostility, and further, on that account, with the breaking up of the body, I should not come to ruin through rebirth in the lower realms — namely, by taking that very resentful and hostile mind as predominant. [And if] they give without honor rather than with honor, scantily rather than plentifully, crudely, not finely; slowly, not swiftly — [I should not] on that account sully myself with a mind of resentment and hostility,’ [the rest] as elaborated above. ‘And relying on this morsel-made food, I should practice in such a way, and should know that very measure by which there would neither be cessation of the life-faculty, nor would I be worn out by alms-food, and there would be support for the holy life; and likewise, while I stand in the state of a recluse, the state of one gone forth, this mode of using alms-food too would be pure and blameless.’ By these modes he, with [proper] reflection, takes food.

What, again, is food?

[There are] four [kinds of] food: morsel-made [food], contact, mental volition, [and] consciousness. In this context, morsel-made food is intended. And what is that? Namely: barley-meal or cakes or rice or porridge, ghee, oil, syrup, meat, fish, dried meat, salt, milk, curd, butter — these and other such kinds of substances which, made into morsels, are eaten. Therefore it is called morsel-made. ‘He takes [it]’ [means] he eats; partakes of, takes, chews, devours, tastes, drinks, sucks — these are synonyms. ‘Not for frivolity’ — that is, [this refers to] those who are enjoyers of sense-pleasures — that is the meaning — They take food in such a way [thinking]: ‘by [this] food, we — of pleased body, satisfied body, when evening time is at hand and night has set in — with women of coiffed hair and rounded breasts, [Damaged], sporting [and] taking pleasure, will display elation and play.’ ‘Frivolity,’ in the noble Dharma-Vinaya, is what consists of evil, unwholesome dharmas connected with sensual lust, connected with intercourse — the thoughts by which one, devoured and oppressed, becomes of agitated senses, of racing mind, of leaping mind, of unsettled mind, of uncalmed mind. Those who take food wholly for that are said to ‘take food for frivolity.’ The learned noble disciple, however, possessing the power of reflection, seeing the danger [and] knowing the escape, eats [food] — not in the way those enjoyers of sense-pleasures eat [it]. Therefore he says: ‘not for frivolity, not for intoxication, not for adornment, not for embellishment.’ Now, [as for] how those enjoyers of sense-pleasures take food: ‘Today we have taken food — namely, plentifully and to satisfaction in accord with [our] strength and capacity: rich, virility-giving, nourishing, endowed with color, smell, and taste. [Damaged] When night has departed, we shall be able, [being] fit, to do exercise — namely by stretching [the bow], by pounding, by the exercise-stone, by rolling, by digging the earth, by arm-exercise, by foot-planting, by leaping, by jumping. and relying again on much exercise we shall become strong, with well-exercised limbs, long-time healthy, and for a long time our youth will continue, [and] the disfiguring [process of] aging will not swiftly overcome the body. And we shall live longer, and in plentiful eating we shall be fit, and what we eat will be well digested, and the diminution of the humors will be effected.’ Thus they eat [food] for intoxication with health, intoxication with youth, intoxication with life. It further occurs to them: ‘Having exercised, we shall make bathing-arrangements — namely, with clean water we shall wash our limbs, and with washed limbs we shall dress our hair, and, having anointed the body with various ointments, [we shall] with various cloths, with various garlands, with various ornaments adorn the body.’ That bathing, dressing, [and] anointing — this is called their adornment. And of those so adorned, the wearing of cloths, garlands, [and] ornaments — this is called embellishment. Eating [food] for adornment, for embellishment — thus eating, thus intoxicated, adorned, with embellished limbs, at midday time, at evening time, at meal time, thirsty and hungry, with great delight, great joy, great pleasure, [Conjectural: not] seeing the danger, not knowing the escape, they take food as obtained, simply, again and again, for frivolity, for adornment, for embellishment. The learned noble disciple, however, possessing the power of reflection, seeing the danger [and] knowing the escape, eats [food] — but not in the way those enjoyers of sense-pleasures eat [it]. [He thinks] only: ‘This food, [Conjectural: not to be indulged in] and to be abandoned, I shall abandon even while making use of it.’ ‘Only for the sustaining of this body’: that life [continues] having eaten, not without having eaten — this is called the sustaining of the body. ‘I, having taken this food, shall live, shall not die,’ thus [thinking] he eats. Therefore he says: ‘only for the sustaining of this body.’

How does he take food ‘for its support’?

Continuation (yātrā) is twofold: there is the difficult and there is the not-difficult.

What is difficult continuation?

When, from taking such food, hunger or weakness arises, or suffering, severe illness; or [when] he seeks alms-food by non-Dharma, not by Dharma; [when he is] eating with attachment, with clinging, with greed, with binding, with infatuation, with fixation, with fixation-state; or [when] his body becomes heavy, unworkable, unfit for [pursuit of] abandonment, by which his mind is [only] slowly concentrated; or [when] in-and-out breaths proceed with difficulty; or [when] sloth-and-torpor envelops the mind — this is called difficult continuation.

What is non-difficult continuation?

When, [from] taking such food, no hunger or weakness arises, [and] he does not become [Conjectural: afflicted], pained or gravely ill; or [when] he seeks alms-food by Dharma, not by non-Dharma; or [when he] eats without attachment, without clinging, without greed, without binding, [Lacuna] without fixation, without fixation-state; and his body does not become heavy, becomes workable, fit for [pursuit of] abandonment, by which his mind is swiftly concentrated; [when] in-and-out breaths proceed with little difficulty; [when] sloth-and-torpor does not envelop the mind — this is called continuation with little difficulty. There, by the difficult continuation, life-maintenance occurs, but it is a faulty and defiled maintenance of the body. By the continuation with little difficulty, life-maintenance occurs of the body and it is blameless, undefiled. There, the learned noble disciple avoids the faulty, defiled continuation, [and] turns toward [and] resorts to the blameless, undefiled continuation. Therefore he says: ‘for support.’ That blameless, undefiled continuation which was earlier described — how does he maintain it? Answer: ‘[This eating] is for the stilling of hunger, for the supporting of the holy life; old feelings I shall abandon and new ones I shall not produce. I shall have continuation, strength, comfort, blamelessness, [and] dwelling at ease.’ Using [food] thus, he maintains a blameless, undefiled continuation.

And how, again, does he take food ‘for the stilling of hunger’?

When the meal-time has come and hunger has arisen, in eating [food] he eats just that measure [needed] for the removal of that very hunger-obsession and hunger-weakness, such that for him, having eaten, hunger-weakness does not afflict [him] at the untimely time — at evening time, or during the night, [or] on the next day until the meal-time has come. Thus he takes food for the stilling of hunger.

How does he take food ‘for the supporting of the holy life’?

He eats such a measure, takes such food, that for him, applied to the wholesome side, [whether] in the visible-here-and-now, immediately after eating or on that very day, the body is not heavy, and is workable, [and] fit for [pursuit of] abandonment, by which his mind is swiftly concentrated, in-and-out breaths proceed with little difficulty, sloth-and-torpor does not envelop the mind, by which he becomes fit and capable, [and] swiftly [becomes fit] for the attainment of the not-yet-attained, for the realization of the not-realized, for directly realizing what has not yet been directly realized. Thus he eats for the supporting of the holy life.

How does he eat [under the rubric of] ‘old feelings I shall abandon’?

When, with respect to the past, he has eaten either out of measure, or unwholesomely, or [eaten what is] undigested — by which manifold bodily affliction has arisen in him, namely itching, leprosy, skin-disease, pallor-disease — [as elaborated] above, and on that account there arise in him bodily feelings — painful, intense, harsh, biting, disagreeable — [then,] for the calming of that affliction and of those painful feelings caused by it, he uses beneficial, suitable, favorable, conformable medicine by the method prescribed by the physician, and eats agreeable food, by which the abandonment of that arisen affliction and the painful feelings caused by it occurs. Thus he takes food [thinking] ‘old feelings I shall abandon.’ With respect to the present, [being] healthy, well, [and] strong, he does not eat out of measure, [nor] unwholesome [food], nor [eat] while [food] is undigested in the body, [so that] with respect to his future state — tomorrow or the day after — cholera would not lodge in his body, nor any other bodily affliction arise — namely itching, leprosy, skin-disease, pallor-disease — [as elaborated] above, on account of which bodily feelings would arise [as] above. Thus also [under the rubric] ‘new feelings I shall not produce’ — he eats.

How [is the rubric] ‘continuation shall be for me’?

[And] ‘[Conjectural: strength] and comfort and blamelessness and dwelling at ease [shall be for me]’ — [thus] he eats. That, having eaten, he lives — thus there is continuation. That hunger-weakness is removed — thus there is his [Conjectural: strength]. That he abandons old feelings and does not produce new ones — thus there is his comfort. That he seeks alms-food by Dharma, unattached, unclinging — [as elaborated] above — thus there is [his] blamelessness. That, for one who has eaten, the body is not heavy, is workable, fit for [pursuit of] abandonment — [as elaborated] above — thus there is his dwelling at ease. Therefore he says: ‘with reflection he takes food, not for frivolity, not for intoxication, not for adornment,’ [the rest] as elaborated above. — This is the detailed division of knowing the right measure in food. What is the summary meaning? Answer: what he eats, and how he eats [it]. Namely, morsel-made food: barley-meal, cakes, rice, porridge, [as elaborated] above.

How does he eat [it]?

With reflection he eats [it], not for frivolity, not for intoxication, not for adornment, [as elaborated] above. Further, another summary meaning: he eats [food] taken hold of by the antidote, avoiding the extreme of sense-pleasure, and avoiding the extreme of self-mortification, and [eats] for the supporting of the holy life. [Conjectural: How is it taken hold of by the antidote?] When he says: ‘with reflection he takes food.’

How is it avoiding the extreme of sense-pleasure?

When he says: ‘not for frivolity, not for intoxication, not for adornment, not for embellishment.’

How is the self-mortification extreme avoided?

When he says: ‘for the stilling of hunger; old feelings I shall abandon and new ones I shall not produce; I shall have continuation, strength, and comfort.’

How does he eat [food] for the supporting of the holy life?

When he says: ‘for the supporting of the holy life, and blamelessness, and dwelling at ease will be for me.’ Further, another summary meaning: this [taking of food] is twofold — eating and non-eating. Non-eating: when, completely, entirely, in every way, he does not eat anything, and, not eating, dies. Eating is twofold: balanced eating and unbalanced eating. Balanced eating: [eating] neither too little nor too much, not unwholesome, not [while] undigested, not defiled. Unbalanced eating: [eating] either too little or too much; [or] he eats [what is] undigested, unwholesome, or defiled. By balanced eating — by non-too-little eating — unarisen hunger-and-weakness he does not produce, [and] arisen [hunger-and-weakness] he abandons. In non-too-much eating, [Conjectural: by balanced eating], the body does not become heavy, unworkable, unfit for [pursuit of] abandonment, [as elaborated] above. By digested eating — by balanced eating — he abandons old feelings and does not produce new ones; thus there is his continuation, strength, and comfort. By undefiled eating, [and] by balanced eating, there is blamelessness and dwelling at ease. Too-little eating: that by which he barely lives. Too-much eating: that by which his body is overburdened and what is eaten is not digested in time. By undigested eating, cholera lodges in the body, or some other bodily affliction arises. As with undigested eating, so with unwholesome eating. The distinction in unwholesome eating is this: the humors accumulate, or a harsh affliction touches him. By defiled eating, [the meaning is that] having sought alms-food by non-Dharma, he eats [it] with attachment, clinging, greedy, bound — [as elaborated] above. Whoever, then, eats [by] balanced eating and avoids unbalanced eating is therefore called ‘acting evenly with regard to food’ (bhojane samakārī). This even-acting with regard to food is declared, made manifest, opened up, [and] proclaimed by these modes — namely, ‘with reflection he takes food, not for frivolity, not for intoxication, not for adornment, not for embellishment,’ [as elaborated] above. In that he first says, ‘with reflection he takes food, not for frivolity, not for intoxication, not for adornment, not for embellishment, only for the sustaining of this body, for its support,’ — by this much he rejects non-eating. Again, when he says, ‘for the stilling of hunger, for the supporting of the holy life,’ [and so on] up through ‘dwelling at ease’ — by this he rejects unbalanced eating. And how, again, does he reject unbalanced eating? [Lacuna] When he says ‘for the supporting of the holy life,’ by this he rejects too-much eating. When he says: ‘old feelings I shall abandon and new ones I shall not produce,’ by this he rejects the undigested-eating-state and the unwholesome-eating-state. When he says: ‘I shall have continuation and strength,’ by this he indicates [Conjectural: the not-too-little-eating-state and the not-too-much-eating-state]. When he says: ‘comfort will be for me,’ by this he indicates the digested-eating-state. When he says: ‘comfort will be for me,’ he also indicates the wholesome-eating-state. When he says: ‘blamelessness will be for me, and dwelling at ease,’ he indicates the undefiled-eating-state. He who, having sought alms-food by non-Dharma, eats [it] with attachment, [with] clinging, [as elaborated] above — he eats as one defiled, and there is blameworthiness for him. For one applied to the wholesome side — in seclusion, in yoga, in attention, in recitation, in reflection on the meaning — those very evil, unwholesome thoughts flow into the mind, which make his mental continuum incline toward them, slope toward them, tend toward them, by which there is for him no dwelling at ease. And this dwelling at ease is twofold: [first,] by avoidance of too-much-eating, by which the body does not become heavy, unworkable, unfit for [pursuit of] abandonment, [as elaborated] above; by the other [aspect] — non-making of relish — by which there is no lack of ease caused by thought-disturbance. Such being the case, all these terms become explanatory of acting evenly with regard to food. This is called knowing the right measure in food, in detail and in brief.

  • What is application to wakefulness in the early and late watches of the night?
  • What is the early [watch]?
  • What is the late watch?
  • What is wakefulness-yoga?
  • What is application to wakefulness-yoga?

Here, the early watch is the evening period: taking its start from sunset, it is the first part of the night, a watch and more. Herein, this is the wakefulness-yoga. When he says: ‘by day, by walking and sitting, he purifies the mind of obscuring dharmas; [Lacuna] having purified [the mind], washing his feet outside the dwelling, entering the dwelling, he lies down on the right side, foot placed on foot, perceiving light, mindful, fully aware, attending to the perception of rising; in the last watch of the night, waking quickly, very quickly, by walking and sitting he purifies the mind of obscuring dharmas.’ Herein, application to the wakefulness-yoga is this: as when a disciple of the Blessed Buddha, a hearer of the wakefulness-yoga, becomes desirous of training therein. For him, so disposed, the desire which [arises] with regard to the wakefulness-yoga — ‘I shall accomplish the application to wakefulness authorized by the Buddhas’ — the energy, effort, vigor, valor, [Conjectural: strength], undertaking, enthusiasm, perseverance, non-turning-back of mind, [and] mental gathering, [Conjectural: continuity].

How, by walking and sitting, does he purify the mind of obscuring dharmas?

[On the terms used:] ‘Day’ — is taken as the period from the sun’s rising up to its setting. ‘Walking’ means extended back-and-forth movement on a wide, measured-out piece of ground — that bodily action. ‘Sitting’ — is when someone here sits on a couch, on a stool, on a grass-mat, folding the legs cross-wise, having set the body upright, establishing mindfulness to the fore. ‘Obscurations’ means: the five hindrances (nivaraṇa).

Obscuring dharmas are those dharmas that occasion the hindrances and conduce to them. And what are they?

Sense-desire, ill-will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-regret, doubt; [Conjectural: attractiveness], the sign of aversion, darkness, thoughts of kin, of country and of not-dying, recollection of past laughter, play, delight and company; and the three times — [that is,] unwise dharma-reflection that pertains to the three times.

From these, how, by walking, does he purify the mind?

[And] from how many [does he so purify it]?

From sloth-and-torpor and from the [other] obscuration that is the food of sloth-and-torpor he purifies [the mind]. The light-sign (ālokanimitta) is by him well, thoroughly grasped, well attended to, well savored, well penetrated. He, with mind accompanied by light, accompanied by radiance — under cover or in the open, while walking on the walking-path — by some gladdening object instructs the mind, rouses it, gladdens it — namely, by recollection of the Buddha, or of the Dharma, the Saṅgha, virtue, generosity or the deities. Or [he draws on] those dharmas which he has heard, grasped, [and] retained that concern the danger of sloth-and-torpor — namely: sūtra, geya, vyākaraṇa, gāthā, udāna, nidāna, avadāna, itivṛttaka, jātaka, vaipulya, adbhuta-dharma, [and] upadeśa — wherein, in many ways, sloth-and-torpor is censured and deplored, and the abandonment of sloth-and-torpor is praised, eulogized, commended. Those [passages] he recites aloud in a full voice, or he proclaims them to others, or reflects on their meaning, weighs them, examines them; or he gazes around at the directions, or holds his gaze on the [Conjectural: moon], the constellations, the planets and the stars, [or] wets his face with water. Thus his obsession by sloth-and-torpor, when unarisen, does not arise; and when arisen, departs. Thus by him the mind is purified of that obscuring dharma.

By sitting, from which obscuring dharmas does he purify the mind?

From sense-desire, ill-will, restlessness-and-regret, doubt, and the [other] dharmas that feed them. When the obsession of sense-desire has arisen, for its dispelling — or when it has not arisen, for keeping it far off — sitting cross-legged, having set the body upright, having established mindfulness to the fore, he attends to the discolored, the festering, [Conjectural: the decomposed], the swollen, the gnawed, the bloodied, the bone, the skeleton, or some other excellent sign of concentration; or [he draws on] those dharmas which, [touching on] the abandonment of sense-lust itself, have been grasped for the abandonment of sense-lust, retained, mastered by voice, examined by mind, well penetrated by view — namely sūtra, geya, vyākaraṇa, [as elaborated] above: those [passages] which in many ways censure and depreciate sense-lust, sense-desire, sensual attachment, sensual compulsion, sensual fixation, deplore them, and praise the abandonment of sense-lust in many ways, eulogize [and] commend [it]. To those very dharmas — sitting thus — he attends wisely; thus his unarisen sense-desire-obsession does not arise, and arisen sense-desire-obsession departs. Now in [the case of] ill-will, this is the distinction: sitting thus, with a mind accompanied by loving-kindness — free of enmity, [Conjectural: free of opposition], free of harm, vast, become great, boundless, well cultivated — resolving on one direction, [Conjectural: pervading] it, he attains and dwells; Likewise the second, the third, the fourth — upward, downward, sideways, the entire endless world — [Conjectural: pervading] it, he attains and dwells. The rest, as before. In the case of restlessness-and-regret, this is the distinction: thus seated, he establishes the mind inwardly, he fixes it; he purifies [it]; he makes it one-pointed; he concentrates [it]. The rest, as before. In the case of the doubt-hindrance, this is the distinction: sitting thus, to the past he does not unwisely attend, to the future and the present he does not unwisely attend [in such manner as]: ‘Was I in the past? Was I not in the past?

Or who was I in the past?

How was I in the past?

Who shall I be in the future? How shall I be in the future? Being what shall I be?

From where has this being come?

Falling from here, whither shall he go?’ Having avoided such unwise attention, he attends wisely [to] past, future, [and] present. He sees only dharmas, only a [bare] entity — the real as real, the unreal as unreal — only cause, only effect; he does not superimpose what does not exist, [and] does not destroy what does exist, does not depreciate [it]. He knows the actual as actual, namely as impermanent, as suffering, as empty, as non-self — [seeing] impermanent, suffering, empty, non-self dharmas [as such]. He, thus wisely attending, is without doubt and without indecision about the Buddha, [without doubt] about the Dharma, the Saṅgha, suffering, origin, cessation, path, cause, [and] about the dharmas arisen from causes — without doubt, without indecision. The rest [is to be understood] as above. Now, regarding ill-will, it should be said: the dharmas grasped by him with respect to aversion and the sign of aversion, and for its abandonment, [as elaborated] above; regarding restlessness-and-regret, it should be said: the dharmas grasped, with respect to restlessness-and-regret, and for its abandonment, [as elaborated] above; regarding doubt, it should be said: those dharmas grasped by him with respect to doubt, and for its abandonment, [as elaborated] above. By this [practice], the mind is purified of the sense-desire-hindrance, [and] of the ill-will-, sloth-and-torpor-, restlessness-and-regret-, [and] doubt-hindrances, and of the obscuring dharmas that are their food. Therefore he says: ‘by walking and sitting he purifies the mind of obscuring dharmas.’ And this is the purification of the mind from obscuring dharmas with the Dharma as the authority. There is also purification of the mind from obscuring dharmas with oneself as the authority, and with the world as the authority.

What is that with oneself as the authority?

When, on the arising of some particular hindrance, knowing by himself its [Conjectural: unsuitability], he does not accommodate the arisen hindrance — he abandons, dispels, puts an end to [it], not letting himself be marked by that hindrance, which defiles the mind, weakens wisdom and belongs to the side of ruin — thus, making himself the authority, he purifies the mind from the obscuring dharmas.

How, making the world the authority, does he purify the mind from obscuring dharmas?

Here, when a hindrance has arisen for him, or its time of arising is present, it occurs to him: ‘If I should produce an unarisen hindrance, the Teacher would reproach me, and the deities, the wise, [and] my co-religionists would censure me, regarding [it] as non-Dharma.’ [Thus,] making the world the authority, he purifies the mind from the obscuring dharmas. He, making the world the authority, does not produce an unarisen hindrance, and abandons one that has arisen. Thus, making the world the authority, he purifies the mind from obscuring dharmas. For the sake of guarding his lodging, and [thinking] ‘I shall conform to worldly custom’, through the first watch of the night, by walking and sitting, he purifies the mind of obscuring dharmas. Having purified the mind of obscuring dharmas, washing his feet outside the dwelling, [and] having washed, entering the dwelling, he lies down — only for the building up of his own nourishment-born great elements, [thinking]: ‘This body, when [thus] replenished, will be more workable and more amenable in the continuous, [Damaged] application to the wholesome.’

For what reason does he lie down on [his right] side?

Because it shares a nature with the lion.

What, here, is the same-nature?

The lion, among all animals of the animal-womb, is the boldest, the most enthusiastic, of firm valor. The monk too, applied to wakefulness, with aroused energy, dwells bold, enthusiastic, of firm valor. Hence for him the lion-couch is appropriate — not the ghost-couch, the deity-couch, nor the sense-pleasure-enjoyer’s couch — for they [whose couches those are] are all indolent, of low vigor, of feeble strength and valor. Moreover, it is the nature of things that for one who lies down on the right side in lion-fashion the limbs do not toss about so, no forgetfulness of mindfulness for one [thus] sleeping, and he does not sleep deeply, and he does not see evil dreams. For one who lies down otherwise, the contrary faults are to be understood. Therefore he says: ‘he lies down on the right side.’ The light-sign is by him well grasped, well attended to, well savored, well penetrated. Just attending to it, with mind accompanied by light, he lies down, and for him, even sleeping, the mind does not enter a darkened state. Thus, perceiving light, he lies down.

How, mindful, does he lie down?

Those dharmas heard by him, reflected on, cultivated, wholesome, conducive to benefit — the mindfulness that follows on them continues until the time of sleep, so that those very dharmas present themselves to him asleep as when awake, and the mind moves abundantly in those very dharmas. Thus, according to his mindfulness, as he is mindful, he lies down with a wholesome mind, or with an indeterminate mind. Thus, mindful, he lies down.

How, fully aware, does he lie down?

For him, sleeping thus mindfully, when at any time, by some secondary defilement, mental defilement occurs, of that arising defilement he is rightly fully aware; he does not accommodate, he abandons, he penetrates [it], he turns the mind back [from it]. Therefore he is said to lie down fully aware.

How, attending to the perception of rising, does he lie down?

He lies down having made the mind held [steady] by energy, with [a disposition to] easy waking, with a well-gladdened mind, just like a forest deer; he does not let the mind wholly, entirely incline toward the onset of sleep, slope toward it, or tend toward it. It occurs to him: ‘Oh, may I in every respect, in every way, accomplish the wakefulness authorized by the Buddhas!’ And for its accomplishment he dwells with longing, relish, application and desire, and is applied. Moreover, it occurs to him: ‘Just as today, for the sake of wakefulness with aroused energy, I have dwelt — and, for the cultivation of wholesome dharmas, skilled, not lazy, endowed with exertion — tomorrow at dawn, when night has gone forth, in still greater measure I shall dwell with aroused energy, endowed with exertion.’ Now, by [the first kind of] rising-perception he [Conjectural: does not] sleep deeply, by which he is able to rise quickly, very quickly at rising-time — not waking after the time has passed. By the second [kind of] rising-perception, he arranges the lion-couch authorized by the Buddhas, neither subtracting nor adding [from it]. By the third [kind of] rising-perception, the desire [for wakefulness-yoga] he does not let slacken. When forgetfulness arises, in each subsequent moment he applies himself to renewed undertaking. Thus, attending to the rising-perception, he lies down. He, in the last watch of the night, waking quickly, very quickly, purifies the mind of obscuring dharmas. ‘The last watch’ means the late night, a watch and more. He, perceiving light, mindful, fully aware, attending to the perception of rising, having slept through the middle watch, a watch and more, at whatever time he rises, at that time he rises up, [being] workable-bodied. Having risen, not overcome by strong sloth-and-torpor obsession, by which there would be in his rising slowness, sluggishness, [or] laziness and indolence; rather, in the absence of that slowness, sluggishness, laziness and indolence, his rising is quick, very quick, at a mere turn of attention, without having to struggle. The purification from the obscuring dharmas is to be understood as above. This much is the detailed division of application to wakefulness in the early and late watches of the night.

What are the summary meanings?

Here, for the person applied to wakefulness-yoga, there are four right tasks.

Which four?

As long as he is awake, he does not let go of the wholesome side, [remaining] in continuous, [Damaged] cultivation of wholesome dharmas; and at the [proper] time he lies down, not out of time; and he goes to sleep with an undefiled mind, not with a defiled mind; and at the [proper] time he wakes, [and] does not overstep the rising-time. With reference to these four right tasks, the application to wakefulness has been taught by the Blessed One to the disciples.

How, again, is it taught?

When he first says: ‘by day, by walking and sitting, he purifies the mind of obscuring dharmas; thus in the first watch of the night,’ by this the first right task is declared, namely: as long as he is awake, he does not let go of the wholesome side, [remaining] in continuous, [Damaged] cultivation of wholesome dharmas. When he then says: ‘washing the feet outside the dwelling, entering the dwelling, he lies down on the right side, foot placed on foot,’ by this the second right task is declared, namely: at the [proper] time he lies down, not out of time. When he then says: ‘perceiving light, mindful, fully aware, attending to the perception of rising, he lies down,’ by this the third right task is declared, namely: he goes to sleep with an undefiled mind, not with a defiled mind. When he then says: ‘in the last watch of the night, waking quickly, very quickly, he purifies the mind of obscuring dharmas,’ by this the fourth right task is declared, namely: at the [proper] time he wakes, [and] does not overstep the rising-time. Thus where it is said ‘perceiving light, mindful, fully aware, attending to the rising-perception, he lies down,’ from [these] two causes he goes to sleep with an undefiled mind — namely, by mindfulness and full awareness; [and] from two further causes he wakes in time, [and] does not overstep the time — namely, by perception of light and perception of rising. How so? He goes to sleep having taken hold of a wholesome object; and by full awareness he quickly, very quickly knows rightly the mind that is falling away from that wholesome object and becoming defiled. Thus by these two causes his sleep is undefiled. And by perception of light and perception of rising, he does not sleep deeply, [and] his torpor-obsession does not become far-going. Thus by these two causes he wakes in time, [and] does not overstep the rising-time. This, then, is the summary meaning of application to wakefulness: [the preceding] detailed analysis, and this summary meaning. This is called application to wakefulness in the early and late watches of the night.

What is dwelling with full awareness (saṁprajānadvihāritā)?

When someone here dwells with full awareness in setting forth and returning, in looking ahead and looking around, in bending and stretching [the limbs], in carrying the saṅghāṭī, the robe and the bowl, in eating, drinking, chewing, tasting, [Lacuna] in sitting, lying and waking, in speaking, in silence, in the dispelling of sleep-fatigue — he dwells with full awareness.

  • What is returning?
  • What is dwelling with full awareness in setting forth and returning?

Setting forth: when someone here approaches a village or another village, a family or another family, a dwelling or another dwelling. Returning: when someone here returns from a village, from another village, from a family, from another family, from a dwelling, from another dwelling. [Now,] dwelling with full awareness [in setting forth and returning]: Setting forth, he rightly knows ‘I am setting forth’; ‘here I should set forth, here I should not set forth’ — he rightly knows; ‘this is for me a time of approach, this is not a time of approach’ — he rightly knows. This is called for him full awareness. He, endowed with that full awareness, setting forth, knows ‘I am setting forth’; where he should set forth, there he sets forth, and at the [proper] time he sets forth, not out of time, [and] as he should set forth, with such bearing, such conduct, such manner [and] such deportment, so he sets forth. This is called for him dwelling with full awareness, namely in setting forth and returning.

What is looking ahead, what is looking around?

What is dwelling with full awareness in looking ahead and looking around?

For him, [while engaged] in the previously proclaimed dharmas of setting forth and returning, that seeing of forms with the eye which, without prior thought, without prior desire, occurs in between — this is called looking ahead. When, having approached [a sight], with prior thought, with prior effort, with prior intent, [there is] the seeing of forms with the eye — namely of kings, of kings’ ministers, townsmen, country-folk, brāhmaṇas, the wealthy, guild-masters, caravan-leaders; or of other external things: of dwellings, of [Conjectural: houses], of inner rooms, of palaces, of mansion-terraces; or again, the seeing of other worldly spectacles — this is called looking around. When he rightly knows looking-ahead and looking-around by their specific characteristics, [and] how one should look ahead, how one should look around — this too he rightly knows. This is called for him full awareness. He, endowed with that full awareness, if looking around, knows ‘I am looking around’; what should be looked at ahead or around, that he looks at; when one should look ahead or around, then he looks ahead, [then he] looks around; as one should look ahead or around, so he looks ahead, [so he] looks around. This is called for him dwelling with full awareness, namely in looking ahead and looking around.

What is bending, what is stretching, what is dwelling with full awareness in bending and stretching?

He, thus looking ahead and looking around, preceded by setting forth and preceded by returning, whether he bends his feet or stretches [them], or bends his arms, stretches [them], or bends his hands, stretches [them] — thus whichever limb or sub-limb he bends or stretches — this is called bending and stretching. If he knows bending-and-stretching by their specific characteristics, [and] what should be bent, what should be stretched, he rightly knows; when one should bend, when one should stretch — that too he rightly knows; as one should bend, as one should stretch — that too he rightly knows. This is called for him full awareness. He, endowed with that full awareness, [when] bending [or] stretching, knows: ‘I am bending, I am stretching’; what should be bent or stretched, that he bends [or] stretches; when one should bend [or] stretch, then he bends [or] stretches. This is called for him dwelling with full awareness, namely in bending and stretching.

What is wearing the saṅghāṭi?

What is wearing robes? What is carrying the bowl? What, in wearing the saṅghāṭi and robes and carrying the bowl, is dwelling with full awareness? First, that principal robe of his — of sixty patches or of ninety patches, double-stitched or single-stitched — this is called saṅghāṭi; putting it on [and] using it, [and] properly looking after it, is called wearing [it]. And his middle [robe], or lower [robe], or determined robe, or extra robe, or [an extra robe] fit for assignment, which he assigns — this is called a robe; putting them on [and] using them, [and] properly looking after them, is called wearing [them]. And that determined vessel, whether iron or earthenware, for alms-food — this is called the bowl; using it, [and] properly looking after it, is called carrying [it]. If, [furthermore,] he rightly knows this saṅghāṭi, robe, [and] bowl, [along with their] wearing or carrying, by their specific characteristics; [and] what wearing or carrying of saṅghāṭi, robe, [or] bowl is allowable or not allowable, that too he rightly knows; when the saṅghāṭi, robe, [or] bowl should be worn or carried, [that] he rightly knows; as it should be worn or carried, that too he rightly knows. This is called for him full awareness. He, endowed with that full awareness, if he wears the saṅghāṭi or robe, or carries the bowl, he knows, ‘I am wearing or carrying [it]’; what should be worn or carried, he wears or carries; when it should be worn or carried, then he wears or carries [it]; as it should be worn or carried, so he wears or carries [it]. This is called for him dwelling with full awareness, namely in wearing the saṅghāṭi and robe, and carrying the bowl.

Whatever use of alms-food [there is] — all that is called ‘eaten.’ Its further division is twofold: chewed and tasted. Chewed: barley-meal or cakes, rice or porridge, or some other prepared food — variously cooked, edible, life-sustaining — this is called chewed, as something eaten and not drunk.

Namely: a dancer, a comedian, a jester, or any other such [Conjectural: amusement] going on, in dance, song or music; likewise womankind, and in particular one young, of fine complexion, attractive — and [in general] those forms whose seeing would harm the holy life, would be an obstacle to the holy life, [or] would conduce to the manifestation of evil, unwholesome thoughts — such forms are not to be looked at ahead, nor looked around at. What kind of forms are to be seen? One should not approach with shaking body, shaking arms, [or] shaking head; not laughing aloud; not arm-in-arm, not with shoulders shrugged; not without leave. One should not sit on a seat without examining the seat; not laying the whole body upon it, not [with] foot on foot, not [with] thigh on thigh, not [with] feet contracted, not [with] feet spread; not wrapped over the head, not lifted high, not spread wide, not hitched up — the robe put on all round, not too high, not too low, not [hanging] like an elephant’s trunk, not like a palmyra-fan, not like a nāga’s hood, nor worn like a lump of beans. [Lacuna] Thus many dharmas of this kind have been grasped by him, retained, well mastered by voice, examined by mind, well penetrated by view. Thus he becomes much-learned.

How is he an attainer [of meditative states]?

He becomes a gainer of the perception of impermanence, of the perception of suffering in the impermanent, of the perception of non-self in suffering, of the perception of food as repugnant, of the perception of the bloody, of the perception of the scattered, of the perception of the bone, [and] of the perception that reviews emptiness. He becomes a gainer of the first dhyāna, [the] second, third, [and] fourth; of the sphere of infinite space, of infinite consciousness, of nothingness, [and] of neither-perception-nor-non-perception; of loving-kindness, compassion, equanimity, [and] sympathetic joy; of the fruit of stream-entry, of once-returning and of non-returning; of [Conjectural: the range of psychic power], of past abodes, of the divine ear, of decease-and-rebirth, of the ways of [others’] minds, of arhatship, [and] of meditation in the eight liberations. He is capable and competent to admonish others by the three wonders: the wonder of psychic power, the wonder of mind-reading, the wonder of admonition. Thus he is an attainer.

How is he compassionate?

Toward others he is full of compassion, full of pity, desirous of their welfare, desirous of their benefit, [their] ease, [their] comfort, [their] security from bondage. Thus he is compassionate.

How is he of unwearying mind?

He demonstrates [the teaching], an exhorter, an arouser, a gladdener, a knower of the four assemblies, skilled in Dharma-teaching, non-lazy, full of initiative, of aroused energy, of aroused-energy nature. Thus he is of unwearying mind.

How is he patient?

Abused, he does not abuse in return; angered [against], he does not anger in return; [Conjectural: struck, he does not strike back]; [Conjectural: mocked, he does not mock in return]. [Conjectural: he endures the stick]; even in tight bonds, imprisonment, beating, threatening, [or] cutting, he [counts] himself at fault, and has recourse to the ripening of [his own] action; he is not angry with others, nor does he bear a latent tendency [of anger]. Thus disregarded, deprecated, [or] despised, he undergoes no change, intends nothing but [their] welfare — and he is patient of cold, heat, hunger, thirst, gnats, mosquitoes, wind, sun, [and] creeping things; of ill-spoken, ill-come [Conjectural: ways of speech] from others; of bodily feelings — painful, intense, harsh, sharp, disagreeable, life-threatening — he is patient, of accommodating nature. Thus is he patient.

How is he confident?

Of unshrinking mind, in an assembly he teaches Dharma with unfaltering voice, his mindfulness and ready speech unfailing; and fear arising from timidity does not arise in him, does not come upon him; nor does sweat run from his [Conjectural: armpits], [Damaged]. Thus he is confident.

How is he endowed with the speech-faculty?

He is endowed with urbane, noble speech: sweet, distinct, intelligible, worth hearing, agreeable, independent, unlimited. Thus he is endowed with the speech-faculty, speaking well. He, endowed with these eight causes, becomes an admonisher, a reminder, an advisor, an instructor, [and] a Dharma-teacher.

How is he an admonisher?

Namely, he admonishes for a deficiency of virtue in higher virtue, for a deficiency of conduct in higher conduct, by what is seen, by what is heard, by what is suspected, by what is real, not by what is unreal; in [proper] time, not out of time; connected with meaning, not unconnected with meaning; gently, not harshly; with friendliness, not with hostility. Thus he is an admonisher.

How is he a reminder?

He reminds [the other] of a transgression, or of the Dharma, or of its meaning.

How does he remind of a transgression?

When someone who has committed a transgression [Conjectural: does not] remember it, he reminds him: ‘Friend, in such a place, in such a matter, at such a time, you committed such-and-such [a transgression].’ Thus he reminds of the transgression.

How does he remind of Dharma?

When someone, alone, [tries to] recall the dharmas [previously] heard, grasped — wishes to recall — namely sūtra, geya, vyākaraṇa, [as elaborated] above — [but] does not recall [them], he reminds him, gives him the prompting, or the asking-and-inquiring [that draws it out]. Thus he reminds of dharmas.

How does he remind of the meaning of Dharma?

When someone forgets the meanings, he reminds him, refreshes [his memory], makes [the meaning] manifest, teaches, proclaims; and what is wholesome, connected with meaning, connected with the holy life, long since done, long since spoken — of that too he reminds. Thus he is a reminder.

How is he an advisor?

In seclusion, retreat, yoga, attention, [and] śamatha-vipaśyanā (calm and insight), from time to time he gives appropriate advice, and from time to time engages in connected talk — namely, agreeable talk leading to the mind’s freedom from hindrance: virtue-talk, wisdom-talk, liberation-talk, talk of few-desires, talk of the knowing-and-seeing of liberation, contentment-talk, abandonment-talk, dispassion-talk, cessation-talk, decrease-talk, non-mixing-talk, [and] talk in accord with dependent origination, with this-conditionality. Thus he is an advisor.

How is he an instructor?

By Dharma and Vinaya, in the dispensation of the Teacher who instructs — being a teacher (ācārya), or [being] a preceptor, or a co-religionist, or a guru, or a person occupying the place of a guru — in any matter of [the disciple’s] overstepping or transgression, knowing of it, from time to time he chastens [him], [and] prescribes a punishment, and expels him; and again, by Dharma, once he has come to terms, he accepts his friendly service, his respectful conduct, and his understanding; and he is one who restores him; in what should be done and not done, [Damaged] in what he habitually practises and what he does not, he teaches and instructs. Thus he is an instructor.

How is he a Dharma-teacher?

From time to time he gives the preliminary-action talk — namely, [the talks on] generosity, virtue, heaven, the danger in sense-pleasures, the escape, [and on] the dharmas on the side of purification, [all] of which he proclaims in detail. From time to time he gives talk connected with the four noble truths, [talk] taking up suffering, origin, cessation, [Conjectural: and the path] — for the maturation of beings, [Damaged] for the purification of beings, for the long endurance of the true Dharma — with suitable words and letters, coherent, conforming, fitting the occasion, apt, befitting, compliant, the limbs and requisites of the prudent [teacher]; and he gives that talk at the [proper] time, respectfully, in due sequence, gladdening, pleasing, encouraging, not depressing — suitable, accompanied [appropriately], unscattered, in accord with Dharma, in accord with the assembly, of friendly mind, beneficial mind, compassionate mind, not depending on gain, honor, [or] praise; he [Conjectural: does not] exalt himself, [and] does not deprecate others. Thus he is a Dharma-teacher. He who is endowed with these eight factors and is thus from time to time an admonisher, reminder, advisor, [and] instructor — therefore he is called a good friend (kalyāṇamitra). This much is the detailed division of association with good friends (kalyāṇamitratā).

What is the summary meaning?

If this friend, helper, compassionate one is, from the start, desirous of [the disciple’s] welfare and desirous of [his] ease, and knows that very welfare-and-ease as it actually is, [being] unperverted, of unperverted view, capable and skilled in means — namely, for the accomplishment of that welfare-and-ease, for its conferral — and is skilled, not lazy, full of initiative, of aroused-energy nature, namely, taking up that very conferral of welfare-and-ease, [then,] wholly perfect by these four causes, he is in brief to be understood as the good friend. And this is the summary meaning of association with good friends.

What was previously [given as] the detailed division, and what [is here] the summary meaning — this is called association with good friends.

What is hearing-and-reflection on the true dharma?

By ‘true dharma’ is meant: that which has been declared by the Buddhas and the disciples of Buddhas, by the true, the rightly-gone, [and] the good persons — taught, set forth, opened up, and proclaimed.

What, again, is that?

Namely sūtra, geya, vyākaraṇa, [and the rest of the twelve, as elaborated above]. The twelvefold word-genre is called true dharma.

What is sūtra?

Whatever talks were given by the Blessed One, here and there, with reference to those various trainees and their various conducts — talks connected with the aggregates, with the elements, with the sense-bases, with dependent origination, with food and truth, or with disciples, solitary buddhas, and tathāgatas; or connected with the foundations of mindfulness, the right exertions, the bases of psychic power, the faculties, the powers, the awakening-factors, the factors of the path; or talks given on unattractiveness, on mindfulness of breathing, on the training, on the confidence born of understanding (avetyaprasāda) — those very talks, taken up by the compilers for the long endurance of the teaching and arranged in due order, joined in due order, with appropriate sequences of names, words, and letters — that is, in such a way as to indicate the relevant meanings, which are wholesome, meaningful, and [Conjectural: connected with the holy life] — these are called sūtra.

What is geya?

[A discourse] at whose end and conclusion verses are sung, and any sūtra whose meaning needs drawing out (neyārtha) — this is called geya.

What is vyākaraṇa?

[A discourse] in which, regarding disciples who have passed away, their [next] rebirth is predicted, together with sūtras whose meaning is final (nītārtha) — this is called vyākaraṇa.

What is gāthā?

What is not spoken in prose, but composed in lines — of two, three, four, five, or six lines — this is called gāthā.

What is udāna?

[A discourse] spoken by way of declaration, without announcing a person’s name and clan, for the future continuance of the true dharma and of the teaching. This is called udāna.

What is nidāna?

[A discourse] spoken by way of indication, with a person's name and lineage announced; and the vinaya-connected Prātimokṣa-sūtra with its origin and occasioning cause — this is called nidāna.

What is avadāna?

[A discourse] given with a parable, by which parable the present matter is illuminated — this is called avadāna.

What is vṛttaka?

Whatever is connected with former lives (pūrvayoga) — this is called vṛttaka.

What is jātaka?

Where the bodhisattva-conducts and difficult deeds of the Blessed One, in his successive births in the past, here and there, are recounted — this is called jātaka.

What is vaipulya?

Where the bodhisattvas' path is taught — for supreme, perfect awakening, for the perfecting of the ten powers and the unobstructed knowledge — this is called vaipulya.

What are the wonderful dharmas?

Where the special qualities of the Buddhas, the disciples of Buddhas, monks, nuns, female probationers, [Lacuna] female novices, [Lacuna] female lay-followers — shared and unshared, surpassing all others, held to be wondrous and marvelous — are declared, these are called wonderful dharmas.

What are upadeśas?

All the mātṛkās, the abhidharma, and the digests drawn from the sūtras. Sūtra-commentary is called upadeśa. And of this twelvefold word-genre there is sūtra, there is vinaya, there is abhidharma. When [the source] first says: ‘sūtra, geya, vyākaraṇa, gāthā, udāna, avadāna, vṛttaka, jātaka, vaipulya, wonderful dharmas’ — this [group of ten] is sūtra. When it then says: ‘nidāna’ — this is called vinaya. When it then says: ‘upadeśas’ — this is called abhidharma. This twelvefold scripture, subsumed under the three baskets, taught by the true, the rightly-gone, is called the true dharma. And the hearing of it is the hearing of the true dharma.

And what again is hearing?

When someone here is a sūtra-bearer (sūtra-dhara), or a vinaya-bearer, or a mātṛkā-bearer, or a sūtra-vinaya-bearer, or a sūtra-abhidharma-bearer, or a vinaya-mātṛkā-bearer — this is called hearing of the true dharma. And that hearing, again, is twofold: hearing of the letter and hearing of the meaning.

What is reflection?

When someone here, alone and secluded, [takes up] those very dharmas as heard — having set aside the six matters that are unthinkable (namely: reflection on self, on the being, on the world, on the karmic-results of beings, on the meditators’ domain of meditation, and on the Buddhas’ domain of Buddhas) — he reflects on them by their own characteristic and by their general characteristic. That reflection, again, is twofold: by way of enumeration (joined with the enumeration-procedure for dharma), and by way of weighing (by reasoning, examining merits and faults). Whether he reflects on the aggregate-connected teaching, or on any other teaching stated before, he reflects by these two.

How so? What is called ‘form’?

‘Ten form-sense-bases.’ And that form included in the dharma-sense-base — that is the form-aggregate; the three feelings are the feeling-aggregate. The six perception-bodies are the perception-aggregate. The six volition-bodies are the [Conjectural: formations-aggregate]. The six consciousness-bodies are the consciousness-aggregate. Thus, by way of enumeration-and-counting, he reflects on the aggregates with progressive subdivision; the entry-procedure for this enumeration-mode of reflection is to be understood as boundless.

How does he reflect on the aggregate-teaching by way of examining reasoning?

He examines by four reasonings. Which four? Namely: the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of cause-and-effect, proof-establishing reasoning, and nature-of-things reasoning.

What is the reasoning of dependence?

Dependence is twofold: dependence-of-arising and dependence-of-designation. Of these, dependence-of-arising: the causes and conditions on which the manifestation of the aggregates depends. In that arising of the aggregates, those causes and conditions are depended upon. [Dependence-of-designation:] the sequences of names, words, and letters on which the designation of the aggregates depends. In that designation of the aggregates, those sequences of names, words, and letters are depended upon. This is called dependence-of-arising in the aggregates and dependence-of-designation. Whichever is the dependence-of-arising, and whichever is the dependence-of-designation — that is the reasoning, the procedure, the means for the arising of the aggregates and for the designation of the aggregates; therefore it is called the reasoning of dependence. Reasoning of cause-and-effect: the engagement of the so-arisen aggregates, by means of their own cause and their own condition, in performing their respective proper functions — for example: by the eye, forms are to be seen; by the ear, sounds are to be heard; and so on up to: by the mind, dharmas are to be cognized. [Conversely:] form is to stand in the eye's range; sound in the ear's range; and so on through dharmas in the mind's range — and whatever else is of this kind. The reasoning, the procedure, the means in the respective effects and causes of this or that dharma — this is called reasoning of cause-and-effect.

What is proof-establishing reasoning?

[The propositions:] ‘the aggregates are impermanent, dependently arisen, suffering, empty, non-self’ — these he examines by three means of valid cognition: namely, reliable scripture, direct perception, and inference. By these three means of valid cognition, furnished with proof, which grip the hearts of the wise, delineation and establishment are made — namely, of the impermanence, dependent-arising, suffering, emptiness [Lacuna] of the aggregates. This is called proof-establishing reasoning.

What is nature-of-things reasoning?

By what reason are these aggregates as they are, the world-arrangement as it is? By what reason is earth marked by hardness, water by fluidity, fire by heat, wind by motion?

[By what reason are] the aggregates impermanent, [and] by what reason is nirvāṇa calm?

[Likewise:] form is marked by being affected; feeling by experiencing, perception by recognizing, formations by forming, consciousness by cognizing. This is the nature of these dharmas; their own-nature is just this. This is the nature of things, and this very nature of things is the reasoning, the procedure, the means here. [Whether one asks:] Could this be so, or otherwise, or not at all — in every case the nature of things alone is the recourse; the nature of things alone is the reasoning, [It serves] for the mind to contemplate, for the mind to assent — this is called nature-of-things reasoning. Thus by these four reasonings the aggregate-teaching is examined. And whatever other teaching too — when, by these two modes (enumeration and examining-by-reasoning) it is rightly contemplated — that teaching's [contemplation] is called hearing-and-reflection on the true dharma.

What is non-obstacle?

Non-obstacle is twofold: the inward and the outward. What follows will state the inward and outward obstacles; and by their reverse, non-obstacle is to be understood.

What, then, is the inward obstacle?

When someone [Conjectural: has made no merit] in the past, and, [Conjectural: no merit having been done, does not obtain], from time to time, the requisites conforming to life, namely robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, and provisions; he has intense and prolonged lust, intense and prolonged hatred, intense and prolonged delusion. Or, again, he has previously committed actions that lead to illness, by which he is now full of illness — or in this very life he lives irregularly, whereby his wind, bile, or phlegm frequently rises, or indigestion settles in his body. He becomes [over-]heavy in food, of much business, much to do, frequently in large gatherings. Or fond of work, fond of speech, fond of sleep, fond of company, [Lacuna] or self-aggrandizing, fickle, heedless, dwelling in a bad place. Such are the inward obstacles, to be understood.

What are the outward obstacles?

As when he relies on bad persons, from whom he does not, from time to time, gain conforming advice and instruction; or when he dwells in a bad region — where, when he tries to settle in to dwell, by day there is a fierce, abundant sun-glare, and by night loud noise, the clamor of a great crowd; or sharp contact of wind and sun, or danger even from humans — such are the outward obstacles, to be understood. What is the summary meaning?

In brief, obstacle is threefold: the obstacle of application (prayoga), the obstacle of seclusion (prāvivekya), and the obstacle of retreat (pratisaṁlayana). Of these, the obstacle of application: that obstacle which, when present and manifest, makes him incapable and unfit — completely, entirely — for any application to the wholesome side.

And what is that?

When he becomes afflicted, gravely ill — his wind, bile, or phlegm is frequently disturbed, or indigestion (viṣūcikā) settles in his body; or when a scorpion bites him, or a centipede, or a human harasses him, or a non-human; or when he is not a gainer of robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine, and provisions — such is the obstacle of application, to be understood.

What is the obstacle of seclusion?

When he becomes [over-]heavy in food, of much business, much to do, much to attend to; when he becomes fond of work and delights in it, his mind bent on those various duties; when he becomes fond of speech, and though capable and fit for seclusion, exertion, retreat, and cultivation, he is content with mere recitation and study, fond of sleep, seized by sloth-and-torpor, of lazy disposition, cherishing the pleasure of sleep, the pleasure of [lying on the] side, the pleasure of reclining; when he becomes fond of company with householders and renouncers, engaging in king-talk, or thief-talk, food-talk, drink-talk, [Conjectural: clothes-talk], talk of courtesans, of streets, of the countryside, tales about great ministers, sea-stories — passing the time with such useless talk, delighting in it, much given to frequent gatherings of company, his mind scattered over those various matters, distracted; when he becomes fond of association, and pines for householders and renouncers who are absent; when he becomes fond of diversion (prapañca), delighting in diversion — in the forefront wherever there is something to go to, but in seclusion one who has laid down the burden — such are the dharmas to be understood as the obstacle of seclusion: [dharmas] by which, when present and manifest, it is not easy to dwell in remote lodgings in forests and woods, or at the foot of trees, or in empty huts.

What is the obstacle of retreat?

‘Retreat’ here means śamatha and vipaśyanā. There is the obstacle of śamatha and the obstacle of vipaśyanā. Of these, the obstacle of śamatha: [Conjectural: heedlessness] and dwelling in a bad place. When, in the heedless, sloth-and-torpor wraps the mind round, or he savors mere śamatha, or inclines the mind toward slackness, or there is a darkened state of mind — and as for [obstacles arising] from dwelling in a bad place, there is a disturbance made by humans or from elsewhere, by which his mind is scattered outward. This is the obstacle of śamatha; it is to be understood as an obstacle of retreat. What is the obstacle of vipaśyanā? Namely, self-aggrandizement and fickleness. Of these, self-aggrandizement: when [he thinks] ‘I am of high family, gone forth, not lowly — and others are not so,’ he exalts himself, takes himself up, and deprecates others. Likewise [thinking], ‘I am gone forth from a wealthy family, not needy; I am handsome, well-favored, pleasing; I am much-learned, with a store [of learning]; I am of fair speech, endowed with the faculty of speech — and other monks are not so’ — he exalts himself, takes himself up, and deprecates others. He, holding himself up [thus] — there are monks who are elders, [Conjectural: of long standing], deeply entered into the holy life — does not from time to time inquire of them, does not question them. And they, in turn, do not from time to time open up to him the unopened points, [Conjectural: do not make plain the points not yet made plain], and do not reveal to him the deep meaning of the phrases, having penetrated it well and rightly by wisdom, even as far as the purification of knowledge-and-vision. Thus that self-aggrandizement of his becomes an obstacle — namely, an obstacle to vipaśyanā. Further, when he gains only a slight, scant, mere knowledge-and-vision, a mere dwelling at ease, and by that mere knowledge, that mere vision, he exalts himself, takes himself up, and being thus self-aggrandizing, is content with that much and does not strive further — thus too an obstacle is made for him through self-aggrandizement: namely, an obstacle to vipaśyanā. Or, again, fickle — of unpeaceful faculties, restless faculties, arrogant faculties; corrupted in mind, [Conjectural: speaking what is ill-spoken], doing what is ill-done — he does not firmly reflect on the dharmas, does not reflect resolutely. By this he does not bring vipaśyanā to fullness, does not purify it — thus fickleness too becomes his obstacle, namely an obstacle to vipaśyanā. [In sum:] two dharmas are obstacles to śamatha — namely, heedlessness and dwelling in a bad place. Two dharmas are obstacles to vipaśyanā — namely, self-aggrandizement and fickleness. Whatever is the obstacle to śamatha, and whatever is the obstacle to vipaśyanā — this together is called the obstacle to retreat. And this is the summary meaning of obstacle. This summary meaning together with the previous detailed division — gathered into one is called obstacle. And by the reverse of this obstacle, non-obstacle is to be understood: the absence, departure, non-presence, and non-occurrence of these obstacles is what is called non-obstacle.

What is generosity?

That blameless gift which one gives for the sake of adorning the mind, for the sake of refining the mind, for the sake of the requisites of yoga, and for the attainment of the highest good. Here: who gives, to whom does one give, [Conjectural: what does one give], by what does one give, how does one give, why does one give — by which his giving becomes blameless.

Answer: the giver and the patron give. [Lacuna] — this one is called the giver. The one whose own [thing] is given, and that by one who wants to give, not by one who does not — this one is called the patron (dānapati).

And to whom does one give?

Answer: one gives to four [kinds of recipient]: the suffering, the helpful, the dear, and the distinguished. Of these, ‘the suffering’ means: the pitiable, travelers, beggars, the blind, the deaf, the helpless, those without recourse, those lacking life-requisites — and others of such kind. Helpers are: mother and father, wet-nurse, fosterer, rearer, or those who deliver one from a forest-wilderness, from famine, from fear of enemy armies, from bondage, or from sickness; and those who counsel one's welfare, counsel one's ease, bring about welfare and ease, help in matters that have arisen, share one's joys, share one's sorrows, and do not abandon one in danger — these and others of such kind are the helpers.

Who are the dear?

Acquaintances toward whom one feels love, respect, or devoted speech — those one talks with, knows well, holds dear — and others of such kind. These are called the dear.

Who are the distinguished?

Recluses and brahmins reckoned as good — the non-harming, those who delight in non-harming; the lust-free and those practicing for the subduing of lust; the hatred-free and those practicing for the subduing of hatred; the delusion-free and those practicing for the subduing of delusion. These and others of such kind are called the distinguished.

What, then, does one give?

In brief, what is reckoned as sentient or reckoned as non-sentient — [such a] thing one gives.

What is the sentient-reckoned thing?

Namely the giving of son, female slave, male slave, worker, attendant, elephant, horse, cow, sheep, fowl, woman, man, boy, or girl — or other such things. Or with respect to one's own body — hand, foot, head, flesh, blood, fat, and so on — he offers these. This [self-giving] too is a [Conjectural: sentient-reckoned] gift, [the kind] in which bodhisattvas are seen [to engage]; but it is not the gift intended in the present context. [Here intended is rather:] all those [sentient beings] over whom he has lordship, mastery, and effective power, and whom he is fit to bestow on others. Bestowing them, he makes himself blameless: others do not corrupt their minds on that account, for that reason; and the beings bestowed on others are [Conjectural: not] harmed by them — this is called the blameless gift of a sentient thing.

What is the non-sentient-reckoned thing?

Namely: wealth-things, grain-things, and land-things. Of these, wealth-things: gems, pearls, beryl, conch-shell, stone, coral, agate (aśmagarbha), musāragalva, gold, silver, red gems (lohitikā), [and] right-spiraling [shells]; or any other such jewel, gold, silver, cloth, vessel or furniture, fragrance, or garland — this is called wealth.

What is grain?

Whatever is food or drink: barley, rice, wheat, jujube, horse-gram, sesame, beans, sugar-cane juice, grape juice — or any other such thing. This is called grain.

What is the land-thing?

Namely: a field, a house, a shop, the establishment of a merit-hall or a monastery — or any other such thing. This is called land-thing. Of these, the sentient-reckoned thing [Lacuna] — this is what one gives.

By what does one give?

By volition accompanied by non-greed — that is, mind-formation, mental action — and by the bodily action produced therefrom, and the verbal action, for the relinquishing of the [Conjectural: thing to be given], in one's own continuum or [Lacuna] — by these one gives.

How does one give?

One gives with faith; with scriptural tradition as one's view, seeing the fruit, one gives respectfully; with humble mind, with one's own hand, with both hands; without contempt, at the right time; in such a way that the gift will be of use to others, without injuring [those others] in the giving; having acquired [it] by dharma, fairly, without violence, one gives what is clean, what is fine, what is allowable; [gives in such a way] that others are not made blameworthy [by accepting it], nor is oneself [made blameworthy by giving it]; one gives frequently; having disciplined the stain of stinginess and the stain of hoarding, one gives the gift; with glad mind before the giving, with mind clarified during the giving, and one is [Conjectural: not] remorseful afterward. Thus one gives.

Why does one give?

Answer: one gives out of compassion — namely, to the suffering; out of gratitude — namely, to the helpers; with love, respect, and devotion — namely, to the dear; with the prayer for worldly and otherworldly distinction — namely, to the distinguished. ‘For these reasons one gives’ — so it is said. By these modes — for him, whether householder or renouncer — for the sake of adorning the mind, for the sake of refining the mind, for the sake of the requisites of yoga, for the attainment of the highest good, that gift becomes blameless. This is called generosity.

What is the ornament of the recluse (śramaṇālaṁkāra)?
