Mindfulness of Breathing

The conduct-purifying object for the person of applied-thought-conduct (¶21), and the longest of the five. ¶1–6 define ānāpānasmṛti and describe the breath: in-breath, out-breath, and their inner counterparts (¶2); two causes and two supports (¶3); paths and abodes (¶4); four synonyms (¶5); and two faults of engagement (¶6). ¶7–20 set out the five familiarities (paricaya): counting (¶8–9), entry into the aggregates (¶10), into dependent origination (¶11), and into the truths (¶12–13), and the sixteen aspects (¶14–20), which are the sixteen modes of breath-mindfulness, 'I breathe in mindful …' (14.2–14.17), carried through to the exhaustion of the outflows (20.1–20.4).

What is mindfulness of breathing?

Mindfulness having in-and-out-breaths as its object — this is called ānāpānasmṛti.

What are the two in-breaths?

In-breath (āśvāsa) and inner-in-breath.

What are the two out-breaths?

Out-breath (praśvāsa) and inner-out-breath. The in-breath is the air that, immediately after the out-breath, moves inward as far as the navel region. The inner-in-breath is, when this in-breath has ceased and the out-breath has not yet arisen, the brief wind resembling it that arises at the resting-place in the interval. This is called the inner-in-breath. The out-breath and inner-out-breath should be understood likewise. The distinction here: the air moves outward from the navel region as far as the tip of the mouth or nose, and from there outside.

There are two causes of in-and-out-breaths.

What two?

The relevant kind of karma, and the cavity of the navel region; or alternatively, the cavity of the body that arises from this.

What are the two supports of in-and-out-breathing?

The body and the mind.

Why?

In-and-out-breaths proceed body-supported and mind-supported, and that as is appropriate. If they proceeded body-supported only, they would proceed in beings attained to the unconscious attainment, the cessation attainment, and in beings born among the unconscious gods. If they proceeded mind-supported only, they would proceed in beings who have attained the formless [attainments] or are born in the formless [realms]. If they proceeded body-supported and mind-supported, but not as is appropriate, they would proceed in beings attained to or born in the fourth absorption, and in beings in the kalala (zygote), arbuda (early embryo), and peśī (fleshy mass) stages of gestation. But they do not so proceed. Therefore in-and-out-breaths proceed body-supported and mind-supported, and that as is appropriate.

The two paths of the breaths: What two? In-breaths go downward, out-breaths go upward. The two abodes of the breaths: What two? The gross cavity and the subtle cavity. The gross cavity is from the navel region as far as the door of mouth-and-nose, and from the door of mouth-and-nose as far as the navel cavity.

What is the subtle cavity?

All the pores of the body.

The four synonyms of in-and-out-breaths: What four? Airs, in-and-out, in-breath-and-out-breath, and body-conditioners. Of these, one synonym is shared with other airs — namely, 'air.' The other three are exclusive.

Two faults [arise] for one engaged with the in-and-out-breaths.

What two?

[Conjectural: Over-slack] engagement, and over-tight engagement. In [Conjectural: over-slack] engagement, falling into laziness, the mind is enveloped by sloth-and-torpor or distracted outwardly. In over-tight engagement, bodily disturbance arises, or mental disturbance.

How does bodily disturbance arise?

For one who presses the in-and-out-breaths by forceful restraint, irregular airs proceed in the body. They first stir in such-and-such limbs and minor limbs; these are called sphurakas (throbbings). When these throbbing airs grow, they become rujakas (paining airs); and these, moreover, produce pain in those limbs and minor limbs. This is called bodily disturbance.

How does mental disturbance arise?

The mind is distracted, or is overcome by [Conjectural: severe] dejection and distress. Thus mental disturbance arises.

Five familiarities of this ānāpānasmṛti should be known: counting-familiarity, aggregate-entry-familiarity, dependent-origination-entry-familiarity, truth-entry-familiarity, and sixteen-aspect-familiarity.

What is counting-familiarity?

In brief, counting-familiarity is fourfold: one-by-one counting, two-as-one counting, forward counting, and reverse counting.

What is one-by-one counting?

When the in-breath has entered, with mindfulness bound to the in-and-out-breath, he counts 'one.' When the in-breath has ceased and the out-breath arises and goes out, he counts 'two.' Thus he counts up to ten. This counting is neither too compressed nor too extended. This is called one-by-one counting.

What is two-as-one counting?

When the in-breath has entered and ceased, and the out-breath has arisen and gone out, he counts 'one.' By this method of counting he counts up to ten. This is called two-as-one counting. He combines in-and-out into a single unit and counts it as one — thus called two-as-one counting.

What is forward counting?

By either one-by-one counting or two-as-one counting, he counts forward up to ten. This is called forward counting.

What is reverse counting?

Starting from ten in reverse — nine, eight, seven, six, five — down to one. This is called reverse counting. When, relying on one-by-one counting or two-as-one counting, he has made familiarity with forward and reverse counting, and the mind does not become distracted in the meantime, but counts with undistracted mind — then the further, superior counting is prescribed for him.

What is the superior counting?

By either one-by-one or two-as-one counting, he makes two-as-one and counts. By two-as-one counting, four in-and-out-breaths are one unit. By one-by-one counting, an in-and-out pair is one. Thus he counts up to ten. By such progressive enlargement, he eventually makes a hundred breaths one unit and counts. Then by hundred-as-one counting in sequence he counts up to ten. When this counting-engaged practitioner makes ten-as-one and counts up to ten — as long as he fulfills the ten — by this ten-as-one counting the mind does not become distracted [Conjectural: in the meantime]. By this much, his counting-familiarity is accomplished. Of this counting-engaged practitioner, if the mind becomes distracted in the meantime, he turns back and starts counting again from the beginning, either forward or reverse. When his mind, [Conjectural: through thorough familiarity], of its own accord, mounted on the very path that flows of itself, bound to the in-and-out-breath object, proceeds unbroken and uninterrupted — grasping the in-breath as it proceeds, grasping the state empty of out-breath when the in-breath has ceased, grasping the out-breath as it proceeds, grasping its cessation when it has ceased — unwavering, unmoving, without the aspect of distraction, and with delight — by this much the counting-stage is transcended. Then no more counting is to be done. Mind is no longer bound to any object other than the in-and-out-breaths. The in-and-out-breaths — together with the inner in-breaths and inner out-breaths, and with their states of proceeding and ceasing — are to be followed and characterized. This is called counting-familiarity. This counting-familiarity is prescribed for those of dull faculties. For them this [counting] serves, [Conjectural: where there is distraction], for the abiding of the mind and for the mind's delight; otherwise, without counting, sloth-and-torpor would envelop their mind, or the mind would be distracted outwardly; but for them, engaged in counting, this does not happen. But those of sharp faculties, with keen understanding, have [Conjectural: no] liking for taking up the counting-practice. Once instructed, they penetrate the counting-engagement very quickly, and do not find delight in it. They bind mindfulness to the in-and-out-breath object, and where they proceed, how far they proceed, in what manner they proceed, and when they proceed — all of this they follow and characterize with established mindfulness. This is the form of their engagement. Through familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice of this engagement, bodily pliancy and mental pliancy arise. He attains one-pointedness and comes to delight in the object.

He who has thus made counting-familiarity, by attending to the grasper-and-grasped entity, enters the aggregates. The in-and-out-breaths, and that body which is their basis — attending to these, he enters the form-aggregate. The experience-and-undergoing of those in-and-out-breaths, conjoined with the mindfulness that grasps them — this he enters as the feeling-aggregate. The cognizing of them — this he enters as the perception-aggregate. The mindfulness, the volition, and the discernment present here — this he enters as the formations-aggregate. The mind, mentation, consciousness — this he enters as the consciousness-aggregate. Frequently abiding in this — thus having entered the aggregates — this is called aggregate-entry-familiarity.

When through this he has seen mere aggregate, fully understood it as mere formation, mere entity, then he enters the dependent origination of these very formations.

How does he enter?

He searches and seeks: 'These in-and-out-breaths — supported by what? Conditioned by what?' It occurs to him: 'These in-and-out-breaths are body-supported, body-conditioned; mind-supported, mind-conditioned. The body and the mind — what are they conditioned by?' He enters: 'The body and the mind are conditioned by the life-faculty.' [Lacuna] The previous formation — [Conjectural: what is it conditioned by?] he enters: 'The previous formation is conditioned by ignorance.' Thus: the previous formation is conditioned by ignorance; the life-faculty is conditioned by [Conjectural: the previous] formation; body and consciousness are conditioned by the life-faculty; the in-and-out-breaths are conditioned by body-and-mind. From the cessation of ignorance, cessation of formations. From the cessation of formations, cessation of the life-faculty. From the cessation of the life-faculty, cessation of body-and-mind. From the cessation of body-and-mind, cessation of in-and-out-breaths. Thus he enters dependent origination. Frequently abiding in this, he is said to have made familiarity in the dependent-origination aspect. This is called dependent-origination-entry-familiarity.

Having thus made familiarity in dependent origination: 'These formations which are dependently arisen — they are impermanent' — he enters [this]. Because of impermanence, not having been [Conjectural: they come to be, and having come to be] they pass away. Again, not having been, they come to be; having come to be, they pass away. They are subject to birth, aging, illness, death. What is subject to birth, aging, illness, death — that is suffering. What is suffering — that is not-self, not autonomous, devoid of master. Thus by the aspects of impermanent, suffering, empty, and not-self, he has entered the truth of suffering. Whatever arising there is of these formations — that is suffering, is disease, is a boil; all of it conditioned by craving. Whatever is the complete abandoning of this craving that gives rise to suffering: 'This is peace, this is excellent. Knowing this thus and abiding frequently thus, the complete abandoning of craving will come to be for me.' Thus he enters the truth of arising, the truth of cessation, and the truth of the path. Frequently abiding in this — when he realizes the truths — this is called truth-entry-familiarity.

For one who has thus made familiarity in the truths, with the abandoning of dharmas to be abandoned by seeing, the dharmas to be abandoned by cultivation remain. For their abandoning, he makes the sixteen-aspect-familiarity.

What are the sixteen aspects?

Mindful while breathing in: he trains 'I breathe in mindful.' Mindful while breathing out: he trains 'I breathe out mindful.' Long. Short. Experiencing the whole body. Breathing in experiencing the whole body, he trains 'I breathe in experiencing the whole body.' Breathing out experiencing the whole body, he trains 'I breathe out experiencing the whole body.' Calming the body-conditioner, breathing in, he trains 'I breathe in calming the body-conditioner.' Calming the body-conditioner, breathing out, he trains 'I breathe out calming the body-conditioner.' Experiencing rapture; experiencing happiness — he trains. Experiencing the mind-conditioner; calming the mind-conditioner, breathing in: he trains 'I breathe in calming the mind-conditioner.' Calming the mind-conditioner, breathing out, he trains 'I breathe out calming the mind-conditioner.' Experiencing the mind. Gladdening the mind; concentrating the mind; liberating the mind, breathing in: he trains 'I breathe in liberating the mind.' Liberating the mind, breathing out, he trains 'I breathe out liberating the mind.' Contemplating impermanence; contemplating abandoning; contemplating dispassion; breathing in: he trains 'I breathe in contemplating cessation.' Contemplating cessation, breathing out, he trains 'I breathe out contemplating cessation.'

What is the distinction of these?

Of the aspects: the trainee who has [Conjectural: seen the footing] is an obtainer of the four foundations of mindfulness, and, with the in-and-out-breath as object, he undertakes attention for the abandoning of the remaining fetters. Therefore he says 'mindful.' Breathing in, he trains 'I breathe in mindful.' When he takes the in-breath or out-breath as object, he trains 'I breathe in long, I breathe out long.' When he makes the inner-in-breath or inner-out-breath the object, he trains 'I breathe in short, I breathe out short.' Thus indeed the in-and-out-breaths proceed long; the inner-in-breaths and inner-out-breaths are short, and proceed accordingly. Just so he characterizes them, and knows. When he resolves upon the in-and-out-breaths as having reached the subtle cavity and entered into diffusion throughout the body, he makes [the whole body] the object. When the out-breath and the inner-out-breath have ceased, and the in-breath and the inner-in-breath have not yet arisen, he makes the state empty of out-breath and in-breath — removed from them, separated from them [Damaged] — the object. At that time, calming the body-conditioner, breathing in — he trains 'I breathe in calming the body-conditioner.' Calming the body-conditioner, breathing out, he trains 'I breathe out calming the body-conditioner.' Moreover, through his familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice — those rough, unpleasant-to-touch in-and-out-breaths that proceeded for one not previously familiarized — for the familiarized one, other breaths proceed, soft and pleasant to the touch. Therefore he says, 'calming the body-conditioner, I breathe in' — he trains.

Engaged in ānāpānasmṛti in this manner, if he is one who attains the first or the second absorption — at that time, 'experiencing rapture, breathing in, he trains: experiencing rapture, I breathe in.' If he is one who attains the third absorption without rapture, at that time he is 'experiencing happiness.' Beyond the third absorption, ānāpānasmṛti is no longer applicable. Hence [the practice] is declared to be comprised as far as the third absorption.

Of this practitioner — experiencing rapture, or experiencing happiness — if at some time or other, from a lapse of mindfulness, there arises [the thought]: 'I am,' or 'this very I am,' 'I shall be' or 'I shall not be,' 'I shall be with form, I shall be without form,' 'percipient, non-percipient,' 'I shall be neither percipient nor non-percipient' — when in this way there arises, accompanied by deluded perception and volition, stirring, [Conjectural: conceiving], proliferation, fabrication, craving, that which has arisen, he penetrates quickly with discernment. He does not tolerate it; he abandons it; he removes it; he extinguishes it. Thus, 'experiencing the mind-conditioner, calming the mind-conditioner, breathing in, I breathe in calming the mind-conditioner' — he trains.

If he attains the basic first, second, or third absorption — and he is then necessarily an attainer of the anāgamya, the preparatory stage of the first absorption — taking that as support, he reviews his arisen mind: with passion or without passion; with hatred or without; with delusion or without; contracted, sunk, uplifted, agitated or not agitated, calmed or not calmed, concentrated or not concentrated, well-cultivated or not well-cultivated, liberated or not liberated — thus he knows the mind as it actually is. He experiences it. Therefore 'experiencing the mind.' When the mind has sunk into the hindrance of sloth-and-torpor as he is collecting [it] inwardly — then he displays one or another gladdening object, rouses, energizes, [Conjectural: gladdens] the mind. Therefore 'gladdening the mind.' When he sees that it is [Conjectural: covered] by the hindrance of agitation or the hindrance of remorse as he is exerting [it] too far — then he displays one or another gladdening object and settles it inwardly. He calms, concentrates. Therefore 'concentrating the mind.' When through familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice that mind has become distanced from the active occurrence of hindrances, purified from the hindrances, liberated — therefore 'liberating the mind, breathing in, I breathe in liberating the mind' — he trains.

Of the one whose mind is liberated from the hindrances obstructive to path-cultivation, the latent tendencies remain. They are to be abandoned. For their abandoning, he brings the path to presence — namely, he examines carefully, properly, and methodically the impermanence of formations. Therefore 'contemplating impermanence.' Previously, there was śamatha engagement supported by the first, second, or third absorption, or by the anāgamya. Now he applies himself directly to the vipaśyanā of contemplating impermanence. Thus his mind, perfumed by śamatha-vipaśyanā, is liberated in the elements, namely, from the latent tendencies.

What are the elements?

The element of abandoning, the element of dispassion, and the element of cessation. The abandoning of all formations to be abandoned by seeing — that is the abandoning-element. The abandoning of all formations to be abandoned by cultivation — that is the dispassion-element. The cessation of all bases of clinging — that is the cessation-element. Thus, attending to the three elements as peace, as security, as health, he cultivates śamatha-vipaśyanā. Through familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice, the mind is liberated from the remaining cultivation-abandonable defilements. Therefore 'contemplating abandoning, contemplating dispassion, contemplating cessation, breathing in, I breathe in contemplating cessation' — he trains.

Thus, when the defilements abandonable by seeing and by cultivation have been abandoned, he becomes an arhat, one whose outflows are exhausted. There is nothing further to be done by him. nor any [Conjectural: adding to] what has been done. This is called the sixteen-aspect-familiarity. And this fivefold familiarity — this is called his ānāpānasmṛti.

Where an applied-thought-conduct person is engaged, he engages with a liking for taking it up. This object involves activity; [Conjectural: where there is distraction], [it is] inward, one's own, very near — whereby for one engaging there, the agitation of applied thought does not arise. The mind quickly settles on the object; it delights; it comes to be. This is the fivefold conduct-purifying object for the applied-thought-conduct person.
