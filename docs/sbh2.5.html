Analysis of Elements

Śrāvakabhūmi · Sanskrit (Shukla 1973) ↔ English (1 rendering)
← All topicsView English only →

The fourth of the five carita-viśodhana practices, corresponding to the māna-carita (conceit-temperament) practitioner. Analysis of the six elements — pṛthivī (earth), ap (water), tejas (fire), vāyu (air), ākāśa (space), vijñāna (consciousness) — each treated in inward (ādhyātmika) and outward (bāhya) aspect. The body-parts list, the piṇḍa-saṁjñā (lump-perception) dissolution, and the temperament rationale for why conceit yields to elemental analysis (the body that conceit clings to disclosed as a temporary aggregate of impersonal elements).

Analysis of Elements
sbh2.5:1.1
tatra dhātuprabhedaḥ katamaḥ |
What is the analysis of elements?
sbh2.5:1.2
tadyathā ṣaḍ dhātavaḥ |
Namely, the six elements:
sbh2.5:1.3
pṛthivīdhāturabdhātustejodhāturvāyudhāturākāśadhāturvijñānadhātuśca |
the earth element, water element, fire element, wind element, space element, and consciousness element.
Asanga gives a six-element scheme rather than the four-great-element analysis dominant in the Theravada tradition. The six adds space (ākāśa) and consciousness (vijñāna), giving a complete decomposition of the embodied person, not just the physical body. This follows MN 140 (Dhātuvibhaṅga) and MN 62 (Mahā-Rāhulovāda) rather than VSM Ch 11.
sbh2.5:2.1
tatra pṛthivīdhāturdvividhaḥ |
The earth element is twofold:
sbh2.5:2.2
ādhyātmiko bāhyaśca |
inward and outward.
sbh2.5:2.3
tatrādhyātmiko yadasminkāye adhyātmaṁ pratyātmaṁ khakkhaṭaṁ kharataramupādattaṁ |
The inward earth element: whatever in this body, internally, individually, is hard or harsh, taken up [into experience].
upādatta — 'taken up, appropriated' — is technical. It marks the element-instance that has become part of a being through the appropriation-process of consciousness. The body's hard parts are upādatta earth; external rocks are not. The practice is to see that, whether or not upādatta, it is still earth.
sbh2.5:2.4
bāhyaḥ punaḥ pṛthivīdhāturyadbāhyaṁ khakkhaṭaṁ kharagatamupagatamupādattaṁ |
The outward earth element: whatever externally is hard, has gone to hardness, has come to be taken up.
sbh2.5:2.5
sa punarādhyātmikapṛthivīdhātuḥ katamaḥ |
What is the inward earth element specifically?
sbh2.5:2.6
tadyathā keśā, romāṇi, nakhā, dantā, rajo, malaṁ, tvaṅmāṁsamasthi, snāyuḥ, sirā, vṛkkaṁ, hṛdayaṁ, plīhakaṁ, klomamantrāṇyantraguṇāḥ |
Hair of the head, body hair, nails, teeth, dust, dirt, skin, flesh, bone, sinew, vein, kidney, heart, spleen, lungs, intestines, mesentery;
Same body-parts list as the aśubhā section, but here framed as instances of the earth element rather than objects of disgust. A key Yogācāra point: the same observation can serve as either aśubhā (antidote to lust) or dhātu-prabheda (antidote to pride), depending on how it is held.

sbh2.5:4.1

What is the fire element?
sbh2.5:4.2

The fire element is twofold: inward and outward.
sbh2.5:4.3

What is the inward fire element?
sbh2.5:4.4
yadadhyātmaṁ pratyātmaṁ tejastejogatamūṣmā ūṣmāgatamupagatamupādattaṁ |
Whatever, internally, individually, is fire or heat, has gone to fire or heat, has come to be taken up.
sbh2.5:4.5
tadyathā yadasminkāye tejo yenāyaṁ kāya ātapyate |
Namely: the fire in this body by which the body is heated,
Four functions of inward fire enumerated: general bodily warmth (ātapyate), feverish heat (saṁtapyate), burning sensation (paritapyate), and digestive heat (paripākaṁ gacchati). The last function is the standard pakkācchaka of the Pali commentaries.

sbh2.5:4.8
yasya cotsadatvāt jārito jārita iti saṅkhyāṁ gacchati |

yadbāhyaṁ tejastejogatamūṣmā ūṣmāgatamupagatamupādattaṁ |
Whatever externally is fire or heat, has gone to fire or heat, has come to be taken up.
sbh2.5:4.11
tatpunaryanmanuṣyā araṇīsahagatakebhyo gomayacūrṇebhyaḥ samanveṣante |

And anything else of this kind.

sbh2.5:7.1
vijñānadhātuḥ katamaḥ |
What is the consciousness element?
sbh2.5:7.2
yaccakṣurvijñānaṁ śrotraghrāṇajihvākāyamanovijñānaṁ |
Eye-consciousness; ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, mind-consciousness;
sbh2.5:7.3
tatpunaścittaṁ manovijñānaṁ ca |
That, further, is mind (citta) and mind-consciousness (mano-vijñāna).
The line does not introduce a full later Yogācāra eight-consciousness scheme. After listing the six sense-consciousnesses, it glosses the consciousness element more generally as citta and mano-vijñāna. Treat this as a compact consciousness-element definition, not as a separate doctrinal taxonomy of citta/manas/vijñāna.
sbh2.5:7.4
ayamucyate vijñānadhātuḥ |
This is called the consciousness element.
sbh2.5:8.1
tatra mānacaritaḥ pudgala imaṁ dhātuprabhedaṁ manasi kurvan kāye piṇḍasaṁjñāṁ vibhāvayati |
The conceit-conduct person, attending to this analysis of elements, dispels the perception of the body as a unified lump.
Māna-carita is kept as conceit-conduct, matching the conduct-label style in the surrounding practice chapters. Piṇḍa-saṁjñā is the perception of the body as one solid thing, the implicit 'this is me' that conceit hooks into. Vibhāvayati means 'dispels, breaks down' this lump-perception. Once the body is perceived as six elements in their inward and outward forms, there is no unified entity to claim ownership of.
sbh2.5:8.2
aśubhasaṁjñāṁ ca pratilabhate |
He also obtains the perception of unattractiveness;
The practice has a useful side-effect: aśubha-saṁjñā arises naturally as well. Dhātu-prabheda is dual-purpose — primary antidote to pride, secondary antidote to lust. A practitioner whose primary issue is pride but who also struggles with attraction can use this practice as covering both grounds.
