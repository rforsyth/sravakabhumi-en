The Pervasive Object

Śrāvakabhūmi · Sanskrit (Shukla 1973) ↔ English (1 rendering)
← All topicsView English only →

The first of the four ālambana (objects of meditative attention) introduced in Yogasthāna II. The 'pervasive' object is the universal one — common to all practitioners regardless of temperament — and it provides the framework within which the other three (carita-viśodhana, kauśalya, kleśa-viśodhana) operate. Comprised of four kinds: savikalpa-pratibimba (image with conceptual construction), nirvikalpa-pratibimba (image without conceptual construction), vastu-paryantatā (the full extent of the substance), and kārya-pariniṣpatti (the consummation of the work).

The Revata passage below explicitly has rūpāṇāṁ śāntatve at 13.2 and ārūpya-śāntatāyām at 13.3. Translation therefore gives 'calmness' while leaving the Sanskrit JSON unchanged.

The central Yogācāra claim: the meditator does not engage with an actual exterior thing, but with a mental image — an adhimokṣa-anubhava (resolve-experience) produced by attention grounded in the concentrated plane.

sbh2.1:7.1

What is the substance’s full extent [vastu-paryantatā]?
sbh2.1:7.2
yadālambanasya yāvadbhāvikatā yathāvadbhāvikatā ca |
It is the yāvad-bhāvikatā (as-far-as-it-goes-ness) of the object, and its yathāvad-bhāvikatā (as-it-actually-is-ness).
yāvad-bhāvikatā / yathāvad-bhāvikatā — 'as-far-as-it-goes-ness' / 'as-it-actually-is-ness.' The full reach of contemplative engagement has two halves: the scope of the substance (how far it extends) and its nature (how it actually is).
sbh2.1:7.3

sbh2.1:7.4

[That] beyond which there is no further form-aggregate, feeling-aggregate, perception-aggregate, formations-aggregate, or consciousness-aggregate —

the comprehensive gathering of all conditioned substances by the five [aggregates]; the comprehensive gathering of all dharmas by the elements and bases; the comprehensive gathering of all substances-to-be-known

What is yathāvad-bhāvikatā?
sbh2.1:7.8
yā ālambanasya bhūtatā |
The actuality [bhūtatā] of the object
sbh2.1:7.9
tathatā ca tasṛbhiryuktibhiḥ |
and its suchness [tathatā], endowed with the reasonings —
The root has tasṛbhir yuktibhiḥ ('by three reasonings'), but the next line lists four: dependence, cause-and-effect, proof-establishing, and nature-of-things. Translation leaves the number implicit at 7.9 and lets the explicit fourfold list at 7.11 govern.
sbh2.1:7.10
yuktyupetatā |
its reasoning-endowed-ness;
sbh2.1:7.11
yadutāpekṣāyuktyā, kāryakāraṇayuktyā | upapattisādhanayuktyā | dharmatāyuktyā ca |
namely, by dependence-reasoning [apekṣā-yukti], cause-and-effect reasoning [kārya-kāraṇa-yukti], proof-establishing reasoning [upapatti-sādhana-yukti], and nature-of-things reasoning [dharmatā-yukti].
The four yukti (reasonings) — apekṣā-yukti (dependence-reasoning: by what does an effect depend on its cause), kārya-kāraṇa-yukti (cause-and-effect reasoning: what cause yields what effect), upapatti-sādhana-yukti (proof-establishing reasoning: by means of valid cognition), and dharmatā-yukti (nature-of-things reasoning: that things are just so by their own nature) — are the canonical Yogācāra epistemological fourfold. Same list and same definitions in [Saṃdhinirmocana](https://www.84000.co/translation/toh106) X.7 and Asaṅga's Abhidharmasamuccaya. The four together exhaust how yathāvad-bhāvikatā (as-it-is-ness) is investigated: causally, dependence-wise, by proof, and by appeal to the nature of things itself (which terminates the regress of reasoning). The chapter's use here is not merely epistemological scaffolding but a claim that meditative penetration of any jñeya-vastu must finally be checked against all four kinds of reasoning to count as vastu-paryantatā.
sbh2.1:7.12

Thus the yāvad-bhāvikatā and yathāvad-bhāvikatā of the object, summarized as one, is called the full extent of the substance.

sbh2.1:8.5

By that fulfillment, the support [āśraya] is transformed [parivartate].
āśraya-parivṛtti — 'transformation of the support' or 'fundamental turning of the substrate.' One of the most important technical terms in Yogācāra. It names the structural change in the practitioner's psychophysical basis that occurs when meditative practice fully matures. Not a state-attainment; a change in the substrate that produces states.
sbh2.1:8.6

sbh2.1:8.7

Through the transformation of the support [āśraya-parivṛtti], transcending the image, there arises in that very substance-to-be-known direct knowing-and-seeing without conceptual construction.
The result: the practitioner transcends the image (pratibimbam atikramya) and reaches direct, non-conceptual knowing-and-seeing (nirvikalpaṁ pratyakṣaṁ jñāna-darśanam) in the very substance-to-be-known. The image was a vehicle; once it has done its work, it is transcended.

The five practices and five temperaments are not Asanga's innovation but canonical: rāga → aśubhā, dveṣa → maitrī, moha → idaṁ-pratyayatā, māna → dhātu-prabheda, vitarka → ānāpāna-smṛti.
