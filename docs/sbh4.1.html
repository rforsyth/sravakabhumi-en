The Seven Attentions

Śrāvakabhūmi · Sanskrit (Shukla 1973) ↔ English (1 rendering)
← All topicsView English only →

The canonical sapta-manaskāra (seven attentions) framework for the desire-realm-to-first-jhāna transition. The seven: lakṣaṇa-pratisaṁvedī (recognizing the characteristic), ādhimokṣika (resolved), prāvivekya (giving rise to seclusion), rati-saṁgrāhaka (gathering joy), mīmāṁsā (investigative), prayoga-niṣṭha (culminating practice), prayoga-niṣṭhā-phala (the fruit of culminating practice). Two-paths framing (laukika / lokottara) distinguishes the worldly mundane-path traveler from the supramundane.

The seven attentions begin from a specific qualified state. The practitioner has attained: labdha-manaskāra, cultivated praśrabdhi and cittaikāgratā, attention is dṛṣṭi-sthira (view-stable) and khara (forceful), object-resolve is pariśrabdha (relaxed), and the śamatha-vipaśyanā signs have been grasped. Roughly the late stages of the nine mental abidings (stages 7–8).

The seven attentions are accessible to anyone with the contemplative skill; jhāna-attainment doesn't itself imply Buddhist enlightenment.

The engine of the mundane path: comparative recognition driving release.

The characteristic move of the entire seven-attention sequence: see the grossness of where you are; see the calmness of where you're going. The practitioner is not willing themselves into the first dhyāna — they are recognizing the actual qualitative difference, and that recognition produces the release of attachment that allows transition.

Critical Yogācāra refinement: sense-pleasures are double. Kleśa-kāma is the defilement-aspect (the craving). Vastu-kāma is the substance-aspect (the objects themselves — sights, sounds, etc.). The practice of kāma-vairāgya is primarily directed at kleśa-kāma, not vastu-kāma. The ascetic is not trying to make the world go away; they are trying to make their craving for the world go away.

Asanga anchors his framework in the Buddha's own similes.

Each is not a source of pleasure but a counter-action to suffering: food counters hunger, clothing counters cold/heat-and-shame, bedding counters fatigue, medicine counters illness. The Yogācāra version of the standard paccavekkhana (reflection on the four requisites).

The fourth attention manages emerging-jhāna-factors. As prīti-sukha characteristic of jhāna emerges, two failure-modes can derail: styāna-middha (sloth, sinking into bliss) and auddhatya (agitation, excitement). Remedies: prasadanīya manaskāra (inspiring) when sinking, saṁvejanīya manaskāra (uplifting) when drooping.

'My mind is not properly dispassionate, not liberated from sense-pleasures. My mind is restrained by formations like water held by a dam,

restrained by *dharmatā*.
sbh4.1:17.11
yannvahaṁ bhūyasyā mātrayā tasyānuśayasyāśeṣaprahāṇāya bhūyasyā mātrayā prahāṇārāmo vihareyaṁ |
Let me, by greater measure, abide more delighting-in-abandonment for the complete abandoning of that latent tendency.'
One of the most psychologically sophisticated passages in classical Buddhist meditation literature. The fourth attention has produced apparent dispassion, but Asanga warns: this could be real dispassion or suppressed desire held by the meditation-state itself. The practitioner runs an experimental test: deliberately attend to an attractive sign and watch what the mind does. Real abandonment shows equanimity; false abandonment shows sevanā-nimna/pravaṇa/prābhāra — three escalating signs of inclination.
sbh4.1:17.12

[Delighting-in-]cultivation.
sbh4.1:17.13

This is called the examination attention.
Vāri-vad dhṛtaṁ — 'held like water.' The mind is currently dispassionate but only because the meditative apparatus is holding the desire back, like water held by a dam. Remove the dam, and the desire flows again. A diagnostic protocol for distinguishing real progress from suppressed defilement.

Complete provisional release. The mind has fully disengaged from all desire-realm defilements through the attentions, but the bīja (seed) is not yet uprooted. The release is tāvat-kālika — time-bound. The precise definition of the difference between laukika (mundane) and lokottara (supramundane) liberation. A practitioner who reports 'I've gotten over my desire-realm cravings' has attained prayoga-niṣṭha — real but not final.

The seventh attention is the first dhyāna. It is not a step toward first dhyāna — it is the attention in first dhyāna. The whole seven-attention sequence has been the staged transition; the seventh attention is arrival.

Asanga shows that the seven-attention sequence yields exactly the canonical first dhyāna.

The single algorithm carries the practitioner from the desire-realm all the way to the peak of existence.
