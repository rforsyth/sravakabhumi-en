The Nine Stages of Mental Abiding

Śrāvakabhūmi · Sanskrit (Shukla 1973) ↔ English (1 rendering)
← All topicsView English only →

The classical formulation of the nine cittasthiti (mental abiding) stages: sthāpayati (places), saṁsthāpayati (places continuously), avasthāpayati (places again), upasthāpayati (places closely), damayati (tames), śamayati (pacifies), vyupaśamayati (fully pacifies), ekotīkaroti (makes single-pointed), samādhatte (concentrates). Together with the six powers (ṣaḍ-vidha-bala) that traverse them, the four modes of mental engagement (catur-vidha manaskāra), and the bridge to vipaśyanā, this is the canonical śamatha map adopted across the Buddhist meditation tradition (cited by Asaṅga's brother Vasubandhu, by the Tibetan stages-of-the-path literature, and through modern lineages).

sbh3.1:2.1
tatra navākārā cittasthitiḥ katamā |
What is the nine-fold mental abiding?
sbh3.1:2.2
iha bhikṣuradhyātmameva cittaṁ sthāpayati |
Here a monk places [sthāpayati] the mind inwardly,
The nine cittasthiti are named by nine distinct Sanskrit verbs sharing the root √sthā (to stand, to place), prefixed to mark stages: sthāpayati (places — initial binding), saṁsthāpayati (places continuously — refines the binding), avasthāpayati (places again — recovers after drift), upasthāpayati (places closely — secures after recovery), damayati (tames — checks the pull of distracting signs), śamayati (pacifies — checks the pull of distracting thoughts/defilements), vyupaśamayati (fully pacifies — refuses to tolerate occurrences when they slip through), ekotī-karoti (makes single-pointed — sustains uninterrupted flow with effort), samādhatte (concentrates — sustains the same flow effortlessly). The progression is not nine distinct meditation techniques but nine increasingly subtle stages of the same mind-binding operation. Adopted verbatim by Kamalaśīla's Bhāvanākrama and through it into the entire Tibetan lam rim literature; cited (with the same nine names) in Asaṅga's Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra XIV.

sbh3.1:3.1
kathaṁ sthāpayati |
How does he place [the mind]?
sbh3.1:3.2
sarvabāhyebhya ālambanebhyaḥ pratisaṁkṣipyādhyātmamavikṣepāyopanibaghnāti |
Withdrawing it from all external objects, he ties it inwardly toward non-distraction.
upanibadhnāti literally means 'binds' or 'ties up' — the mind is tied to the object the way a rope is tied to a stake. More active than the English 'places'.
sbh3.1:3.3

That first binding [of the mind] against distraction — this is placement.
sbh3.1:4.1
kathaṁ saṁsthāpayati |
How does he place it continuously?
sbh3.1:4.2
tatprathamopanibaddhaṁ yadeva cittaṁ tadbalamaudārikamasaṁsthitamaparisaṁsthitaṁ tasminnevālambane pravarddhanayogena prasādayogena sābhinigrahaṁ sūkṣmīkurvan abhisaṁkṣipan saṁsthāpayati |
That mind first bound is strong, gross, unsettled, and not fully settled. On that very same object, through the practice of strengthening and the practice of clarification, with deliberate restraint — making it subtle and gathering it together — he places it continuously.
The first stage's binding is described as balam audārikam asaṁsthitam — strong, gross, unsettled. Stage 2 refines that crude holding into something more subtle and gathered, on the same object.

sbh3.1:5.2

If, while he is placing the mind,
sbh3.1:5.3

The local stage-3 definition breaks off after the condition: if the mind becomes outwardly distracted through loss of mindfulness. The later power-mapping at 12.5 supplies the missing function: through mindfulness he draws the bound mind back and places it again (pratisarann avasthāpayati). The English marks the reconstruction rather than leaving the stage definition as a fragment.

sbh3.1:9.1

How does he make [the mind] single-pointed?
sbh3.1:9.2
sābhisaṁskāraṁ niścchidraṁ nirantaraṁ samādhipravāhamavasthāpayatyevamekotīkaroti |
With deliberate effort, he establishes a continuous, unbroken, uninterrupted flow of samādhi — thus he makes it single-pointed.
Three adjectives describe the flow at this stage: niścchidra (without gaps), nirantara (uninterrupted), and the flow is of samādhi itself. The qualifier sābhisaṁskāraṁ — 'with effort' — distinguishes stage 8 from stage 9.

Through familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice, [he attains] effortless flow,
anābhoga and anabhisaṁskāra — 'without exertion' and 'without deliberate effort.' Svarasa-vāhana is rendered as 'self-sustaining flow' rather than a mechanical 'borne by its own essence.' The decisive contrast with stage 8 is that there is no new technique at stage 9; just the natural maturing of what was practiced at stage 8.

sbh3.1:11.1
tatra ṣaḍvidhabalairnavākārā cittasthitiḥ sampadyate |
The nine-fold mental abiding is accomplished through the six kinds of power [ṣaḍ-vidha-bala] —
sbh3.1:11.2
tadyathā śrutacintābalena | smṛtibalena | vīryabalena | paricayabalena ca |
namely the power of hearing [śruta-bala] and the power of thinking [cintā-bala], the power of mindfulness [smṛti-bala] [and the power of clear comprehension (samprajanya-bala)], the power of diligence [vīrya-bala], and the power of familiarity [paricaya-bala].
The original enumeration at 11.2 lists only four — 'hearing-and-thinking,' mindfulness, diligence, familiarity — but the body of the chapter (¶12) clearly distinguishes six: śruta-bala and cintā-bala (separately at 12.1–4 via the 'taking what he has heard and what he has reflected on'), smṛti-bala (12.5–6), samprajanya-bala (12.7–8), vīrya-bala (12.9–11), and paricaya-bala (12.12). Restored in brackets. The same six-fold list — with the same internal mapping — appears in Asaṅga's Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra XIV.13 and in Kamalaśīla's Bhāvanākrama III. Note the deliberate pairing: 1 power → 2 stages; 1 power → 2 stages; 1 power → 2 stages; 1 power → 2 stages; 1 power → 1 stage; 1 power → 1 stage — giving 6×(2,2,2,2,1,1) = 9.

sbh3.1:13.1
tatra navākārāyāṁ cittasthitau catvāro manaskārā veditavyāḥ |
In the nine-fold mental abiding, four modes of mental engagement should be known:
sbh3.1:13.2
balavāhanaḥ sacchidravāhano niścchidravāhanaḥ | anābhogavāhanaśca |
forceful engagement, interrupted engagement, uninterrupted engagement, and effortless engagement.
The four manaskāra — bala-vāhana (forceful), sacchidra-vāhana (interrupted), niścchidra-vāhana (uninterrupted), anābhoga-vāhana (effortless) — are mapped one-to-many onto the nine cittasthiti at 14.1–4: stages 1–2 are forceful, stages 3–7 are interrupted, stage 8 is uninterrupted, stage 9 is effortless. This mapping is the structural counterpart to [sbh2.0:59.126](sbh2.0.html#sbh2.0:59.126), where the same four manaskāra were introduced abstractly; here they are tied concretely to the nine stages. The same four reappear at the corresponding vipaśyanā stages at 15.2–3 — calm and insight share the same architecture of mental engagement. The English drops a mechanical '-borne' calque for readability while preserving the Sanskrit labels in brackets.
sbh3.1:14.1
tatra sthāpayataḥ, saṁsthāpayato balavāhano manaskāraḥ |
In one who is placing and placing continuously, the mode of engagement is forceful (bala-vāhana).
sbh3.1:14.2
avasthāpayata, upasthāpayato, damayataḥ, śamayataḥ, vyupaśamayataḥ, sacchidravāhano manaskāraḥ |
In one who is placing again, placing closely, taming, pacifying, or fully pacifying, the mode of engagement is interrupted (sacchidra-vāhana).
sbh3.1:14.3
ekotīkurvato niścchidravāhano manaskāraḥ |
In one who is making single-pointed, the mode of engagement is uninterrupted (niścchidra-vāhana).
sbh3.1:14.4
samādadhataḥ | anābhogavāhano manaskāro bhavati |
In one who is concentrating, the mode of engagement is effortless (anābhoga-vāhana).
sbh3.1:15.1
evamete manaskārāyāṁ cittasthitau śamathapakṣyā bhavanti |
Thus these [four modes] in mental abiding belong to the śamatha side.
sbh3.1:15.2
yaḥ punarevamadhyātmaṁ cetaḥśamathasya lābhī vipaśyanāyāṁ prayujyate |
But for one who, having attained inward calm-abiding, then applies himself to vipaśyanā —
The hinge from śamatha to vipaśyanā is structural, not technical: the same four manaskāra are reused; the same nine-stage progression applies. What differs is the object. On the śamatha side, the object is a single chosen meditation-object held with one-pointedness; on the vipaśyanā side, the object is the four-fold support of wisdom (which the chapter does not unpack here — it is treated in [sbh3.2](sbh3.2.html)). The bridge is one of the chapter's most important architectural moves: it positions the nine-stage cittasthiti as not merely a śamatha-development scheme but as the general form of attention-stabilization, applicable equally to insight cultivation.
sbh3.1:15.3
tasyaita eva catvāro manaskārā vipaśyanāpakṣyā bhavanti | |
for him these very same four modes belong to the vipaśyanā side.
