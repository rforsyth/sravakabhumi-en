The Vipaśyanā Method

Śrāvakabhūmi · English working translation
← All topicsView side-by-side ↔

The fourfold vipaśyanā (insight) method — vicinoti (investigates), pravicinoti (thoroughly investigates), parivitarkayati (reflects upon), parimīmāṁsām āpadyate (engages in full reflection). The chapter unfolds the framework through three gateways (savikalpa-pratibimba, nirvikalpa-pratibimba, vastu-paryantatā), six investigation-subjects (meaning, things, characteristics, sides, time, reasoning), and four reasonings (dependence, function, demonstration, real nature). Five illustrations apply the method to specific objects: aśubhā, maitrī, idaṁ-pratyayatā, dhātu-prabheda, ānāpānasmṛti — the first two are translated; the remaining three are present in Sanskrit but not yet rendered in English.

*Vipaśyanā* is fourfold. What four? A monk investigates phenomena, investigates closely, deliberates, and engages in thorough examination — taking inward calm-abiding as his support.

How does he investigate? He investigates the purifying-conduct object, the skillful object, or the defilement-purifying object — under the aspect of 'as far as it goes,' and 'as it actually is.' He investigates closely by way of conceptual attention joined with discernment. Making it the basis, he deliberates, considers, and engages in thorough examination.

This *vipaśyanā* should be known as having three gateways and six investigation-subjects. What are the three gateways? *Vipaśyanā* that follows the sign alone; *vipaśyanā* that follows seeking, and seeking-as-sought; *vipaśyanā* that follows reviewing. Following the sign alone: when, by what he has heard and grasped, he attends to the dharma of instruction with unconcentrated attention. He does not think it through, weigh it, or examine it. This is *vipaśyanā* following the sign alone. When he then thinks it through, weighs it, examines it, then it is *vipaśyanā* following seeking. When, having weighed and examined, he then reviews the conclusions just as established, then it is *vipaśyanā* following reviewing. This is the threefold-gateway *vipaśyanā*.

What are the six investigation-subjects? While seeking, he seeks six things: meaning, substance, characteristic, side, time, and reasoning. Having sought these, he reviews them.

How does he seek meaning? 'The meaning of this teaching is this; the meaning of this teaching is that' — thus he seeks meaning. How does he seek substance? Substance is twofold: inward and outward. Thus he seeks substance. How does he seek characteristic? Twofold: specific characteristic and general characteristic. Thus he seeks characteristic. How does he seek side? Side is twofold: dark side and bright side. The dark side is investigated as fault, as danger. The bright side as quality and benefit. Thus he seeks side. How does he seek time? There are three times: past, future, present. 'Thus it was in the past time.' 'Thus it will be', 'in the future time.' 'Thus it is now,' 'in the present time' — so he seeks time. How does he seek reasoning? There are four reasonings: dependence-reasoning, cause-and-effect reasoning, proof-establishing reasoning, and nature-of-things reasoning. By dependence-reasoning he investigates the conventional as conventional, the ultimate as ultimate, and the causal-condition as causal-condition. Thus he seeks. By cause-and-effect reasoning he investigates the function of phenomena: 'This is the dharma; this is its function; this is its function.' By proof-establishing reasoning he investigates by means of the three *pramāṇas* (valid means of knowing): trustworthy testimony, inference, and direct perception. 'Is there a self? Is there a self here, or not? Is it apprehended by direct perception, or not? Is it established by inference, or not?' By nature-of-things reasoning he resolves upon the suchness of phenomena, the established nature of things, the unthinkable nature of things, the abiding nature of things — he does not further think it through, does not further discriminate. Thus he seeks reasoning.

This is the *vipaśyanā* with the six investigation-subjects and three gateways, in summary. By this, all *vipaśyanā* is comprehended. Why are six divisions established? It is said: Three kinds of comprehension are intended: comprehension of the meaning of what is taught; comprehension of 'as far as it goes' of the substance; and comprehension of 'as it actually is.' The investigation of meaning yields comprehension of what is taught. The investigation of substance and the investigation of specific characteristic together yield comprehension of how far the substance extends. The investigation of general characteristic, of side, of time and reasoning together yield comprehension of how things actually are. This much is to be known by the practitioner: namely, the meaning of what is taught; and the *yāvad-bhāvikatā* of the substance to be known, and the *yathāvad-bhāvikatā*.

The practitioner engaged in foulness investigates the six investigation-subjects. It is said: Taking what he has heard and grasped about foulness as governing, with attention concentrated on its plane, he comprehends the meaning thus: 'This foulness — this is repulsive, this is disgusting, this is foul-smelling, this is putrid.' By such aspects [there is] comprehension of the meaning of the foulness-teaching previously heard. Thus he investigates the meaning by way of foulness.

How does he investigate the substance? Comprehending the meaning, he sees this foulness as established in two locations: inwardly and outwardly.

How does he investigate the specific characteristic? Inwardly, he resolves on the foulness-as-such of the foulness within the body: 'There exist in this body: hair of the head, body hair, nails, teeth, dust, dirt, skin, flesh, bone, sinew, vein, kidneys, heart, spleen, lungs, intestines, mesentery, stomach contents, bowel contents, urine, feces, tears, sweat, saliva, mucus, fat, lymph, marrow, grease, bile, phlegm, pus, blood, brain, cranial fluid, urine.' He further resolves that this multifold inward-body foulness is comprised by two elements: the earth element and the water element. From hair-of-head and body-hair through liver and feces — this he resolves as the earth element. From tears and lymph through urine — this he resolves as the water element.

Outwardly, he resolves on the foulness as found outside the body, by way of aspects beginning with the discolored. He resolves on the discolored: a corpse he has himself seen, or heard described, or imagined — of a woman, a man, a friend, an enemy, or one indifferent to him; low, middling, or excellent; young, middle-aged, or elderly. Taking the sign, he resolves on a corpse one day dead, blood having clotted, just turning to pus — as the discolored. A corpse two days dead, fully turned to pus, before worms have arisen — as the festering. A corpse seven days dead, with worms arisen, swollen, fully bloated — as the swollen. A corpse being eaten by crows, vultures, dogs, jackals — as the gnawed. A corpse with skin, flesh, and blood gone, only sinews holding the bones together — as the bloody-skeletal. When the limbs have been scattered in different directions and pulled apart — bones with flesh, bones without flesh, bones with little flesh remaining — as the dispersed.

Furthermore, there are two skeletons: the body-skeleton and the limb-skeleton. The body-skeleton runs from the pelvis up the spine to where the skull rests. The limb-skeleton is the connected arm-bones and thigh-and-leg bones. The body-skeleton is properly called the *śaṅkalikā*; the limb-skeleton is called the *asthi-śaṅkalikā* (bone-skeleton). Furthermore, two skeleton-signs may be taken: the painted, the stone-and-wood-carved, the actually-existing, or the not-actually-existing. He attends to the sign; [when he attends to the not-actually-existing,] he resolves only on the *śaṅkalikā*, not on the *asthi-śaṅkalikā*. When he attends to the sign of the actually-existing skeleton, he resolves on the *asthi-śaṅkalikā*.

This outward color-form has three kinds of change: change-by-its-own-nature, change-by-another, and change of both kinds. From the discolored through the swollen — that is change-by-its-own-nature. From the gnawed through the dispersed — that is change-by-another. The bones and the skeleton — that is change of both kinds. One who thus knows directly the outward foulness in this aspect investigates the specific characteristic of outward foulness in just this aspect.

How does he investigate the general characteristic of foulness? Just as inwardly the body's color-form is unaltered foul-color [in some respects] and outwardly the body's color-form is altered foul-color [in others], he resolves that they share the same nature and the same property: 'This unaltered color-form is also of this nature.' Whatever beings possess this fair color-form — they too have it of just this nature, of the kind belonging to outward foulness.

How does he investigate the side? It occurs to him: 'If I, [in regard to] this fair color-form, [generate] this foul-nature,' 'not knowing it as it is — neither as inward nor as outward foul color-form — should generate craving for fair color-form, that is mere perversion, gathered into the dark side.' 'It produces escape-by-suffering, with affliction, with calamity, with burning.' 'From this arise, in time to come, birth, aging, illness, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection, and despair.' 'Whereas the seeing-as-it-is of the foulness-nature within fair color-form is the bright side.' 'This dharma is without suffering, without affliction; from this, despair through to all subsequent suffering ceases.' 'Therefore, what is the dark-side condition is not to be tolerated; it is to be abandoned, purified.' 'The bright-side, when not arisen, is to be aroused;' 'when arisen, its abiding, growth, abundance, and amplitude are to be made.' Thus he investigates the side.

How does he investigate time? It occurs to him: 'This inward fair color-form is so taking the present time. This outward foul color-form is also so taking the present time.' 'Taking the past time, however,' '[the outward foul one] was a fair color-form.' 'As that was — taking past time — fair, just as I am now', 'taking present time,' 'so by progression, taking present time, [the present fair one] has become foul. This fair color-form of mine, taking present time, is foul.' 'It will not, in time-to-come, not be foul —' 'there is no such case.' 'As is this outward [body], so is mine taking present time.' Thus through past, future, and present times: 'this body of mine is of such a nature, has been such, will be such, and does not transcend this nature.' Thus he investigates time.

How does he investigate reasoning? It occurs to him: 'There is no self, no being, inward or outward, that could be either fair or foul.' 'Rather, this is mere form — mere accumulation of itching and scratching — wherein this notion, this designation, this convention is applied': 'fair' or 'foul.' 'Furthermore: vital force, heat, and consciousness —' 'when these abandon the body,' 'then it lies cast off like an unconscious log.' 'Of this dead one, by stages, these altered states are to be known:' 'the discolored through the bone-skeleton stages. This body of mine —' 'pierced by previous-action defilements,' 'born of mother-and-father impurities, accumulated by gruel and barley —' 'by what cause, by what condition, is this present-time fair color-form known as fair?' 'But the inward body is at all times, both inwardly and outwardly, foul.' Thus by way of the conventional, the ultimate, and the causal, he investigates dependence-reasoning.

It occurs to him: 'This foulness, thus practiced, cultivated, made much of, conduces to the abandonment of sensual craving.' 'And sensual craving is to be abandoned.' Thus he investigates by cause-and-effect reasoning. It occurs to him: 'It has been spoken by the Blessed One: Foulness practiced, cultivated, made much of, conduces to the abandonment of sensual craving. This, for me, is the trustworthy testimony.' 'Direct knowing-and-seeing arises in me also:' 'as I, however much, cultivate foulness and attend to it,' 'thus the obsession of sensual craving — when not arisen — does not arise;' 'when arisen, it goes away.' 'There is also this consequential gradation:' 'when one attends to a counter-aspect dharma,' 'defilement arises through the object of the counter-aspect.' Thus he investigates by proof-establishing reasoning. It occurs to him: 'This is an established nature-of-things, an unthinkable nature-of-things:' 'that foulness-cultivation is the antidote to the abandonment of sensual craving.' 'This is to be considered,' 'not further discriminated,' 'but resolved upon.' Thus by nature-of-things reasoning he investigates foulness. This is the threefold-gateway, six-investigation-subject *vipaśyanā* of one engaged in foulness.

How does the practitioner engaged in lovingkindness investigate the six investigation-subjects? Taking the lovingkindness-teaching as governing, comprehending the meaning of 'lovingkindness — characterized by the resolve to bring happiness to all, established in benefit-and-happiness-aspiration' — he investigates the meaning.

He, comprehending the meaning, investigates closely: 'This is the friend-side; this is the enemy-side; this is the indifferent-side.' 'All these sides, falling within the continuum of others, are outward substance' — so he resolves. Or: friend-side as inward, enemy-and-indifferent-side as outward. Thus he investigates lovingkindness in the substance.

He investigates closely: 'These three sides — neither suffering nor happy,' 'desiring happiness — let them be happy.' Among these: the friend is characterized by helpfulness; the enemy by harmfulness; the indifferent by neither. Among those who are not-suffering-and-happy and who desire happiness, three kinds of happiness-desire are known: some desire sensual happiness; some desire form-realm happiness with rapture; some desire form-realm happiness without rapture. Of those who are afflicted by sensual happiness: 'let them be happy with blameless [happiness].' Likewise of those wishing rapture-happiness or rapture-less happiness. Thus by way of specific characteristic he investigates lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'Of friend, enemy, and indifferent,' 'equanimity-of-mind is to be made by me,' 'equality-of-mind.' 'Why?' 'With the friend, bringing happiness is not difficult for me —' 'bringing happiness.' 'And the indifferent-side —' 'with that also, not very difficult for me.' 'But the enemy-side —' 'with that, most difficult.' 'So bringing happiness is to be made by me here above all.' 'What need to speak of —' 'friend or indifferent?' 'Why?' 'In this case, no one actually scolds or is scolded,' 'angers or is angered,' 'abuses or is abused,' 'beats or is beaten —' 'apart from these letters of speech that resound.' 'They are mere syllables, mere measure-of-syllable. And this body, of form, gross, made of the four great elements — it is here that such contacts come to me, [I] standing here:' 'namely, contacts of speech,' 'contacts of hand, clod, stick, weapon. This body of mine —' 'is impermanent; these contacts that harm — these too are impermanent.' 'Moreover:' 'all beings are subject to birth, aging, illness, death; by their very nature they suffer. It would be unbecoming for me,' 'if to beings already naturally suffering, I should bring further suffering rather than happiness — that would be like an enemy acting on a friend, since these beings already do this to themselves.'

Furthermore, it has been spoken by the Blessed One: 'I do not see any easily-found being' 'who, in this long course of *saṁsāra*, has not been my mother, father, brother, sister, teacher, preceptor, or one in the position of a guru.' Thus by this consideration also: the enemy-side is enemy-side only as a designation. There is no fixed essence here — friend-or-enemy-being. The friend, by the passage of time, becomes the enemy; the enemy too becomes the friend. Therefore equanimity-of-mind toward all beings, the view of equality, is to be made; an equal benefit-aspiration, happiness-aspiration, happiness-bringing — the resolve to bring happiness. Thus by way of general characteristic he investigates lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'Whatever ill-will arises in me toward harm-doing beings — that is the death-nature' — extended at length as before. 'And whatever non-ill-will is now present — that is the deathless-nature' — extended at length as before. Thus he investigates the dark-and-bright sides of lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'Those happiness-desiring beings, taking past time — they have passed away. To whom shall we now bring happiness?' 'But those beings of present time — taking present time,' 'taking future time — let them be at all times happy.' Thus he investigates the time of lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'There is no self, no being, who would be the desirer-of-happiness,' 'or to whom happiness would be brought.' 'Rather, this is mere aggregate, mere accumulation of formations, wherein this notion, designation, convention is applied.' 'And these formations are caused by *karma* and *kleśa*.' Thus by dependence-reasoning he investigates lovingkindness. 'It is an established nature-of-things, an unthinkable nature-of-things, that lovingkindness-cultivation conduces to the abandonment of ill-will.' Thus by nature-of-things reasoning he investigates lovingkindness.
