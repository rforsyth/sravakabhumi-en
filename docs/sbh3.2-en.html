Vipaśyanā is fourfold (1.3–1.6): the monk investigates, investigates closely, deliberates, and engages in thorough examination. ¶3–6 give it three gateways (following the sign alone, seeking, reviewing) and six things as its object: meaning, thing, characteristic, side, time, and reasoning, the last by four reasonings (5.25–5.38). ¶7–28 run the six things through the five conduct-purifying objects; ¶29–39 treat the kinds of engagement, purification from obstacles, and the yoking of śamatha and vipaśyanā; ¶41–56 the cultivation of attention, its four kinds (41.3–41.4) and then the beginner's instruction: five signs to take up (46.4–46.5), worked through for aśubhā (46.6–54.1), maitrī (54.2–54.18), dependent origination (55.1–55.8), and the elements (55.9–55.16). The breath-mindfulness instruction is lost in the lacuna after 55.16.

Vipaśyanā is fourfold.

What four?

A monk investigates phenomena, investigates closely, deliberates, and engages in thorough examination — taking inward cetaḥ-śamatha as his support.

How does he investigate?

The purifying-conduct object, the skill object, or the defilement-purifying object — he investigates under the aspect of 'as far as it goes'; under the aspect of 'as it actually is' he investigates closely; by conceptual attention joined with discernment, taking it as a sign, he deliberates; he judges, and engages in thorough examination.

This vipaśyanā should be known as having three gateways and the six kinds of things as its object.

What are the three gateways?

Vipaśyanā that follows the sign alone; vipaśyanā that follows seeking; vipaśyanā that follows reviewing. Following the sign alone: when, by what he has heard and grasped, he attends to the dharma of instruction with unconcentrated attention. He does not think it through, weigh it, or examine it. This is vipaśyanā following the sign alone. When he then thinks it through, judges it, weighs it, examines it, then it is vipaśyanā following seeking. When, having judged and examined, he then reviews it just as it has been established, then it is vipaśyanā following reviewing. This is the threefold-gateway vipaśyanā.

What are the six kinds of things taken as object?

While seeking, he seeks six things: meaning, thing, characteristic, side, time, and reasoning. Having sought these, he reviews them.

How does he seek meaning?

'The meaning of this teaching is this; the meaning of this teaching is that' — thus he seeks meaning.

How does he seek the thing?

The thing is twofold: inward and outward. Thus he seeks the thing.

How does he seek characteristic?

Twofold: specific characteristic and general characteristic. Thus he seeks characteristic.

How does he seek side?

Side is twofold: dark side and bright side. The dark side is investigated as fault, as danger. The bright side as quality and benefit. Thus he seeks side.

How does he seek time?

There are three times: past, future, present. 'Thus it was in the past time.' 'Thus it will be', 'in the future time.' 'Thus it is now,' 'in the present time' — so he seeks time.

How does he seek reasoning?

There are four reasonings: dependence-reasoning, cause-and-effect reasoning, proof-establishing reasoning, and nature-of-things reasoning. By dependence-reasoning he investigates the conventional as conventional, the ultimate as ultimate, and the causal-condition as causal-condition. Thus he seeks. By cause-and-effect reasoning he investigates the function of phenomena: 'This is the dharma, this its function: this [dharma] has this function.' By proof-establishing reasoning he investigates by means of the three pramāṇas (valid means of knowing): trustworthy testimony, inference, and direct perception. 'Is there Is there [Conjectural: trustworthy testimony] for this, or not? Is it apprehended by direct perception, or not? Is it established by inference, or not?' By nature-of-things reasoning he resolves upon the such-actuality (tathābhūtatā) of phenomena, the established nature of things, the unthinkable nature of things, the abiding nature of things — he does not further think it through, does not further discriminate. Thus he seeks reasoning.

This, in summary, is the vipaśyanā with three gateways and the six kinds of things as its object. By this, all vipaśyanā is comprehended.

Why are six divisions established?

It is said: Three kinds of comprehension are intended: comprehension of the meaning of what is taught; comprehension of the extent of the thing; and comprehension of 'as it actually is.' The investigation of meaning yields comprehension of what is taught. The investigation of the thing and the investigation of specific characteristic together yield comprehension of the extent of the thing. The investigation of general characteristic, of side, of time and reasoning together yield comprehension of 'as it actually is.' This much is to be known by the practitioner: namely, the meaning of what is taught; and the 'as far as it goes' (yāvadbhāvikatā) of the thing to be known, and the 'as it actually is' (yathāvadbhāvikatā).

The practitioner engaged in unattractiveness investigates the six things. It is said: Taking what he has heard and grasped about unattractiveness as governing, with attention grounded in the concentrated plane, he comprehends the meaning thus: 'This is unattractive, [Conjectural: truly so], repugnant, foul-smelling, rank.' By such aspects [there is] comprehension of the meaning of the unattractiveness-teaching previously heard. Thus he investigates the meaning by way of unattractiveness.

How does he investigate the thing?

Comprehending the meaning, he sees this unattractiveness as established under two divisions: inwardly and outwardly.

How does he investigate the specific characteristic?

Inwardly, first, he resolves on the unattractiveness within the body as putrid unattractiveness: 'There exist in this body: hair of the head, body hair, nails, teeth, dust, dirt, skin, flesh, bone, sinew, vein, kidneys, heart, spleen, lungs, intestines, mesentery, stomach contents, bowel contents, urine, feces, tears, sweat, saliva, mucus, fat, lymph, marrow, grease, bile, phlegm, pus, blood, brain, brain-membrane, urine [discharge].' He further resolves that this multifold unattractiveness within the body is comprised by two elements: the earth element and the water element. From hair-of-head and body-hair through feces — this he resolves as the earth element. From [Conjectural: tears and sweat] through urine — this he resolves as the water element.

Outwardly, he resolves on the unattractiveness as found outside the body, by way of aspects beginning with the discolored. He resolves on the discolored: a corpse he has himself seen, or heard described, or imagined — of a woman, a man, a friend, an enemy, or one indifferent to him; low, middling, or excellent; young, middle-aged, or elderly. Taking the sign, he resolves on a corpse one day dead, the blood drained, [Conjectural: not yet] turned to pus — as the discolored. A corpse two days dead, turned to pus, before worms have arisen — as the festering. A corpse seven days dead, with worms arisen and bloated — as the [Conjectural: bursting] and the swollen. A corpse being eaten by crows, ospreys, vultures, dogs, jackals — as the gnawed. A corpse with skin, flesh, and blood gone, only sinews holding the bones together — as the bloody-skeletal. When the limbs have been scattered in different directions and pulled apart — some with flesh, some without, some with a little flesh left — as the dispersed.

[He resolves] hand-bones separately, foot-bones separately, knee-bones separately, thigh-bones, upper-arm-bones, forearm-bones; the backbone; the jaw, the row of teeth in the middle; [and] the skull — having seen [these], he resolves on them as [Conjectural: bone]. Then, when he attends to [Conjectural: the bones] as a connected, unbroken frame — he becomes a mere sign-grasper, not a detail-grasper in its limbs and sub-limbs. In this mode he resolves on the skeleton (śaṅkalikā). But when he becomes a detail-grasper, then he resolves on the bone-skeleton. Furthermore, there are two skeletons: the body-skeleton and the limb-skeleton. The body-skeleton runs from the pelvis up the spine to where the skull rests. The limb-skeleton is the connected arm-bones and thigh-and-leg bones. The body-skeleton is properly called the skeleton (śaṅkalikā); the limb-skeleton is called the bone-skeleton (asthi-śaṅkalikā). Furthermore, there are two graspings of a skeleton's sign: of one made as a painting, or made of stone, wood, or grass, or of a real skeleton. [When,] of an unreal skeleton, he attends to the sign, then he resolves only on the skeleton, not on the bone-skeleton. When he attends to the sign of a real skeleton, then he resolves on the bone-skeleton.

This outward color-form, a derived form, has three kinds of change: change-by-its-own-nature, change-by-another, and change of both kinds. From the discolored through the swollen — that is change-by-its-own-nature. From the gnawed through the dispersed — that is change-by-another. The bones and the skeleton — that is change of both kinds. One who thus knows the outward unattractiveness as it actually is, by this aspect, investigates the specific characteristic of outward unattractiveness in just this aspect.

How does he investigate the general characteristic of unattractiveness?

Just as inwardly the body's outward [Conjectural: fair] color-form is unaltered, while outwardly the body's unattractive color-form is altered, he resolves on [the outer one's] sameness of nature, likeness of nature, with the inward unattractive color-form: 'This fair color-form of mine too is of this nature.' Whatever beings possess this fair color-form — theirs too is of just this nature, just like this outer one. Thus he investigates the general characteristic.

How does he investigate the side?

It occurs to him: 'If, with regard to this fair color-form, I do not know this unattractiveness as it actually is,' 'whether inwardly or outwardly, and should generate craving for fair color-form, that is mere perversion, gathered into the dark side.' 'It is a dharma [Conjectural: with strife], with suffering, with affliction, with calamity, with burning.' 'From this arise, in time to come, birth, aging, illness, death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection, and distress.' 'Whereas the seeing of the unattractiveness-nature within fair color-form as it actually is, is the bright side.' 'This dharma is without suffering, without affliction — and so on, up to: the distresses that have this as their cause cease.' 'Therefore, what is the dark-side condition is not to be tolerated; it is to be abandoned, cleared away.' 'The bright-side, when not arisen, is to be aroused;' 'when arisen, its abiding, growth, abundance, and amplitude are to be made.' Thus he investigates the side.

How does he investigate time?

It occurs to him: 'This inward [Conjectural: fair] color-form is so taking the present time. This outward unattractive color-form is also so taking the present time.' 'Taking the past time, however,' '[the outward unattractive one] was a fair color-form.' 'As that was — taking past time — fair, just as mine is now', 'taking present time,' 'so, in sequence, taking the present time, it has become unattractive. This [Conjectural: fair] color-form of mine, taking present time, being [Conjectural: fair] —' '— that it will not be unattractive in the time to come:' 'there is no such case.' '— just as this outward one is, taking the present time.' Thus through past, future, and present times: 'this body of mine is of such a nature, has been such, will be such, and does not transcend this nature.' Thus he investigates time.

How does he investigate reasoning?

It occurs to him: 'There is no self, no being, found inwardly or outwardly, that could be either fair or unattractive.' 'Rather, this is mere form, [Conjectural: mere body], wherein this notion, appellation, designation, convention is applied': 'fair' or 'unattractive.' 'Furthermore: vital force, heat, and consciousness —' 'when these abandon the body,' 'then it lies cast off, senseless, like a log.' 'Of this dead one, by stages, these altered states are to be known:' 'the discolored through the bone-skeleton stages. This body of mine —' 'shot through with previous karma and defilements,' 'born from the impurities of mother and father, accumulated by gruel and barley —' '— that is the cause, that the condition, by which this temporary fair color-form is discerned;' 'But the inward body is at all times, both inwardly and outwardly, unattractive.' Thus by way of the conventional, the ultimate, and the causal, he investigates dependence-reasoning.

It occurs to him: 'This unattractiveness, thus practiced, cultivated, made much of, conduces to the abandonment of sensual craving.' 'And sensual craving is to be abandoned.' Thus he investigates by cause-and-effect reasoning. It occurs to him: 'It has been spoken by the Blessed One: Unattractiveness practiced, cultivated, made much of, conduces to the abandonment of sensual craving. This, for me, is the trustworthy testimony.' 'Direct knowing-and-seeing arises in me also:' 'in whatever way I cultivate unattractiveness and attend to it,' 'in that way the obsession of sensual craving — when not arisen — does not arise;' 'when arisen, it goes away.' 'There is also this inferential method:' 'How could, when one attends to the opposing dharma,' 'defilement arise through that opposing object?' Thus he investigates by proof-establishing reasoning. It occurs to him: 'This is an established nature-of-things, an unthinkable nature-of-things:' 'that unattractiveness-cultivation is the antidote for abandoning sensual craving.' 'It is [Conjectural: not] to be thought through,' 'not to be discriminated,' 'but resolved upon.' Thus by nature-of-things reasoning he investigates unattractiveness. This is the vipaśyanā with three gateways and the six kinds of things as its object, of one engaged in unattractiveness.

How does the practitioner engaged in lovingkindness investigate the six things?

Taking the lovingkindness-teaching as governing, comprehending the meaning of 'lovingkindness — characterized by the resolve to bring happiness to all, established in benefit-and-happiness-aspiration' — he investigates the meaning.

He, comprehending the meaning, investigates: 'This is the friend-side; this is the enemy-side; this is the neutral-side.' 'All these sides, falling within the continuum of others, are an outward thing' — so he resolves. Or: friend-side as inward, enemy-and-neutral-side as outward. Thus he investigates lovingkindness in the thing.

He investigates: 'These three sides — neither suffering nor happy,' 'desiring happiness — let them be happy.' Among these: the friend is characterized by helpfulness; the enemy by harmfulness; the neutral-side by neither. Among those who are neither suffering nor happy and who desire happiness, three kinds of happiness-desire are known: some desire sensual happiness; some desire form-realm happiness with rapture; some desire form-realm happiness without rapture. Of those who lack sensual happiness: 'let them have sensual happiness — blameless.' Likewise of those wishing rapture-happiness or rapture-less happiness. Thus by way of specific characteristic he investigates lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'Of friend, enemy, and neutral,' 'the same mind is to be made by me,' 'an equal mind.' 'Why?' 'With the friend, bringing happiness is not difficult for me —' 'bringing happiness.' 'And the neutral-side —' 'with that also, not very difficult for me.' 'But the enemy-side —' 'with that, most difficult.' 'So bringing happiness is to be made by me here above all.' 'What need to speak of —' 'the friend-side or neutral-side?' 'Why?' 'In this case, no one actually scolds or is scolded,' 'angers or is angered,' 'abuses or is abused,' 'beats or is beaten —' 'apart from these letters of speech that resound.' 'They are mere sounds, mere [Conjectural: names]. And this body, arisen as it has, of form, gross, made of the four great elements — it is here that such contacts come to me, [I] standing here:' 'namely, contacts of speech,' 'contacts of hand, clod, stick, weapon. This body of mine —' 'is impermanent; these contacts that harm — these too are impermanent.' 'Moreover:' 'all beings are subject to birth, aging, illness, death; by their very nature they suffer. It would be unbecoming for me,' 'if to beings already suffering by nature I should bring further suffering rather than happiness — [Conjectural: that would be doing to a friend what an enemy does], what these beings do to themselves.'

Furthermore, it has been spoken by the Blessed One: 'I do not see any easily-found being' 'who, in the passage of this long time, has not been my mother, father, brother, sister, teacher, preceptor, or one in the position of a guru.' Thus by this consideration also, the enemy-side is for me the [Conjectural: friend-side]. There is no fixed essence here — friend-or-enemy-being. The friend, by the passage of time, becomes the enemy; the enemy too becomes the friend. Therefore [there is] none [that is fixed]. An equal mind toward all beings, the view of equality, is to be made; an equal benefit-aspiration, happiness-aspiration, happiness-bringing — the resolve to bring happiness. Thus by way of general characteristic he investigates lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'Whatever ill-will arises in me toward harm-doing beings — this is a dharma [Conjectural: with strife]' — extended at length as before. 'And whatever non-ill-will is now present — this is a dharma [Conjectural: without strife]' — extended at length as before. Thus he investigates the dark-and-bright sides of lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'Those happiness-desiring beings, taking past time — they have passed away; how could we now bring happiness to them?' 'But those beings of present time — taking present time,' 'taking future time — let them be at all times happy.' Thus he investigates the time of lovingkindness.

He investigates closely: 'There is no self, no being, who would be the desirer-of-happiness,' 'or to whom happiness would be brought.' 'Rather, this is mere aggregate, mere formations, wherein this notion, designation, convention is applied.' 'And these formations are caused by karma and kleśa.' Thus by dependence-reasoning he investigates lovingkindness. [Lacuna] 'It is an established nature-of-things, an unthinkable nature-of-things, that lovingkindness-cultivation conduces to the abandonment of ill-will.' Thus by nature-of-things reasoning he investigates lovingkindness.

How does he investigate the meaning, when engaged in vipaśyanā on the object of idaṁ-pratyayatā pratītyasamutpāda (this-conditionality / dependent-origination)?

Taking as governing the dharma that is governed by that, [he knows:] 'From the arising of those-and-those dharmas, these-and-these dharmas arise; from the ceasing of those-and-those dharmas, these-and-these dharmas cease.' 'There is no dharma-possessor here — no ruler, no agent, no creator, no fashioner of dharmas; no primal nature, no other person, as propeller of dharmas.' Thus, as one discerning the meaning, he investigates the meaning. Again and again he investigates closely. [Namely:] the twelve members of becoming. He resolves [them] internally and externally. Thus he investigates the thing. He again investigates closely: 'Ignorance is the not-knowing of the prior limit,' and so on in detail as in the Pratītyasamutpāda-vibhaṅga. Thus he investigates the own-characteristic. Again he investigates closely: 'Thus all these dependently-arisen formations — since they come to be after not being and pass away after being, one after another — are impermanent; since they are subject to birth, aging, sickness, and death, [they are] painful; since they are not autonomous and no inward person is found, [they are] empty and non-self.' And he investigates their general-characteristic. Again he investigates closely: 'Whatever delusion there is about these impermanent, painful, empty, non-self formations as they actually are — this is a dharma [Conjectural: with strife], on the dark side; [and] non-delusion, again, is on the bright side' — and so on in detail. Thus he investigates the side [dark/bright]. [Lacuna] He again investigates closely: 'There is action; there is its result; but the agent is not found — as one who would be the agent or experiencer, [the agent exists] only as dharma-convention; with respect to those very ignorance-conditioned formations, up through birth-conditioned aging-and-death, there is the notion, designation, convention: agent, experiencer, one of such a name, such a birth, such a clan, such food, experiencing such pleasure and pain, so long-lived, so long-lasting, with such a limit to life.' Moreover, this result is twofold; and the cause is twofold. [The results:] the result that is the individual existence and the result that is the enjoyment of objects; [the causes:] the propellant cause and the actualizing cause. Of these, the individual-existence-result is what is born of maturation — the six sense-bases; the object-enjoyment-result is the feeling, born of the six contacts, that is governed by desired or undesired action. Of these, the propellant cause is: [Conjectural: delusion] about the twofold result; the meritorious, demeritorious, and imperturbable [formations], preceded by delusion; and, held by the formations, the seed for the manifestation of the sprout of consciousness of further becoming; held by consciousness, the seed of name-and-form of further becoming, the seed of the six sense-bases, the seed of contact-and-feeling. For the future arising of what bear the name of birth — consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, feeling — [there is] in sequence the prior seed-taking. This is the propellant cause. Now, experiencing the feeling born of ignorance-contact, by craving with that as its object he generates the craving for further becoming. Clinging, of the craving-side and of the delusion-side, he takes hold. By whose force, by whose presence, that action becomes capable of yielding maturation — this is the actualizing cause. Taking these two kinds of cause as governing, [he sees]: 'Thus arises the entire mass of suffering — characterized by the threefold suffering.' Thus by dependence-reasoning he investigates. 'This idaṁ-pratyayatā pratītyasamutpāda, frequented and cultivated, conduces to the abandonment of delusion; this is trustworthy testimony, this is personal [knowing], and this is the inferential method; this is the established nature-of-things.' Thus he investigates by cause-and-effect reasoning, by proof-establishing reasoning, and by nature-of-things reasoning.

How does he investigate the meaning, when engaged in vipaśyanā on the object of dhātuprabheda (the distinction of elements)?

The meaning of lineage is the meaning of dhātu, [as are] the meaning of [Conjectural: seed], the meaning of cause, and the meaning of nature. Thus, as one discerning the meaning, he investigates the meaning. Resolving the six elements beginning with earth, internally and externally, he investigates the thing. Earth is characterized by hardness; [and so on] up through wind, characterized by motion; consciousness, characterized by knowing; and the space-element, characterized by hollowness and by not being filled by form. Thus he investigates the own-characteristic. All these elements are alike in being impermanent, up through being non-self. Thus he investigates the general-characteristic. 'Whatever elevation and conceit there is, in one who perceives a lump and does not know the manifoldness of the elements, on account of this body of many elements — this is a dharma [Conjectural: with strife], on the dark side; the reverse is on the bright side.' Thus he investigates the side. In past, future, and present times, depending on the six elements, the embryo's descent into the mother's womb occurs. Thus he investigates the time. Just as, depending on grass or wood, space enclosed [by them] comes to be called 'house' — likewise, taking up the six elements, depending on bones and sinews, skin, flesh, and blood, [with] space enclosed [by them], there arises the notion, designation, convention: 'body, body.' And former actions and defilements, and their own seed, are the cause of these. Thus by dependence-reasoning he investigates. 'Dhātuprabheda, frequented and cultivated, conduces to the abandonment of [the stiffness of] conceit; this is trustworthy testimony, this is personal [knowing], and this is the inferential method; this is the established nature-of-things, the unthinkable nature-of-things.' Thus he investigates by cause-and-effect reasoning, by proof-establishing reasoning, and by nature-of-things reasoning.

How does he investigate the meaning, when engaged on the object of ānāpānasmṛti (mindfulness of breathing)?

Mindfulness whose object is bound to in-breath and out-breath — that non-forgetfulness, articulated awareness, of the mind — is ānāpānasmṛti. Thus he investigates. [The breaths] are found inwardly: the in- and out-breaths, since they are bound to the body; and they are also comprised in the outer sense-base. Thus he investigates the thing. There are two breaths: the wind that enters — that is the in-breath; and the wind that goes out — that is the out-breath. 'These long in-and-out-breaths, these short ones — I experience them with the whole body.' Thus he investigates the own-characteristic. When the in-breath has ceased, the out-breath arises; when the out-breath has ceased, the in-breath [arises]. The proceeding of the in- and out-breath is bound up with the life-faculty and this conscious body; thus the in- and out-breaths are impermanent, [Conjectural: and so is their basis]. Thus he investigates the general characteristic. Of one whose mindfulness on in-and-out-breath is not established, whatever agitation of the mind is produced by vitarka — this is a dharma [Conjectural: with strife], on the dark side; the reverse is on the bright side — and so on in detail. Thus he investigates the side. In past, future, and present times, the body is bound to the in- and out-breath, and the in- and out-breaths are bound to body and mind. Thus he investigates the time. There is no one apart [from these] who breathes in or breathes out, or to whom these in- and out-breaths belong; rather, on formations arisen from causes, dependently arisen, there is this notion, designation, convention. Thus by dependence-reasoning he investigates. 'Ānāpānasmṛti, frequented and cultivated, conduces to the cutting-off of vitarka; this is trustworthy testimony, this is personal [knowing], and this is [Conjectural: the inferential method]; this is the established nature-of-things, the unthinkable nature-of-things.' Thus he investigates by cause-and-effect reasoning, by proof-establishing reasoning, and by nature-of-things reasoning.

Thus, having investigated the six things with the conduct-purifying object, while again and again calming the inward mind, he again and again investigates that same [object], as it has been investigated, by the modes of vipaśyanā. For him, vipaśyanā purifies on the basis of śamatha. And on the basis of vipaśyanā, śamatha reaches abundance, amplitude. As for the vipaśyanā on the skill-object and the defilement-purifying object, I will speak of its working of the six things later, in its own place.

There, engagement is ninefold, bright-side-comprised; and the inverse — ninefold, dark-side-comprised — is also to be known by the yogī. Namely: conformable engagement, practiced engagement, unperverted engagement, non-slack engagement, timely engagement, observation engagement, dissatisfaction engagement, engagement that does not shed the yoke, and right engagement. By this ninefold bright-side-comprised [engagement], the mind is concentrated swiftly, swiftly. And it advances toward excellence in concentration. Whatever stage is to be traversed by it, attained by it — that it reaches quickly, very quickly, without delay. By the dark-side-comprised ninefold engagements, the mind is not concentrated swiftly, swiftly. Nor does it advance toward excellence in concentration. Whatever stage is to be traversed, to be attained — there [the practitioner] delays in going.

What, then, is conformable engagement?

If [the practitioner] is lust-conducted, he binds the mind to aśubhā (unattractiveness); the hatred-conducted [binds it to] maitrī; up through the vitarka-conducted [to] ānāpāna-smṛti; the balanced-conduct one, lightly-dusted, [binds it to] whatever object has easy uptake (priyārohatā) for him. He engages with that [object]. This is conformable engagement.

What is practiced engagement?

Practice has been done by him, even if only a little, so that he is not in every respect a mere beginner. For one who is a beginner, even when engaged on the conformable object, the hindrances [Conjectural: recur frequently]. And [there is] bodily and mental encumbrance, by which his mind is [Conjectural: not] concentrated. This is practiced engagement.

What, then, is non-slack engagement?

He is one engaged continuously, and one engaged respectfully. If he rises from concentration — whether for alms-food, or to attend on a respected teacher, or for the sake of attending a sick person, or for a duty of respect, or any such matter — he does all of it with a mind inclined, tending, leaning toward [the concentration]. And having done [the duty] quickly, very quickly, having completed it, he engages again [in meditation], having settled himself for seclusion. If he meets with a gathering of monks, nuns, lay-followers, kṣatriyas, brāhmaṇas, he does not pass much time in association. He converses moderately. He does not give rise to lengthy speech. He does not withdraw [from his purpose] otherwise. Thus he is one of aroused energy: 'Today indeed I shall attain what is to be attained!' Why so? 'Many are my conditions for death — wind in me might trouble, or bile, or phlegm; food might disagree, by which cholera might settle in my body. Or a snake might bite me; a scorpion or centipede; or fear from a human.' On these occasions, at all times, he [Conjectural: reflects, cultivating the perception of impermanence], and dwells heedful. And thus, further, he dwells heedful: 'Even if I should live seven days, six, five, four, three, two, one day, a watch, a half-watch, an hour, a half-hour — ah, may I live so long as I enjoy alms-food; so long as, having breathed in, I breathe out; and so long as I live, may I undertake yoga in the Teacher's dispensation by yoga and attention. — so that in that much [time] much might be accomplished by me in the Teacher's dispensation.' This is non-slack engagement.

What, then, is unperverted engagement? [Lacuna] From time to time he cultivates the sign of śamatha, [Lacuna] the sign of pragraha (lifting up), the sign of upekṣā (equanimity). He knows śamatha, the sign of śamatha, and the time of śamatha; [likewise] vipaśyanā, the sign of vipaśyanā, the time of vipaśyanā; pragraha, the sign of pragraha, the time of pragraha; upekṣā, the sign of upekṣā, the time of upekṣā. There, śamatha is the ninefold mental abiding. And the mind there is signless, conception-less, calm, very calm, abiding in śamatha, undivided — hence it is called śamatha. There, the sign of śamatha is twofold: the object-sign, and the cause-sign. The image conformable to the knowable thing is the object-sign — by which object he calms that mind. [The cause-sign:] in a mind cultivated by śamatha, for the later purification of śamatha, whatever engagement in vipaśyanā [there is] — this is the cause-sign. What is the time of śamatha? Answer: when the mind is excited, or [Conjectural: fears excitement] — that is the time for cultivating śamatha. Likewise when the mind is well-cultivated by vipaśyanā [and is] afflicted by distraction in what is to be done — that is the time for cultivating śamatha. There, vipaśyanā has four modes, three doors, and is the investigation of objects subdivided into six things.

There, the sign of vipaśyanā is twofold: the object-sign and the cause-sign. Among these, the object-sign, the sign of vipaśyanā [Conjectural: on its own side], is the image conformable to the knowable thing — the object-sign, by which object he makes wisdom examine [it]. There, the cause-sign: in a mind cultivated by vipaśyanā, for the later purification of vipaśyanā, the [Conjectural: engagement] in cetaḥ-śamatha [is the cause-sign]. There, the time of vipaśyanā: [Lacuna] when the mind is cultivated by śamatha, and from the very beginning, for understanding the knowable thing as it actually is — that is the time for cultivating vipaśyanā.

What, there, is pragraha (lifting up)?

Whatever — by some pleasing object taken up — is the gladdening, showing-forth, encouraging of the mind. There, the sign-of-pragraha: by which gladdening object as sign the mind is lifted; [and] the arousal of energy conformable to it; there, the time of pragraha: when the mind is sunk, or fears sinking — that is the time for cultivating pragraha.

What, there, is upekṣā (equanimity)?

Whatever mental evenness toward the object, in an undefiled mind, on the śamatha-vipaśyanā side; its straight, spontaneous proceeding; the workability of a workable mind, the giving-over of the mind, its functioning without exertion. There, the sign-of-upekṣā: that object with which the mind regards [things] with equanimity; and not arousing an excess of energy toward that very object. There, the time of upekṣā: when, on the śamatha-vipaśyanā side, the mind is free from sinking and excitement — that is the time for cultivating upekṣā. This is timely engagement.

What, there, is observation engagement?

Those very signs are well-grasped, well-discerned, well-taken up — when he wishes, he emerges — the range of concentration, [that is,] the image, he lets go, by attending to the natural object of the [Conjectural: unconcentrated] stage. This is observation engagement.

What, there, is dissatisfaction engagement?

He is not content with wholesome dharmas, and does not [Conjectural: relent] [Conjectural: in striving]; aspiring to a higher, more excellent state, he dwells much [in that]. He does not, midway, on account of a small or inferior [attainment], fall into despondency when something higher remains to be done. This is dissatisfaction engagement.

What, there, is engagement that does not shed the yoke?

He does not break the precepts undertaken, does not put holes in them; seeing a young, gracefully-formed, attractive woman, he is not a sign-grasper, not a feature-grasper; he is moderate in food; he is yoked to wakefulness, of few duties, few activities, few preoccupations; he is one who recollects and reminds of what was long-done and long-said. Such kinds of dharmas are called engagement that does not shed the yoke. These dharmas are conformable to one-pointedness of mind, [Conjectural: do not shed its yoke], and do not lead to distraction of mind. By them, [the mind is kept] from outward dispersion, from inward mental non-workability. This is engagement that does not shed the yoke.

What, there, is right engagement?

Right engagement is so called because, having resolved and resolved upon the object, he dispels it. If he is engaged in aśubhā, he attends to aśubhā under aśubhā-modes — by a vipaśyanā that follows only the sign. Then that attention, with that object, should be dispelled again and again, and made present again and again. Now, dispelling is fivefold: by gathering the mind inward; by non-recollection and non-attention; by attention to something else; by attention to the antidote; and by attention to the signless element. There, by the ninefold mental abiding, with vipaśyanā as forerunner, [there is] gathering the mind inward; by [Conjectural: turning away] from all signs from the start, binding [the mind] to non-distraction — [this is] non-recollection and non-attention; [passing] from the concentrated-stage object to another concentrated-stage object, while attending [only to] the concentrated stage — [this is] attention to something else; [attending to] aśubhā as antidote to beauty, up through ānāpānasmṛti as antidote to vitarka; [attending to] the space-element as antidote to form — [this is] attention to the antidote. From the non-attention to all signs and from the attention to the signless element — [this is] attention to the signless element. Moreover, though the character of dispelling has been laid out comprehensively for that object, what is intended in the present context is [dispelling] by gathering [the sign] inward and by non-recollection and non-attention. There, by a beginner, by one in his first attempt at engagement, the mind should not, from the start, be bound to any object — neither aśubhā nor another — except only [to this, so as not to be distracted]: 'Is my mind signless, conception-less, calm, very calm, unagitated, unwavering, not eager, free of effort, inwardly delighted?' Thus engaged, with respect to all external signs as they arise and arise, he practises non-recollection and non-attention. This is his dispelling of the object by non-recollection and non-attention.

Practising yoga there, he takes in [delight]; and again he ranges over the [Conjectural: sign-bearing], conceptual object — aśubhā and the rest. And how does he range? By a vipaśyanā that follows the sign alone, that follows seeking and reviewing. And he is not exclusively engaged in vipaśyanā. Turning back again from the sign of vipaśyanā, he attends to that same object in the mode of śamatha. then that object is for him [Conjectural: neither] released nor taken up — because that object is what śamatha proceeds on, hence not released; because he does not make it a sign, does not conceptualize it, hence not taken up. Thus by gathering [the mind] inward he dispels the object. There, for one who has taken up the sign of vipaśyanā again — the object [that is] the sign of the knowable thing — if he only resolves upon the object and does not dispel it again and again, his resolve does not, in succession, become purer, clearer, [it does not] proceed toward direct apprehension of the knowable thing. But when he resolves again and again and dispels again and again, his resolve becomes purer and purest in succession, proceeding to direct apprehension of the knowable thing. Just as a painter, or a painter's apprentice, first engaged in the painter's craft, having received from the teacher a model-image preceded by instruction, makes a copy after looking at it again and again; having made it, again and again he erases it, destroys it, and makes it afresh; the more he breaks [it] up and remakes [it], the purer, the clearer his next picture appears. Thus indeed, by right-engagement, in time he attains equality with the teacher, or even surpasses him. If, on the other hand, he were to make that picture again and again on top of [the previous one] without erasing, he would [Conjectural: never] attain purity of that picture. The same principle is to be known here. There, whatever object he resolves upon, that he dispels; but not necessarily, whatever he dispels, does he resolve upon. He resolves on the limited and dispels only the limited; so too [for] the extended and the [Conjectural: measureless]. Having dispelled the limited, he sometimes resolves only on the limited, sometimes only on the extended, [sometimes] only on the [Conjectural: measureless]; and the same is to be understood for the extended and the [Conjectural: measureless]. There, of form-dharmas, whatever is sign, image, appearance — that is gross, like a magical creation; or, of formless dharmas, the appearance that is preceded by name-convention and governed by what has been experienced. This is called right engagement. This is the ninefold bright-side engagement, conformable to śamatha-vipaśyanā — to be known [thus]. Likewise, in turn, [there is] just the ninefold inverse-mode [dark-side]. Thus by the dark-and-bright-side classification, [there is] the eighteenfold engagement. This is called one-pointedness.

What, then, is purification from obstacles (āvaraṇa-viśuddhi)?

Answer: by four causes, the yogī thus right-engaged purifies his own mind of obstacles. By full-knowledge of own-nature; by [knowledge] of the cause; by full-knowledge of the danger; and by cultivation of the antidote.

What, then, is the own-nature of obstacle?

Answer: there are four obstacles — paritamanā (despondency), nivaraṇa (hindrance), vitarka (discursive thought), and ātmasaṁpragraha (self-exaltation). There, paritamanā is the defiled longing, dissatisfaction — yearning, dejection, distress — of one engaged in renunciation and seclusion. There, nivaraṇa is the five hindrances beginning with sense-desire. There, vitarka is sense-desire-vitarka and the like — defiled vitarkas. There, ātmasaṁpragraha is when, by a trifling, an inferior, a mere touch of knowledge-and-vision, he exalts himself: 'I am a gainer; others are not so.' As before, in detail, this is the own-nature of obstacle.

There, of paritamanā there are six causes: namely, weakness of the basis, through the dominance of previous karma or through the affliction of disease; excessive engagement; deficient engagement; initial engagement; abundance of defilements; and non-practice of seclusion. For nivaraṇa, vitarka, and ātmasaṁpragraha: with respect to the dharmas that are the locus of hindrance, of [Conjectural: vitarka], of self-exaltation, unwise attention, much practised, is the cause of hindrance, vitarka, and self-exaltation. Not attending to unattractiveness, [the practitioner] attends to attractiveness — this here is unwise. Likewise, [Conjectural: not] attending to maitrī, [Lacuna]; abandoning the perception of light with its sign, he attends to the sign of darkness; not attending to śamatha, [he attends to] thoughts of relatives, of the country, of not dying, and of former play, laughter, delight, dalliance. Not attending to idaṁ-pratyayatā pratītyasamutpāda, he attends, with respect to what belongs to the three times, to an improper perception, 'I' or 'mine': this here is unwise [attention].

What, there, is the danger (ādīnava)?

When this fourfold obstacle is present, what has not been attained is not attained; from what has been attained, [he] falls back; he falls from yoga-engagement; he becomes defiled-dwelling, pain-dwelling; he reproaches himself; and is reproached by others; and at the breakup of the body, after death, he is reborn in the unfortunate states. This is the danger here.

What, there, is the antidote (pratipakṣa)?

The antidote to paritamanā, in brief, is the recollections. By attention to recollection, having gladdened the mind, having gladdened [it], he dispels arisen paritamanā and does not produce [it] unarisen. There, for bodily weakness, [Conjectural: excessive engagement], and initial engagement, the antidote is evenness of energy. For deficient engagement, the antidote is willingness to listen and careful inquiry. [For] abundance of defilements — the antidote is engagement of the aśubhā and other objects as appropriate. [For] non-practice — the antidote is just such a [reflective] consideration: 'I have not previously practiced seclusion-cultivation, by which now in me, engaged in seclusion, paritamanā arises. If I do not undertake the practice now, then in future it will again be of just this kind. Having considered, dissatisfaction must be abandoned by me; delight must be made.' For the [Conjectural: remaining], hindrance and the rest, the cultivation of wise attention, the reverse of unwise attention, is to be known as the antidote. There, having fully known the own-nature — as obstruction, as defilement, as belonging to the dark side: 'this is to be avoided' — And [recognizing] 'its avoidance is by avoidance of [its] cause,' he investigates the cause. And '[on the other hand,] of what is to be avoided, what is the fault if it is not avoided?' — thence he investigates the danger. 'How is non-arising in the future, of what has been avoided?' — thence he cultivates the antidote. Thus the mind is purified by him from obstacles.

There, as far as there is abundance of teaching conformable to vipaśyanā, so far there is abundance of vipaśyanā; and as far as there is abundance of vipaśyanā, so far there is abundance of śamatha. And this vipaśyanā is to be known as endless, [Conjectural: through the endlessness of the knowable] — namely, through these three gateways, in the one-by-one [investigation] of the six things, by the method of entry into endless modes — and so as vipaśyanā, when one is rightly engaged, reaches extension, growth, amplitude — taking the power of purity of practice as governing — so likewise the śamatha-side, productive of body-and-mind pliancy, is to be known to reach extension, growth, amplitude. As his body becomes pliant, and his mind, so too the basis of one-pointedness of mind on the object increases; as one-pointedness of mind increases, so the body becomes pliant, and the mind; thus these two dharmas are arisen from one another, bound to one another — namely, one-pointedness of mind [and pliancy of body and mind]; [from them comes] the arising of direct knowledge. There, to what extent is aśubhā attained? To what extent up through ānāpānasmṛti? — and so on. As to this: for this yogī, as a consequence of frequenting, cultivating, and making much of the engagement with aśubhā — whether walking or abiding, even with the object before [him], even while examining signs, by nature and without effort — unattractiveness appears more abundantly. And, by reason of the well-cultivation of aśubhā, the mind does not leap toward, does not become pleased with, does not resolve upon, dharmas connected with sense-lust — equanimity stands by; disgust and repugnance [are] to be known. By the yogī [it is to be known]: 'Aśubhā has been reached by me; the fruit of cultivation has been attained by me.' To this extent aśubhā is attained; by the opposite it is to be known as unattained. As with aśubhā, so likewise maitrī, idaṁ-pratyayatā pratītyasamutpāda, dhātuprabheda, and ānāpānasmṛti are to be known. Here is the distinction: loving-mindedness appears more abundantly, not the sign of aversion; the mind does not leap toward dharmas connected with ill-will — and so on in detail. Impermanence, painfulness, and non-self appear more abundantly, not the sign of delusion accompanied by the view of permanence, pleasure, and a self; the mind does not leap toward dharmas connected with delusion — and so on in detail. The perception of the diversity of elements, of [Conjectural: there being no single element], of the distinctions of the body appears more abundantly, not the perception of the [Conjectural: body as one mass]; the mind does not leap toward dharmas connected with conceit — and so on in detail. The perception of inward calm, the perception of śamatha, appears more abundantly, not the perception of proliferation; the mind does not leap toward dharmas connected with vitarka — and so on in detail.

There, [Conjectural: to what extent] do śamatha and vipaśyanā, both [Conjectural: blended], proceed in equal yoke, so that the path is called 'going yoked' (yuganaddhavāhin)? Answer: one who is a gainer of the ninth mode of the ninefold mental abiding — namely, of the concentrated state — having relied on that consummated samādhi, engages, by higher wisdom, in vipaśyanā of dharmas. For him, seeing into dharmas at that time, the path proceeds of its own accord, without exertion; without effort, vipaśyanā, purified and cleansed, proceeds made pliant and embraced in conformity with śamatha. [Conjectural: To this extent] it is said that his śamatha and vipaśyanā, both [Conjectural: blended], proceed in equal yoke, and the path becomes one going yoked with śamatha and vipaśyanā.

Subsequent uddāna [verse summary]: Sign-grasping, seeking, and reviewing follow as the gateways; by meaning, by characteristics, by [Conjectural: things], by sides, and by times together with reasonings; conformable, likewise practised, non-slack, [Conjectural: unperverted]; timely, observation, [Conjectural: dissatisfaction], not shedding the yoke — [these are] the engagements; and right engagement: these nine grounds are held to be of two kinds; from own-nature, from cause, likewise from seeing the danger, and from the cultivation of the antidote — the purification of obstacles.

What, then, is manaskāra-bhāvanā (cultivation of attention)?

Answer: the beginner, the one engaged for the first time in this work, having had the pervasive characteristic established for him with respect to one-pointedness and the purification from obstacles, avoids wrong engagement and trains in right engagement. From the very first, [thinking] 'I shall attain one-pointedness and delight-in-abandonment,' he engages by four kinds of attention. By which four? By the citta-saṁtāpana (mind-heating) attention, the citta-abhiṣyandana (mind-suffusing) attention, the praśrabdhi-janaka (pliancy-producing) attention, and the jñāna-darśana-viśodhana (knowledge-and-vision-purifying) attention.

What, there, is citta-saṁtāpana attention?

Answer: that attention by which he stirs up the mind with respect to stirring-up-worthy dharmas — this is the mind-heating attention.

What, there, is citta-abhiṣyandana attention?

That pleasing attention by which he gladdens the mind — this is the mind-suffusing attention.

What, there, is praśrabdhi-janaka attention?

Answer: that attention by which, from time to time having stirred up the mind with respect to stirring-up-worthy dharmas, and from time to time having gladdened the mind with respect to gladdening-worthy dharmas, he then inwardly calms [the mind]; in the signless, conception-less, he establishes [the mind] thus, makes one-pointed mindfulness proceed; by which cause, by which condition — as antidote to body-and-mind encumbrance, producing gladness in body-and-mind — body-pliancy and mind-pliancy arise. This is called the pliancy-producing attention.

What, there, is jñāna-darśana-viśodhana attention?

That attention by which, from time to time, with the mind thus [Conjectural: inwardly calmed], he again and again applies himself, by higher wisdom, to vipaśyanā of dharmas — namely, having relied on that very inward cetaḥ-śamatha — this is called the knowledge-and-vision-purifying attention.

He, from time to time, stirs up the mind with respect to stirring-up-worthy dharmas; thus his mind becomes heated, well-heated, agitated, well-agitated — namely, with respect to the dharmas that are the locus of the outflows.

What are all the stirring-up-worthy places?

Answer: four. Namely, ātma-vipatti (one's own misfortune) and para-vipatti (the misfortune of others) — in the present, when at hand, when face-to-face, [taken up] in consequence of wise attention, [each] becomes a stirring-up-worthy place. And there [the contrary pair]: one's own success and others' success — in the past, when ceased, gone, transformed, [taken up] in consequence of wise attention, [each] becomes a stirring-up-worthy place. From time to time he gladdens the mind with respect to gladdening-worthy dharmas. When he gladdens [the mind], his mind thus becomes oily, moist, fluid, clear, serene.

What are the gladdening-worthy dharmas?

Answer: three bases of gladdening: the Jewels (ratnāni), purity of the trainings (śikṣāpada-pāriśuddhi), and the non-shrinking of a mind born of confident expectation of distinctive attainment in oneself. Recollecting the Jewels, he gladdens the mind thus: 'Gains are well-gained by me — I whose Teacher is the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the Fully Awakened One; gains are well-gained — that I have gone forth in the well-stated dharma-vinaya; gains are well-gained — whose fellow brahmacāris are virtuous, possessed of qualities, lovely, of beautiful dharmas. My death will be auspicious, my time-of-passing auspicious, my future auspicious.' Thus, recollecting these four, he gladdens the mind.

How does he gladden the mind while recollecting purity of the trainings, purity of virtue?

'Gains are well-gained by me — I, in respect of the Teacher the Tathāgata Arhat Fully Awakened One, and his well-stated dharma-vinaya, and the well-practicing disciples' saṁgha therein — I am with these fellow brahmacāris of common virtue, of common training, with loving body, speech, and mental action, of common view, of common enjoyment.' Thus recollecting purity of the trainings, purity of virtue, he gladdens the mind — namely, by joy preceded by [Conjectural: non-regret]. How, by relying on the confident expectation of one's own attainment: 'I am capable, of such pure virtue; I am able and a fit vessel. With these fellow brahmacāris of common virtue, common view, and with these good [people] of common course, gone rightly, true persons, I am capable of being thus, of so engaging, of attaining the unattained, realizing the unrealized, directly experiencing the not-yet-directly-experienced in this very life.' Thus he generates joy — by relying on the basis of confident expectation of one's own attainment, he gladdens the mind.

Moreover: by whatever distinctive attainment has been made by him while abiding with energy begun afresh after a previous [phase], recollecting that and confidently apprehending further distinctive attainment, he gladdens the mind — this is the further basis. Heating the mind with respect to stirring-up-worthy dharmas, he turns the mind away from outflow-stationed dharmas, makes it inverse [to them], establishes it facing-away, separates it. Gladdening, suffusing the mind with respect to gladdening-worthy dharmas, he turns the mind, with affection, toward dharmas born of renunciation and seclusion; he attaches [the mind] to them, delights [the mind] in them, conjoins [the mind] with them. Thus, by these two dharmas, his mind proceeds — turned away from all dark-side, turned toward all bright-side — namely, by saṁvega (urgency) and praharṣa (gladness). Since the mind, thus turned away from the dark side by the mind-heating attention and turned toward the bright side by the mind-suffusing attention, from time to time is inwardly applied — namely, by cetaḥ-śamatha with the pliancy-producing attention — from time to time he discerns dharmas, examines, ponders, investigates — by the knowledge-and-vision-purifying attention. Thus his mind, from time to time embraced by śamatha and vipaśyanā, made fit as the cause of all aspects and all qualities, after the passing of those nights and days, of instants, lavas, and muhūrtas, advances toward distinction.

Just as raw gold or silver, by a skilled smith or by a smith's apprentice, when from time to time it has been heated, so that stain and dross are gone, and quenched, is made fit for this or that work of ornament by being soft and workable — the skilled smith or the smith's apprentice, with the craft-knowledge suited to it and the tools of his craft, works it into whatever ornamental form is desired — just so, when the yogī's mind has been agitated by being turned away from the stains and dross of covetousness and the rest, and gladdened by being turned away from defiled-dejection, then wherever the yogī directs it — to the śamatha-side or the vipaśyanā-side — there [the mind] becomes well-attached, well-clinging, undivided, unshaking; and it advances toward the perfecting of the intended purpose.

There, how is the beginner, the one in his first attempt at this work, instructed in manaskāra-bhāvanā?

[The way is] that he, being instructed and engaging, first touches delight in abandonment (prahāṇābhirati) and one-pointedness of mind. Here the knower of yoga (yogajña) at the very first instructs the beginner engaged in yoga thus: 'Come, dear one — relying on the three causes that take up signs, namely what is seen, what is heard, or a conception governed by thought and inference, take up five signs: the urgency-generating (saṁvejanīya), the gladdening (prasadanīya), the danger sign, the luminous sign, and the sign of discerning the thing (vasturūpaṇā).

If that beginner engaged in yoga is of lust-conduct, suited to be led by aśubhā — how is he to be awakened to the taking up of the five signs?

He is to be awakened thus: 'Come, dear one. Whatever village or town you dwell in dependent on: if you hear of someone, man or woman, in another village or town who is afflicted — pained, gravely ill, or dead — man or woman; or any friend, minister, relative, or kinsman of that man or woman; or the loss of [Conjectural: wealth] of the people of that village, caused by an enemy army, by burning by fire, by being carried off by water, by ruin through bad management, by the collapse of ill-run business, by the arrival of an unwelcome heir, or by ruin through a wastrel of the family; or if you do not hear [of it] but see [it] directly. or not in another village or town but in this very one; or not in this village or town and not of others, but you yourself are touched by bodily feelings, painful and sharp' — and so on in detail as before. 'Having seen, heard all this, stir up the mind: ‘Painful indeed is this saṁsāra, hard-won this individual-existence-attainment, in which such forms of misfortune — one's own and others' — are met with. Namely: misfortune of health, of [Conjectural: relatives], of wealth; sickness and the nature of sickness; death and the nature of death. Furthermore, for some, misfortune of virtue and of view, by reason of which beings here and now become pain-dwellers, and in the future are bound for unfortunate destinies. And whatever fortunes [there are] for here-and-now happy-dwelling and for fortunate destinies in the future — these too are impermanent; their impermanence is to be discerned. If misfortune is face-to-face, fortune at that time is turned-away. Even when misfortune is not face-to-face, fortune is hard to obtain and of perishable nature.’' And thus, having agitated the mind, wisely and well, you should apply [yourself] wisely: 'This situation cannot be relied on, cannot be trusted: that for me, wandering in saṁsāra, not finally released, my mind not liberated, these misfortunes and fortunes would not come before me, or would turn away from me; nor that suffering — sharp, harsh, bitter, unspeakable — would not arise for me from this cause: there is no such case. Therefore, taking all this as governing, I must be one delighting in abandonment, heedful. And dwelling much thus, I shall make [Conjectural: an end] of this harm.' Thus apply yourself wisely. Thus, having taken up the urgency-generating sign, take up next the gladdening sign.

And again, take it up thus — examine your own virtues: 'Are my precepts pure, or impure? Is there any transgression of training — through forgetfulness, lack of respect, abundance of defilement, or by being uninstructed?

Or, having transgressed, have I made amends as the dharma directs, and with [right] intention generated the mental resolve not to do [it] again?

Have I done what should be done; have I left undone what should not be done, in brief? Am I one of accomplished intention and accomplished engagement — namely, in the trainings?' Thus, as you examine, if the aggregate of virtue is pure, you need not form the volition 'May [Conjectural: non-regret] arise in me'; rather, it is simply the nature of things that for one of pure virtue non-regret arises. Likewise, for one without regret, the volition should not be made: 'May joy arise in me!' For by the nature of things, joy arises for the non-regretting one. By this one basis of gladdening, joy preceded by [Conjectural: pure] non-regret is to be generated. Having generated [joy], by another base of further gladdening — gladden the mind. If, then, you have, after a previous [attainment], become [one with] even a limited further distinctive attainment, joy is to be generated: 'I am capable, able. Thus, of pure virtue, well-established in the trainings of the Blessed One — in this very life, for the attainment of what is [Conjectural: not yet] attained, the attainment of the unrealized, the realization of the not-yet-realized.' By this basis too, gladden the mind.
