Mindfulness of Breathing

Śrāvakabhūmi · Sanskrit (Shukla 1973) ↔ English (1 rendering)
← All topicsView English only →

The fifth and longest of the carita-viśodhana practices, corresponding to the vitarka-carita (discursive-thinking-temperament) practitioner. Covers the definition (mindfulness with the in-breath and out-breath as object), the anatomy of the breath (āśvāsa and praśvāsa), two faults to avoid in practice, and the five paricayas — five progressive investigative frames applied to the breath: counting (gaṇanā), aggregates (skandha), dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda), the four noble truths (satya), and the breath-practice sixteenfold sequence (ṣoḍaśākāra). This last item is parallel to the sixteenfold ānāpāna pattern rather than the four-truth sixteen-aspect framework used elsewhere. The chapter closes with the arhatship-claim through breath-mindfulness and the temperament-match rationale.

The inner-in-breath is, when the in-breath has ceased and the out-breath has not yet arisen, the brief intervening air at the resting-place that arises in a similar manner.
The four-phase analysis (in-breath, pause-after-in, out-breath, pause-after-out) is more granular than what the Pali suttas give. MN 118 has just assāsa and passāsa. The Yogācāra refinement explicitly carves out the two pauses as part of the practice object — they are not 'gaps' between breaths but legitimate phases the practitioner attends to.

sbh2.6:7.1
asyā ānāpānasmṛteḥ pañcavidhaḥ paricayo veditavyaḥ |
Five familiarities of this ānāpānasmṛti should be known:
sbh2.6:7.2
tadyathā gaṇanāparicayaḥ, skandhāvatāraparicayaḥ, pratītyasamutpādāvatāraparicayaḥ, satyāvatāraparicayaḥ, ṣoḍaśākāraparicayaśca |
counting-familiarity, aggregate-entry-familiarity, dependent-origination-entry-familiarity, truth-entry-familiarity, and sixteen-aspect-familiarity.
The spine of the Yogācāra breath practice: five progressive depths, each complete in itself, cumulative. Counting establishes stability. Aggregate-entry pivots breath into insight by analyzing the breath into the five aggregates. Dependent-origination-entry traces the conditional chain. Truth-entry generates the four noble truths. Sixteen-aspect-familiarity is the breath-practice sixteenfold sequence parallel to MN 118, not the four-truth ṣoḍaśākāra framework used elsewhere.

Asanga explicitly says counting is the training-wheel for those who need it. For sharp-faculty practitioners, priyārohatā is treated here as easy uptake: they can quickly grasp the counting protocol and then move directly to attentive characterization of the breath. The modern question of whether counting is necessary gets a direct answer: only if the mind would otherwise drift.

The mind-tetrad operationalized as hindrance-management. Sloth-and-torpor calls for gladdening. Agitation-and-remorse calls for concentrating. When neither is present, the mind is liberated from hindrances. Same instruction as VSM Ch 4's balancing of faculties, applied here as breath-tetrad commentary.

The transition is temporal: first śamatha-yoga supported by absorption or access, then direct vipaśyanā on impermanence at 19.6. The prior English fragment was not wrong in content, but it did not read as a sentence.
sbh2.6:19.6
ṛjuṁ etarhi anityānudarśīvipaśyanāyāṁ yogaṁ karotyevaṁ asya taccittaṁ śamathavipaśyanāparibhāvitaṁ dhātuṣu vimucyate |
Now, engaging directly in the contemplation of impermanence, he engages in vipaśyanā. Thus his mind, perfumed by śamatha-vipaśyanā, is liberated in the elements,
sbh2.6:19.7
yadutānuśayebhyaḥ |
namely, from the latent tendencies.
sbh2.6:19.8

What are the elements?
sbh2.6:19.9
yaśca prahāṇadhāturyaśca virāgadhātuḥ, yaśca nirodhadhātuḥ |
The element of abandoning, the element of dispassion, and the element of cessation.
Three 'elements' name the three remaining work-products of the path: prahāṇa-dhātu (abandoning, for seeing-abandonable defilements), virāga-dhātu (dispassion, for cultivation-abandonable defilements), nirodha-dhātu (cessation, for the bases of clinging). Śamatha-vipaśyanā on the breath with these as aim brings the mind to liberation.
sbh2.6:19.10

The abandoning of all formations to be abandoned by seeing — that is the abandoning-element.
sbh2.6:19.11
sarvasaṁskārāṇāṁ bhāvanāprahātavyānāṁ prahāṇādvirāgadhātuḥ |
The abandoning of all formations to be abandoned by cultivation — that is the dispassion-element.
sbh2.6:19.12
sarvopadhinirodhānnirodhadhātuḥ |
The cessation of all bases of clinging — that is the cessation-element.
sbh2.6:19.13

sbh2.6:21.1
yatra vitarkacaritaḥ pudgalaḥ prayujyamānaḥ priyārohatayā prayujyate |
Where an applied-thought-conduct person is engaged, he engages through easy uptake [of the object].
sbh2.6:21.2

sbh2.6:21.3

[Damaged/Conjectural]: inward, personal, very near — because of this, the applied-thought agitation that would arise for one engaging there does not arise.
The source phrase savyokṣepamadhyātmaṁ pratyātmaṁ āsannāsannaṁ is unstable. The current English keeps the likely functional sense — the breath object is inward, one's own, and very near, so it does not provoke applied-thought agitation in an applied-thought-conduct person — but marks the line [Damaged/Conjectural] rather than presenting the parse as secure.
