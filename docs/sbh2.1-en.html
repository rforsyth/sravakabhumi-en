The Pervasive Object

The first of the four objects of meditation listed at 1.5. The pervasive object is itself fourfold (2.4–2.5): the image with conceptual construction (¶3–5), the image without conceptual construction (¶6), the full extent of the thing (vastu-paryantatā, ¶7), and the consummation of the work (¶8). ¶7 defines the full extent of the thing through yāvad-bhāvikatā and yathāvad-bhāvikatā, kept in Sanskrit and glossed at 7.2. The text calls the object pervasive because its four kinds are present in every object, are taught by all Buddhas, and belong to both the śamatha and the vipaśyanā side (9.3–9.6). ¶11–19 support the account from scripture: a discourse to Revata (¶11–15) and two verses with their exegesis (¶16–19).

What is the object [of meditation]?

It is said: There are four objects (ālambana-vastu).

What four?

The pervasive object (vyāpy-ālambana), the conduct-purifying object (carita-viśodhana), the skill object (kauśalya), and the defilement-purifying object (kleśa-viśodhana).

What is the pervasive object?

It is said: It too is fourfold: namely, the image with conceptual construction (savikalpaṁ pratibimbaṁ), the image without conceptual construction (nirvikalpaṁ pratibimbaṁ), the full extent of the thing (vastu-paryantatā), and the consummation of the work (kārya-pariniṣpatti).

What is the image with conceptual construction?

A certain person, relying on hearing the true dharma or on instruction-and-counsel, taking what has been seen, heard, or imagined, observes — by aspects of vipaśyanā grounded in the concentrated plane — an image-with-conceptual-construction conformable to the thing to be known (jñeya-vastu). He investigates, investigates closely, deliberates, and engages in thorough examination.

What is the thing to be known?

Namely: unattractiveness (aśubhā), lovingkindness (maitrī), this-conditional dependent origination (idaṁpratyayatā-pratītyasamutpāda), the analysis of elements (dhātu-prabheda), and mindfulness of breathing (ānāpāna-smṛti); skill-in-aggregates (skandha-kauśalya), skill-in-elements, skill-in-sense-bases (āyatana-kauśalya), skill-in-dependent-origination, skill-in-the-possible-and-impossible (sthānāsthāna-kauśalya); the grossness of the lower planes, the [Conjectural: calmness] of the upper planes, [Conjectural: the truth of suffering,] the truth of arising, the truth of cessation, and the truth of the path. This is called the thing to be known.

With respect to this thing-to-be-known: having come to instruction-and-counsel or hearing of the true dharma, and relying on that, he manifests attention grounded in the concentrated plane and resolves on those very dharmas. Thus he resolves on the thing-to-be-known. At that time, the resolve proceeds as ‘directly experienced’ with regard to the thing-to-be-known. That thing-to-be-known becomes present — gathered close, manifest — nor [is it] another thing of that kind; rather, [it is] a resolve-experience. It is such a kind of attention-experience grounded in the concentrated plane by which something corresponding to that thing-to-be-known comes to be — an appearance — and by this it is called an image conformable to the thing-to-be-known. When the practitioner, reviewing this practice, examines that thing-to-be-known under consideration and determines its qualities and faults — this is called the image with conceptual construction.

What is the image without conceptual construction?

This yogī, having grasped the sign from the image, no longer investigates, investigates closely, deliberates, or engages in thorough examination. Rather, [with regard to] that very object, [Conjectural: he calms the mind by way of śamatha]. Rather, by the nine-fold mental abiding, he places the mind inwardly, places it continuously, places again, places closely; tames, pacifies, fully pacifies; makes single-pointed, and concentrates. At that time, that image-without-conceptual-construction is his object — on which he exclusively establishes one-pointed mindfulness. That is the object; he does not investigate, deliberate, or thoroughly examine. And that image is called an image (pratibimba). [Lacuna] These are the alternate names of the image conformable to the thing-to-be-known.

What is the full extent of the thing (vastu-paryantatā)?

It is the yāvad-bhāvikatā (as-far-as-it-goes-ness) of the object, and its yathāvad-bhāvikatā (as-it-actually-is-ness).

What is yāvad-bhāvikatā?

[That] beyond which there is [Conjectural: no] further form-aggregate, feeling-aggregate, perception-aggregate, formations-aggregate, or consciousness-aggregate — the comprehensive gathering of all conditioned things by the five [aggregates]; the comprehensive gathering of all dharmas by the elements and sense-bases; the comprehensive gathering of all things-to-be-known by the noble truths. This is called yāvad-bhāvikatā.

What is yathāvad-bhāvikatā?

The actuality (bhūtatā) of the object and its suchness (tathatā), [endowed] with the four reasonings — its reasoning-endowed-ness; namely, by dependence-reasoning, cause-and-effect reasoning, proof-establishing reasoning, and nature-of-things reasoning (yukti). Thus the yāvad-bhāvikatā and yathāvad-bhāvikatā of the object, summarized as one, is called the full extent of the thing.

What is the consummation of the work?

When, through familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice, the śamatha-vipaśyanā attention with image-as-object is fulfilled. By that fulfillment, the basis (āśraya) is transformed. All encumbrances are tranquillized. Through the transformation of the basis (āśraya-parivṛtti), transcending the image, there arises in that very thing-to-be-known direct knowing-and-seeing without conceptual construction. [This arises] for one who has entered the first dhyāna, when he has gained the first dhyāna, in the range of the first dhyāna; for one who has entered the second, third, or fourth dhyāna — when he has gained the [Conjectural: second, third, or] fourth dhyāna — in the range of the [Conjectural: second, third, or] fourth dhyāna; and for one who has entered the attainments of the spheres of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception, when he has gained them, in their range. This is called the consummation of the work.

These are the four objects — universal, present in all objects, taught by the past, future, and present perfectly-awakened ones. Therefore it is called the [Conjectural: pervasive] object. Furthermore, this object [pertains] both to the śamatha side and to the vipaśyanā side; it has all things, real things, and cause and effect as its thing. Therefore it is called pervasive.

[As to] ‘the image with conceptual construction’ — this is on the vipaśyanā side. [Lacuna] [As to] ‘the full extent of the thing’ — this concerns its having all things and real things as its thing. [As to] ‘the consummation of the work’ — this concerns the cause-effect connection.

As spoken by the Blessed One, addressing the venerable Revata. Thus it is heard: The venerable Revata asked the Blessed One a question.

"To what extent, venerable sir, is a monk-yogī-yogācāra one whose mind is bound to an object? In which object does he bind the mind?

How, further, is the mind well-bound to the object?" "Excellent, Revata, excellent. Good indeed, Revata, that you ask about this matter. Therefore listen, attend well; I will speak. Here, Revata, the monk-yogī-yogācāra, wishing to purify conduct, or to develop skill, or to liberate the mind from outflows — binds the mind to a suitable object. He binds it to a corresponding object, properly. And there he is one who does not abandon his meditation.

How does he bind the mind to a suitable object?

If, Revata, this monk-yogī-yogācāra is of lust-conduct, [Conjectural: he binds the mind to the unattractive as object]. Thus he binds it to a suitable object. The hatred-conduct one [binds it] to lovingkindness; the delusion-conduct one to this-conditional dependent origination; the conceit-conduct one to the analysis of elements. If, Revata, this monk-yogī-yogācāra is of applied-thought-conduct, he binds the mind to mindfulness of breathing. Thus he binds it to a suitable object.

If, Revata, this monk is confused about the specific characteristic of formations, he binds the mind to [Conjectural: skill-in-aggregates]. The cause-confused [binds it] to skill-in-elements; the condition-confused to skill-in-sense-bases. [Lacuna] One wishing to make dispassion toward the desire-realm [binds the mind to] the grossness of sense-pleasures and the calmness of forms; one wishing dispassion toward forms — binds the mind to the grossness of forms and the calmness of the formless. Or, one wishing [Conjectural: to penetrate] the satkāya in every respect and to be liberated [from it] binds the mind to the truth of suffering, the truth of arising, the truth of cessation, and the truth of the path. Thus indeed, Revata, the monk-yogī-yogācāra — binds the mind to a suitable object.

[Conjectural: How does he bind the mind to a corresponding object?] Here, Revata, whatever the monk wishes to investigate as the thing-to-be-known — wishes to investigate closely, wishes to deliberate, wishes to thoroughly examine — that has been previously seen by him, heard, thought, or cognized. Taking that very seen-heard-thought-cognized as governing, with attention grounded in the concentrated plane, he attends to it. He constructs it conceptually and resolves on it. He does not see that very thing-to-be-known itself made present in concentration; rather, a representation of it arises. Its appearance, a mere knowing, a mere seeing, a mere recollection — that is the object. This monk-yogī-yogācāra binds the mind properly to the object. [Lacuna] [Conjectural: And how does he bind the mind properly to the object?] If, Revata, this monk-yogī-yogācāra binds the mind to the object to the full extent that the knowable thing is to be known — and [knows] it as it actually is, with an unperverted mind — thus indeed, Revata, this monk-yogī-yogācāra binds the mind properly to the object.

And how, Revata, is this monk-yogī-yogācāra one who does not abandon his meditation?

If, Revata, this monk is thus properly applied in the object, of continuous-application, and of careful-application, from time to time he cultivates the sign of śamatha, the sign of taking-up [effort], and the sign of equanimity. Through familiarization, cultivation, and frequent practice, through the tranquillization of all encumbrances, he attains purity-of-basis. He touches, contacts; realizes it. By examining the thing-to-be-known, [he attains] purity-of-object; by dispassion-from-lust, purity-of-mind; by dispassion-from-ignorance — he attains purity-of-knowing. He touches, contacts, realizes. Thus indeed, Revata, the monk-yogī-yogācāra is one who does not abandon his meditation. And since, Revata, the monk binds the mind to this object, and binds the mind to the object in this way, thus his mind is well-bound to the object.

Here is the verse: "The yogī who moves in signs, knower of all real things — the image-contemplator, persevering, [Conjectural: attains] purity."

When he says 'moving in signs' — by this, constancy of practice with regard to the sign of śamatha, the sign of taking-up, and the sign of equanimity is named. When he says 'knower of all real things' — by this, vastu-paryantatā (the full extent of the thing) is named. When he says 'the image-contemplator, persevering' — by this, the image-with-conceptual-construction and the image-without-conceptual-construction are named. When he says 'attains purity' — by this, the consummation of the work is named.

Again it has been said by the Blessed One: “The one skilled in the sign of mind finds the taste of seclusion and the seclusion-state — the contemplator, prudent, mindful, eats the rapture-and-happiness free of worldly attachments (nirāmiṣa).”

When he says ‘skilled in the sign of mind’ — by this, the image with conceptual construction and the image without conceptual construction are named under the term ‘sign.’ Vastu-paryantatā is named by the term ‘skilled.’ When he says ‘finds the taste of the seclusion-state’ — by this, the delight-in-abandonment and delight-in-cultivation of the one properly applied to the object are named. When he says ‘contemplator, prudent, mindful’ — by this, the constancy of śamatha-vipaśyanā cultivation is named. When he says ‘eats rapture-and-happiness free of worldly attachments’ — by this, the consummation of the work is named. Thus indeed this pervasive object should be known as validated by authoritative scripture, and as consistent with reasoning. This is called the pervasive object.
